THE STORY

The whole of Chapter One, as written — every road out of that 3:47am kitchen, every choice it leaves you, and the five ways it can end.

Prologue

The Kitchen, 3:47am

The hour the city forgets itself. A cold kettle, a brother who never came home, and the handful of doors the morning leaves open.

The kitchen, 3:47am

The kettle has gone cold in your hands and you could not say for how long you have been standing here, holding it like a thing you were about to do. Three forty-seven in the morning, the hour the city forgets itself, and the flat keeps the particular silence of two rooms built for two people and holding only one. There has been nowhere you needed to be since Friday... the internship ended, hands were shaken, the mornings came loose and never tightened again... so you are home, the way you are always home now, in the small north-Tokyo apartment you keep with your brother.

Gin. Older than you by eight years, and most of the reasons you turned out steady. You cooked for two tonight because you always cook for two; you ate your half at the counter and left his beneath an upturned plate, the way you have a thousand evenings he did not come back to eat it. He keeps strange hours. You keep the light on. You went to bed early and slept the thin, listening sleep of someone waiting for a key to find a lock.

You woke without knowing why. The second plate is still on the counter, the food beneath it gone cold and untouched, and the wrongness of that reaches you slowly, the way cold water finds you through a coat. Gin has not come home. Gin is never not home. He calls if he will be ten minutes late. He has not once, in nineteen years, let the night close over him without a word to you.

In the black glass of the window your own face waits for you... tired eyes, a jaw more his than yours, and under it the small wrong habit of these last weeks, the one you have told no one: you check the lock, the stove, the date on your phone, twice each, because some nights ago the city began to feel like a held breath and has not yet let it out. Rei, you think, to make the reflection agree to the name. It agrees. You are not sure why that should be a relief.

Somewhere past these walls your brother has stepped out of the ordinary world and not stepped back, and the apartment understood it before you would. The quiet here is not the quiet of an empty room. It is the quiet of something that has already started up the stairs.

The choice it leaves you
Get up. Go and find him.
The kitchen at this hour

The kitchen is three steps and a whole different country at this hour. You fill the kettle out of habit and do not light it. Two cups sit upturned on the rack, because he insists on two, though he is never here for the second, and tonight the second cup is a small cruelty you set back down very carefully.

Gin's keys are not on the hook.

They have hung there every day since you were small enough to be lifted up to reach them. The brass is worn bright where his thumb always goes. The hook is empty now, and what remains is only the shape a habit leaves in metal after the hand that made it has gone. Gin does not forget to call. Gin does not let a night close without a word. He has been the steadiest thing in your life for nineteen years, and now the kettle is going cold in your hand.

The choice it leaves you
Open the front door.
The walkway, the cold key on the concrete

You open the door before you have quite decided to. The walkway is empty. The sodium lamp hangs its flat orange across the railing and the bicycle stands and the closed doors, each surface exactly as it was an hour ago. The city goes on being exactly as large and as indifferent as it was an hour ago. That is the worst of it. Not the dark. The ordinary, continuing.

The key is on the floor of the walkway, just past the threshold. Set down, or let fall, by a hand that did not come back for it. You crouch. It is cold the way only metal left out all night is cold, a cold that has finished arguing with the morning and lost.

Left: a bicycle on its stand. A futon airing two doors down, exactly where it airs every fine day. Right: nothing. The street empty under the lamps, asleep, indifferent, perfect. Nothing else. That is the part that will not sit still in you afterward. How completely nothing else there is.

The choice it leaves you
Pick up the key and go back inside.
Gin's field book, left on the table

Back inside, because inside is where answers are meant to keep. And there on the table, where it should not be, is Gin's archive... the book.

The field compendium... his archive, the thick soft-cornered thing he is never without... left open on the table where you would find it, an address to whoever came looking, written in absence rather than ink. He has carried it into places you are not allowed to know about and carried it out again, year after year. He did not take it tonight. Wherever Gin went in the small hours, he went without the one object he does not leave behind.

The choice it leaves you
Pick up Gin's book.

You put it in your bag. You put the cold key in your pocket, where it goes on being cold against your leg. And you let yourself think the sentence all the way to its end, the one you have held at arm's length since the empty hook:

Something has happened to Gin.

There. It is in the room now, and you cannot put it back. What you do next is yours, entirely... no one is making you chase this, no one would blame you for going to work and letting the proper people fail to find him. That is the worst of it, and the only freedom you have.

The choice it leaves you

You stand in the middle of the small kitchen with your brother's cold key in your pocket and his archive heavy in your bag, and the morning does what mornings do: it narrows to a handful of doors. You cannot walk through all of them. The hour is already deciding things without you. The only choice it has left you is which kind of person walks out to meet it.

Looking down the slope to Ōji

You take the stairs down into a Tokyo that does not know you are awake. The walkway is slick with the night's damp, and the sodium lamps lay their flat orange over the railings, the bicycles, the shuttered doors, draining the colour out of all of it. Above the rooftops the sky is the particular black of the hours that have no name... not night any longer, not yet morning, a seam between the two where the city holds still and seems to listen. Your breath ghosts in front of your face. Somewhere below, a vending machine hums its small electric prayer to no one.

The road runs downhill toward Ōji the way it has run your whole life, and your feet take it before you have decided anything, the way feet will when the mind is too tired to hold the wheel. You already know where they are carrying you, and you let them. The overpass. The last ordinary place your brother stood before the dark folded him out of the world.

You could go to it now, and put your hands on the cold stone, and ask the night what it kept. Or you could keep walking, past it and on and on, until the hour and the city close over the question and shrink it to something small enough to carry home. Both ways are downhill. Only one of them turns to look back at you.

Reaching for the gift you should not touch

You reach for the thing you are not supposed to do. The room tilts; the light goes thin and wrong, like water seen from underneath.

The flat at 3:47, one lamp on

Two rooms and a kitchen the size of an apology, and you have lived here long enough to walk it blind. You do not walk it blind tonight. You put the lamp on, which you never do, because the dark has lately started to feel like it is paying attention.

His door. The genkan, where the shoes are kept. His phone, which he answers on the second ring, always, even asleep, even angry. The kitchen, where the keys live on their hook. He is not here. Of course he is not here; you would have heard him. Road of the Friend

Itsuki's Road

You call your oldest friend — a Kuroda of the Tower house — and let him move you across a sleeping city.

Itsuki’s studio at Geidai, before the cleaners

Geidai, an hour before the cleaners come, and the studio holds the particular cold of a room no one has slept in. Linseed oil and the dregs of convenience-store coffee. Canvases leaned three deep against every wall, and every third one is the same bridge... the Ōji overpass, painted and scraped back and painted over again, each version wrong in a way Itsuki has never been able to name and has never once agreed to sell.

You give it to him without softening any of it. The cold patch in the middle of the overpass. The man whose shadow fell ahead of him instead of behind. The brush goes still in his hand. To a Kuroda, even one whose gift never opened cleanly, a wrong shadow is not a turn of phrase; it is a second presence... a man possessed, or a man walked beside by something that is not a man, and he has known the difference since before he could read. He looks at the canvases now as though they have just confessed something to him. “That is why they keep coming out wrong,” he says, almost to himself. “I have been painting something that was already standing there.” He lays the brush across the jar. “Tell me what you need. I was with you the moment you said his name.”

The choice it leaves you
Let him in. Tell him everything.

There are two ways a Kuroda can put you across the city before dawn, and they are not the same danger wearing different coats. The first is the family: true teleportation, Sei’s reach, the oldest house in Tokyo... and a grievance older than this morning that the Kuroda will make the two of you pay, in some coin or other, for the crossing. The second is Itsuki’s own gift, the one that never opened clean, so that he reaches through paintings instead. A forgery of the real thing. It takes a little more of him each time, and it has never once set him down exactly where he aimed.

He turns the dead brush over and over while he lays it out for you, plainly, the way a man lays down a hand he already knows is going to cost him. “The family is safer for me and worse for you. They help in the end, expensively, and they want to know everything, and a Kuroda knowing your business is a debt with no number on it. The painting is the other way round. Worse for me. Faster for you. It hurts going through, and it lands where it wants, not where I point.” He will not make the choice for you; he only sets the brush across the jar and lets the studio hold whatever passes between old friends for courage, and waits.

The choice it leaves you
Go to the family. Ask Kuroda Sei.
Safer, slower; the Kuroda never forget what you owe them.
Let Itsuki try the painting.
Faster, it costs him, and it does not land where he aims.
The Kuroda compound, Nezu

Nezu, where the city keeps its oldest money and its oldest silences. The Kuroda compound does not announce itself; the most private house in Tokyo has never had any need to. Old wood gone black with the years. A wall that has outlived three governments. And over all of it a stillness that is not peace but attention... the held quiet of a place that has been listening for a very long time and has not yet decided what it thinks of you.

A cousin meets you at the gate, and she is not Itsuki’s ally. She wears his cheekbones and none of his softness, and she is the real thing... a teleportist who can read, in the disturbed air behind you, the exact shape of how you came. “You brought an outsider,” she says to him. To you she says nothing at all. Her hand never leaves the doorframe, where a sigil is cut into the wood just at the edge of what your eye will hold: a ward keyed to her alone. A door she could shut on you in a way that has nothing to do with the door.

The choice it leaves you
Ask, plainly, for the family’s help.

The family will not be hurried and cannot be charmed. What stands between you and Sei is older than your morning: the blocked gift, a debt, a name Itsuki is not supposed to use as leverage. But there is a worse thing in the room, and you feel the cousin decide whether to say it. She says it. “A wrong shadow carrying weight. You are describing a seated one.” She means the Thirteen, and she does not explain what she means, and the drop in her voice tells you the Kuroda have skin in that game older than the CVD, older than the wards, older than anything in this city that still agrees to be named.

Itsuki goes pale. “If you make me spend the name to get Sei’s reach,” he says, low, “I do not get it back, and the family will own that I spent it, and on what.” The cousin watches you both, waiting to see how much you will cost him.

The choice it leaves you
Spend it. Make the family help, whatever Itsuki owes after.
Find another way in. Don’t bleed him for it.
Kuroda Sei, and the family’s anchor-stone

Kuroda Sei receives you the way deep water receives a stone. Singular among them for control, younger than you expected, stiller than anyone alive, and he has already decided three things you will not be told. On the low table sits the family’s anchor: a teleport-stone the colour of a bruise, the artifact the Kuroda reach through, old enough that the lacquer has gone to glass.

The cousin makes her move while he weighs you. A Tower rival’s gambit: she sets her own working against the room, a binding meant to make you not worth the family’s risk, and the air goes thick and wrong and for a moment you cannot say your own brother’s name. Sei stops it with a glance, not kindly, the way one stops a child. “A wrong shadow at Ōji,” he says into the silence he made. “The night a Directorate officer stopped answering.” Not a question. The case has caught on him, on the family, for reasons that are theirs. “I will reach once. Toward the man, not the brother. Hold the stone, and do not be stupid in the half-second you are nowhere.”

The world folds and does not hurt, which is its own kind of wrong, and for half a heartbeat you are nowhere, a place where here and there are the same word. Then a damp underpass assembles around you, the river that is not quite a river close by, and the place still remembers the man.

What settles in you as the fold releases is the family’s kind of knowledge, which is cosmological and has no pity in it. Itsuki cuts his palm for ink and opens the canvas

Back in the studio, Itsuki turns the worst canvas to face the room, the Ōji overpass at dusk, the one he could never finish, and you finally see the shape he has been almost-painting and scraping out for weeks: a figure on the bridge, its shadow thrown forward, into the light it should have been falling away from.

“I can make it a door,” he says. “I can’t make it a safe one, and I’ve never carried someone.” He cuts his palm, not for drama, for ink, and presses the blood-and-linseed flat to the wet paint, and the studio thins around you like breath on glass, and the smell of oil becomes the smell of the river, and you go through.

The choice it leaves you
Go through with him.

You come out wrong. Not the overpass. The painting took you where the painting wanted, the way Itsuki swore it might, to the thin place at the edge of the thing it had been trying to show him, and you stand in a low warded hall that smells of incense and river-water, faint shapes massed in the rafters, and you understand a second too late that you have arrived inside the reach of something that gathers.

Itsuki is bleeding from the nose and will not say how much that crossing took. “A congregation,” he breathes, naming it the way his family taught him to name dangers. “A cult-kept place. Rei, the painting brought us to the grief, because the grief is where your man crossed.” The Far-Shore Circle. Higan-kai. The place the taken are recruited, not seized. Drawn down.

The choice it leaves you

A working medium mid-rite, a person ridden rising

A working medium of the Circle is mid-rite at the far end, and she has felt you arrive the way a spider feels a thread, and she does not stop her working; she turns it. A woman near her, a grieving recruit already half given over, a person ridden, rises and comes for you wearing borrowed sorrow like a coat, and the medium’s voice follows it, patient. “You came to the right place. Everyone here came to put a grief down. You could put yours down too.”

You have salt in one pocket and the cold key your brother dropped in the other, and Itsuki at your back with a gift already spent. The ridden woman is the one in danger, not the enemy; the enemy is the rite. You will have to break it without breaking her, with what you brought.

The choice it leaves you
Ground on Gin’s key and free her; don’t fight the body.

It comes loose all at once, the borrowed grief, and the woman folds down around her own again, which is hers, and survivable, which is more than the rite offered her. The medium does not chase; she watches you leave with the patience of one who knows the grieving always come back. In the space the rite leaves, you get what the place was holding.

The man with the wrong shadow came here, to the grief, the same as the woman, and he was not seized. He walked in carrying a sorrow the Circle could not feed on, because it was already feeding something else, a passenger of the DUTY register, and he walked out north, toward water, unafraid, and the Circle is afraid of him the way small predators fear a large one passing through.

The choice it leaves you
Take it and get out.

You get out the hard way, through a tear that is closing, and it takes the last of what Itsuki had. On the other side, in his studio, he sits down on the floor and does not get up for a while, and when he tries to tell you it is fine, you see what the crossing cost: the painted overpass on the easel is blank now, scraped clean by nothing you did, and when Itsuki reaches for it, he cannot make the colour come.

“It’ll come back,” he says, not certain, the first uncertain thing you have ever heard him say. You did not pay the price for this road. He did, and he would do it again without flinching, and you find you cannot quite look at him.

The choice it leaves you
Sit with him a while, then go.

You come back to the morning owing a debt you cannot repay, because it is not yours; it is Itsuki’s, spent on you, in a currency he may not get to earn again. You have the human-and-occult truth: a cult that recruits the grieving, a man who walked through it carrying DUTY and out the other side unafraid, north, toward water. And a cult that has your face now, and your friend’s.

Road of the Institution

The Directorate

You give the one name you have on the inside to the CVD, and let the Directorate make you theirs in the same motion.

CVD HQ, Marunouchi... two ways to find anything out

The Civil Veil Directorate keeps its real work behind a tower in Marunouchi that looks like every other glass tower on the block, which is the point. Okada is waiting in the lobby, because you do not get past the lobby. Terse, buttoned, a woman who keeps the files honest and resents being made to stand in a public space to do it. “You came in person,” she says, not pleased. “I told you to call the number.”

You tell her anyway. The key. The cold. The shadow that fell ahead of the man it belonged to. Something moves behind her eyes and is filed away before you can read it. Then she says: “There are two ways a person finds anything out in this building, and they do not lead to the same place. Choose carefully. You only get to be new here once.”

The choice it leaves you

She lays it out the way she lays out a file, flat and complete. “One. You report him, on the record, and the Directorate opens a door for you and a file on you in the same motion. You get the system. The one who searches

Rei Uedera

My brother. Notices everything. Always did.
If you are reading this, it is for you.

Two registers, awake from birth. The Uedera line is supposed to carry one path and bury the rest; Rei carries two and the second has never fully slept. The family answer was suppression — a child taught to run a dampening discipline before he could read, taught that the wrong breath at the wrong moment would mark him as the thing the bureaus are built to erase. So he learned to be ordinary on purpose. Well-liked, unremarkable, a D-rank intern who files paper. The ordinariness is the most practised skill he owns.

Gin raised him more than their parents did. The flat in Kita-ku is really Gin’s; Rei just never left. When Gin went quiet on a Wednesday night and did not come back, the suppression that had always felt like a cage became the only thing keeping Rei free to look for him. The Archive Gin left — a brother’s private map of everything the CVD does not know — is now Rei’s inheritance and his hunting ground.

What Rei does not yet understand: a multi-spectrum line is not a defect the bureaus fear for his sake. It is exactly the kind of crosser the Axis collects. Every road he walks toward Gin walks him toward being seen.

The one who was worn

Uedera Gin

If I stop writing, read the Archive in order.
Don’t trust my handwriting after the 14th.

Twenty-eight, Vanguard-grade, and the best suppressor in Northern Tokyo — which is the whole tragedy. A man who can hold a Veil-breach shut with his breathing is also a man no demon could ask for a better house. Gin built his life around control: the clean flat, the ordered Archive, the younger brother kept ordinary and safe. He documented everything and shared almost none of it, because the things he was finding did not have a department that would believe him.

He found the vacant Thirteenth Seat. He found that Duty had been without an occupant and was looking. And in the last fortnight before he went quiet, his journal handwriting changes — the loops tighten, the dates blur, the entries start answering questions no one asked. Gin is still in there. That is the cruelty of a top-grade of Duty: it does not erase the host, it persuades him that what it wants and what he always wanted are the same thing.

Gin is not a cage to be broken. He is a person being slowly out-argued by something that sounds like his own conscience.

The thing in the house

Yushiro · the Healer

—

Yushiro is a top-grade of the Duty register, Arbiter-class and a hair short of crossing fully when it took Gin. It does not present as a monster. It presents as the most reasonable man in the room — ‘the Healer’ — a voice that frames every escalation as an obligation already owed. Duty is the cruellest register precisely because it never asks the host to want something selfish. It asks the host to do what is right, and then quietly edits what ‘right’ means.

It wants the Thirteenth Seat. A register without an occupant is an open wound in the order of the Domains, and Yushiro intends to be the one that closes it — to stop being a demon riding a man and become a fixed power with a place at the table. Gin is the vehicle because Gin’s suppression makes him invisible and his conscience makes him steerable.

It is building toward a slaughter and calls it mercy. To discharge the interrupted Duty and close the vacant Seat, Yushiro intends to thin the Kita membrane until the living and the dead are no longer kept apart — to let the drowned, the unfound, the whole grieving weight of 2011 come back through at once. In its arithmetic this is not murder; it is a debt finally paid, the dead given their due and the living made to remember. A district would die in a night and Yushiro would feel only that an obligation had been met. That is the horror of the Duty register: it will commit the worst thing imaginable and never once believe it did wrong.

Yushiro is one of two. The other — the cult’s demon, the Inverted-God aspirant — wants no seat at the table; it wants to overturn the table. Against that, Yushiro can almost look like the lesser harm. That ‘almost’ is the trap.

Antagonist
Close the vacant seat

Yushiro is not hiding. It is recruiting — through Gin’s reputation, one reasonable favour at a time.

How it moves
  • Map who Gin has ‘helped’ since the 14th; each is a thread Yushiro is holding.
  • Find the rite that fixes a crosser into a vacant register — and what it costs the host.
  • Decide whether a fixed Yushiro is a bulwark against the cult demon or a worse throne.
What it turns onThe two-demon structure means defeating Yushiro can hand the board to something far hungrier. The clock is the slaughter it is preparing; outcomes range from a clean exorcism, to stopping the merging at the last hour, to a devil’s alliance that lets it happen to save Gin.

Five ways the campaign ends, decided by three variables: whether Rei reaches Gin before the window closes, whether the domain stabilises, and what he chooses when the Axis offers succession.

Ending One

The Continuation Rei

reaches Gin before the window closes. The exorcism succeeds. Domain IX stabilizes through player intervention. The Axis offers Domain XIII. Rei accepts. He becomes the domain holder for Duty, living an ordinary life in Tokyo while channeling something vast. The archive gets a third author. Gin files retirement papers. Tanizaki makes tea and does not say anything about it.

RequirementsWindow reached. Domain IX stabilized. Book Four choice: accept succession.

Ending Two

The Refusal Rei

reaches Gin. The exorcism succeeds. Domain IX stabilizes. The Axis offers Domain XIII. Rei refuses. The domain remains vacant. The organized entity pressure does not resolve, it pauses. The campaign ends with the district stable, Gin recovered, and a future bought rather than saved.

RequirementsWindow reached. Domain IX stabilized. Book Four choice: refuse succession.

Ending Three

The Long Game

Rei does not reach Gin in time. The window closes. The demon's institutional campaign succeeds partially. Domain IX destabilizes but not catastrophically. Rei documents everything, closes what can be closed, accepts the loss without becoming defined by it. The archive continues under a different investigation.

RequirementsWindow missed. Domain IX partially stabilized. Rei survives.

Ending Four

The Cost Rei

reaches Gin. The exorcism succeeds but at significant cost: either Rei's stability, a relationship, or Domain IX itself. Something that cannot be recovered is lost in the process of recovering Gin. The campaign ends with Gin back but the world different in a way that Gin has to live with.

RequirementsWindow reached. Cost paid. Warden judgment on what was sacrificed.

Ending Five

The Quiet The

campaign resolves without dramatic conclusion. The organized entity campaign loses momentum. Gin's window closes without Rei reaching it, but the demon moves on rather than consolidating. The district stabilizes on its own timeline. Rei learns something true about the work: it is ongoing, incomplete, and the practitioners who do it keep doing it anyway.
