Chapter Two — The Archive written
The table, the book open to a page that wasn't there last night

You clear the cold dinner-for-two off the table without quite looking at the second plate, and you set the Archive down in the cleared space like a thing that might wake. It is heavier than a book that size has any business being. Gin's hand is on every page — the small, exact print of a man who trusted ink more than memory, who once told you that the difference between a practitioner who lives and one who does not is whether they wrote it down before the night made them doubt it.

There is an index card slipped under the front cover, and on it, in that same hand but pressed harder than the rest, a single instruction: If I stop writing, read it in order. Out of order it lies by omission. You did not see it last night. You are almost certain you did not see it last night. The flat is the particular quiet of a place that has decided to listen, and somewhere under the floor the building's old pipes tick as they cool, counting down to nothing.

The choice it leaves you
Read from the first page, exactly as he asked.
The slow way. Gin built the order for a reason, and the reason is that some of these names only make sense once you already believe the ones before them.
Skip to the last entries — the ones near the date he went quiet.
The fast way, and the forbidden one. The Archive 'lies by omission' read out of order; you would be trusting the most compromised pages first.
Four nodes, four locks, four people you have never met

However you come to it, the shape of the thing resolves the way a face resolves out of static once you stop trying to see it. The Archive is not a journal. It is a key-ring. Gin mapped the unregistered network of Northern Tokyo the way the Directorate never could — not as a list of suspects but as a map of trust, who would open a door for whom, and at what hour, and after which password — and he locked the live parts of it behind four people. Four primary nodes. A baker who does not know what he is. A clinic behind a blue curtain. A café that keeps Tuesday evenings free for the kind of customer who comes in already knowing. A shrine room behind a Korean restaurant in Shin-Ōkubo.

None of them will speak to a stranger. All of them, the margins say in Gin's smallest hand, owe him — not money, the dangerous kind of debt, the kind a careful man collects only once. He never collected. He left them for you instead, which is either the most generous thing your brother ever did or the most damning, because to walk this map is to announce, to everyone Gin protected, that the man who kept them safe is gone.

The choice it leaves you
Start with the baker — the harmless one.
Kondō Fumio, Jūjō Ginza, Thursday mornings, anpan. He does not know he is a practitioner; he only knows Gin was kind to him. Begin where it costs least.
Start with the clinic — the one who treats the wounded.
Hayashi Fumiko keeps the bodies the bureaus never see. If you are going to walk into the dark, start by knowing where you can crawl back to.
Start with the café — the one who reads you on sight.
Matsuda Keiko knew what you were before you sat down, the report said. Begin with the one who can tell you what you have become without your having to say it.
The first node opens, and the city tilts a degree toward you

The first door you knock on does not open to a stranger; it opens to Gin's brother, which is a different and more frightening thing. You watch the recognition cross their face — the small flinch, the recalculation, the careful arrangement of features into something that will not give too much away — and you understand that you have walked into a story already in progress, one in which you are the only character who does not know the plot.

They tell you a piece of it. Not all of it; none of them will ever tell you all of it, because Gin trained them not to, and grief makes people loyal to the rules of the dead. But a piece: that in his last weeks your brother was wrong, the way a room is wrong after something has stood in it; that he was carrying something carefully, quietly, the way you carry a thing you have decided to die for rather than set down; and that whatever it was, he was certain — they all say this, each in their own words, and it is the part that frightens you most — that he was protecting you.

The choice it leaves you
Ask what he was protecting you from.
The question under all the other questions. They may not answer. The answer, when it comes, is not a what. It is a who, and it is wearing a face you know.
Ask what he was carrying.
Not who, but what. The Thirteenth Seat is a phrase you do not have yet, and they will not give it to you whole — but they will give you the shape of the hole it left.
Chapter Three — The Last Week written
A briefing he skipped, in a building that does not forget

The nodes give you a week — Gin's last ordinary week, reconstructed out of the things he did that were not ordinary. He skipped a Directorate briefing on the Thursday, the first he had missed in nine years, and a man like Gin does not skip the thing he has done nine hundred times unless the thing he is doing instead is larger. He closed a case on the Friday, a drowning out by the Arakawa, and closed it too fast and too clean, the file signed off in a hand that the clinic's blue-curtain woman, who has seen a great deal of Gin's handwriting across a great many discharge forms, says was already beginning to change.

And on the Saturday he went to an address that is in no file, no registry, no map the Directorate keeps — a place his own Archive notes only as the low place, where the water remembers — and after the Saturday the entries stop being entries and start being arguments. Gin arguing with himself, on paper, in a hand that tightens with each line, about whether a debt that was never his to pay might nonetheless be the only honest thing left to do.

The choice it leaves you
Trace the drowning he closed too fast.
The case is a thread Yushiro is holding. Pull it and you learn how Gin's conscience was turned into a leash — one reasonable mercy at a time.
Find 'the low place where the water remembers.'
The Saturday address. This is the riverbank at Obon, the seam where the drowned come home — and where something offered Gin a way to give them their due.
What a top-grade of Duty does to a good man

You begin, slowly, against your will, to understand the shape of it — and understanding is worse than not, because it forecloses the comfortable lie that your brother was simply taken. He was not taken. He was persuaded, and he is being persuaded still, and the cruelty of the thing wearing him is that it never once asked him to want something selfish. It asked him to do what was right. It found the vacant place in the order of the world where an old obligation had gone unmet, and it framed every step toward catastrophe as a duty already owed, until the line between what Gin wanted and what it wanted stopped being a line and became a rhyme.

He is still in there. That is the part you will carry whichever way this ends. Not a cage to be broken open, not a man replaced by a monster wearing his coat, but a person being slowly out-argued by something that sounds exactly like his own conscience, and winning the argument the way the tide wins — not by force, but by patience, by being there every hour, by never raising its voice.

The choice it leaves you
Read the Archive entries after the 14th anyway.
He told you not to trust the handwriting after the 14th. But the only way to learn to tell his voice from the other is to read where they have started to blur. It will cost your Stability.
Stop, and find a way to reach him directly first.
Not the paper. The man. There are lucid windows, the nodes say, and they are shrinking. Reach one before you let the after-the-14th pages into your head.
Interlude — The Door That Locks Itself written
The handyman, on the cold spot by the genkan

Iwata has fixed forty-one doors in this building over nineteen years and he tells you so before he tells you anything else, because he wants it understood, up front, that he is not a man who believes in things. He kneels in the genkan of Gin's flat with his toolbox open and his reading glasses pushed down his nose, and he is talking the whole time the way men talk when the talking is a wall they are putting up against the quiet. The August heat is sitting on the whole city like a wet hand — you can feel it through the open window, the cicadas grinding it out — but here, in the meter of floor between the door and the step up into the room, the air is wrong. Not cold like a draught. Cold like a held breath. Cold the way the inside of a well is cold in summer, a coldness that has been kept rather than made.

It started after Obon began, he says, not looking at you, working a screwdriver he doesn't need into a screw that doesn't need it. The tenant downstairs said the ceiling sweated. I came up to check the seal on the window. There's no leak. There's nothing. But you stand here — he taps the threshold with the screwdriver, twice, on the exact line where the cold begins — and you'll feel the building think you're a stranger. He laughs at his own phrase and the laugh comes out thin. You are standing in your brother's home, in the gap his coat used to hang in, and the cold is reading you, finding you in the dark and deciding what you are, and you understand with a flatness that is worse than fear that Iwata has described it exactly.

You ask him, carefully, when he last saw Gin. He thinks about it longer than the question should need. That's the thing, he says. I saw him on the Sunday. Said good morning on the stairs. Polite man, your brother, always was. He sets the screwdriver down. But the missus in 3C, she swears she saw him Tuesday, by the door, fitting his key. And I checked the log. He hasn't badged the front entrance since the Saturday before. So one of us is wrong about a man we both know on sight. He says it like a small accounting error. He does not yet hear what he has said: that the building has been admitting someone who looks like Gin, on a day Gin was not here, through a door that has begun to keep its own counsel.

The choice it leaves you
Ask Iwata to stand on the cold line and describe exactly what he feels.
He'll do it because you asked, and he'll go pale, and you'll have made an unsuperstitious man feel the seam in his own home. Some kindnesses you can't take back.
Press him on the Tuesday sighting — get the neighbour's exact words.
The figure at the door wore Gin's face and fitted Gin's key. Confirming it makes the thing wearing your brother specific, locatable, real — and means it has been practising.
Say nothing; let him fill the silence.
Men building a wall against the quiet will hand you the brick that frightens them most, if you don't interrupt. But you'll have used his fear as a tool, and you'll know it.
The key that no longer fits

He shows you the lock because the lock is the reason he was called, and a lock is something he can hold. Gin's key — your brother's key, the one from the envelope the Directorate released to you with the rest of his unremarkable effects — slides halfway into the cylinder and stops. Iwata takes it from you and tries it himself, gently, then less gently, his craftsman's patience curdling into the small affront a tradesman feels when a thing he understands refuses to behave. This is his key, he says. I cut the spare myself, three years ago. It fit yesterday. I oiled it yesterday. He withdraws it, examines the teeth against the light, finds nothing wrong with the teeth. The fault is not in the key. The fault is in the lock, which has, sometime between yesterday and now, quietly become a lock for a different door.

Then he shows you the other thing, the thing he didn't want to call about and called about anyway. He pulls the door shut from the inside and lets go. And the deadbolt turns. By itself — no hand on it, no draught strong enough, the mechanism rotating its full quarter-turn with the heavy, satisfied chunk of a bolt seating home. The flat has locked you both in. Iwata stares at the brass knob as though it has spoken to him in a language he half-remembers from childhood, and for a long moment neither of you reaches for it, because reaching for it means admitting that something in the room wanted the door shut and got its wish. It does that now, he says, very quietly, the wall finally down. Three times since Monday. It locks itself. Like it's keeping something in. Or, — and he can't stop the next part, it comes out of him sideways — like it forgot which side the inside is on.

You turn the bolt back. It comes easily, oiled, ordinary, a thing a man built and a man can undo. But you have felt it now, the small autonomy in the room, the sense that the flat is no longer a container for your brother's absence but a participant in it — that the cold by the genkan and the changed lock and the bolt that seats itself are not three faults but one fact, stated three ways, by a space that has learned to testify. Gin taught you, years ago, in the only register of his you ever fully trusted, that a place holds the shape of who lives there the way a glove holds a hand. This place is holding a shape. It is just no longer your brother's.

The choice it leaves you
Take the lock apart yourself, look for what changed.
You won't find a mechanism. You'll find that the cylinder's pins have reseated to a pattern that matches no key you own — the lock retuned to admit a tenancy that isn't his. Knowing the shape of the new key tells you who the door now answers to.
Let the door lock itself again and watch it happen.
Watching means standing in the cold line while the bolt turns, offering yourself to whatever decides the door. You'll learn whether it's keeping something in — or measuring you for the inside.
Get Iwata out and seal the flat. Don't let it practise on him.
Protecting him is right. It also means you stop having a witness, and you walk away from the one room in Tokyo that is willing to tell you the truth, because the truth is costing an innocent man his sleep.
What changes tenancy, and what stays to attest it

Iwata packs his toolbox the way a man folds a flag, slowly, with a ceremony he'd deny. At the door — which he now opens with his own master key, because Gin's no longer works and he will not say so out loud again — he stops and gives you the thing he has been circling for an hour. My grandmother, he says, kept the house in Niigata. She used to say a house knows when the owner's debt comes due. Said the cold spot was the house making room. He shakes his head at himself, at the disgrace of repeating it, a man who fixes things repeating a thing that cannot be fixed. Making room for what, I asked her. She never said. I think she didn't like the answer either. Then he is gone down the stairs, leaving you in the doorway of your brother's flat at the high heat of Obon, when the line between the living shore and the other is thinnest, and the dead come back across the water, and the lock has been recut for whatever is crossing.

You stand on the warm side of the cold line for a while. You think about the entries in the Archive that stopped being entries after the 14th. You think about the case Gin closed too fast, the drowning, the address in no file. You think about a debt that was never his to pay and the brother who would pay it anyway, in the one currency he had left, which was his own residency in his own life — and you understand, standing here, that this is what it looks like from the outside: not a vanishing, not a violence, nothing the Directorate has a form for. A change of tenancy. The slow administrative handover of a man to whatever he agreed to let in, witnessed only by a handyman who doesn't believe in things and a brother who is learning, room by room, to read the handwriting before he can bear to name it.

You take the key — Gin's useless key, the one that fits nothing now — and you put it in your pocket anyway, because it is the shape of a door he could once open, and because you are not ready to be a man who throws away the last key to his brother. Behind you, as you pull the door to from the warm side, the deadbolt turns. You do not reach for it. You let the flat keep what it is keeping, and you go down the stairs into the heat, and the cicadas take you back, and you carry the cold out into August in the small of your back, where it will sit for days, reading you, deciding what you are.

The choice it leaves you
Pursue the grandmother's phrase: a house making room. Find what that costs the one who lives there.
It's not folklore. It's the membrane logic stated in a kitchen idiom — and following it means accepting that Gin's flat is rehearsing, in miniature, what Yushiro means to do to the whole Kita ward.
Take the dead key to someone who reads locks the way Gin read bodies.
There is a man on the Grid who can cut a key from a refusal. He'll tell you what door yours now opens. He'll also want to know why you'd want to walk through it.
Go back to the Archive. The flat told you what; the nodes will tell you when.
Returning means reading in Gin's demanded order again, and his order is a leash too — the deeper you go in his sequence, the more you think the way the thing wearing him wants you to think.
Interlude — The Briefing They Kept Quiet written
Counter-Demon Verification Directorate, intake desk, 09:14

The CVD intake hall is the coldest warm room in Tokyo. They keep it at exactly the temperature that lets a salaryman keep his jacket on and a frightened person keep their hands from shaking, and the effect, after four minutes in one of the bolted plastic chairs, is that you stop being able to tell which one you are. The clerk behind the glass has a queue ticket dispenser, a stamp, and a face arranged into the specific blankness of a person who has decided in advance that whatever you have come to report is already accounted for. You slide your brother's name across the steel tray under the window and watch her type it, and you watch the small light of recognition that should cross any face when a name lands on a screen, and it does not come. Kanzaki Gin, she reads back, flat, and turns the monitor a careful two degrees away from you.

You tell her he has been gone since the Saturday before Obon. You tell her about the door that locks itself and the neighbour who saw a man wearing his face fit a key on a day he never badged the building, and you hear your own voice doing the thing voices do in rooms like this, going reasonable, going small, sanding the edges off the impossible so the impossible will fit through the slot under the glass. She listens the way water listens. When you finish she does not write anything down. I'm showing him as current, she says. Status active. Last verification — she leans toward the screen and you lean with her, both of you reading a date, and the date is three days ago. Three days ago Gin's flat was a held breath and an empty coat hook. Three days ago someone in this building looked at your brother's file and certified that he was, in the language of the form, present and accounted for.

You ask the only question that matters, which is who. The clerk's hand finds the stamp without her looking at it, the way a hand finds a railing on a stair it has climbed a thousand times. Verifications are system-generated, she says, and the sentence has the worn smoothness of something repeated until it stopped meaning anything, a stone in a river of other stones. If you'd like to file a discrepancy, take a ticket. The dispenser ticks. A paper tongue extends toward you with a number on it, and you understand, holding it, that you have just been handed your place in a line designed to be longer than your patience, by an institution that has already decided your brother is not missing, because someone took the trouble to make sure the form would say so.

The choice it leaves you
Take the discrepancy ticket and wait the line out, however long it runs.
Patience is a confession that you'll play by their clock. The line is engineered to outlast grief; the people who designed it are counting on you leaving before the truth comes due — and while you sit, the thing wearing Gin's face keeps badging doors.
Refuse the ticket; ask, by name, for the officer who signed the last verification.
Naming the signer makes you a person who reads the small print, and small-print readers get remembered. You'll learn whether a human signed it — or whether something is signing in a human's place, and has been for longer than three days.
Say nothing, photograph the date on the screen before she turns it away.
Evidence you weren't given is evidence someone will want back. The timestamp proves a living certification of a vanished man — but holding it makes you a thief of the one fact the Directorate was built to keep filed and quiet.
The liaison who agrees with everything

They send you, eventually, to a man named Sekine, whose office is a glass box on the fourth floor with a view of the air-handling units and a plant that is doing the slow, patient work of dying in a corner. Sekine is kind in the way that costs nothing — he stands when you enter, he offers you the good chair, he uses your brother's given name as if they had been at school together — and within ninety seconds you understand that his kindness is the interface, the soft cladding the building wears so that its hardness never has to touch you directly. He agrees with everything. He agrees that the situation is concerning. He agrees that the verification looks, on its face, irregular. He agrees, with a small sorrowful tilt of the head, that he can find no record of who authorised it, and he agrees so completely and so soon that you realise agreement is how this office says no.

You ask him to mark Gin missing — to change the one field that would turn the whole machine the other way, that would put your brother's face on a board instead of a verification on a server. Sekine's hands come together on the desk, very gently, two halves of a closing thing. Here is the difficulty, he says, and the difficulty, when he lays it out, is exquisite: to file a missing-person flag on a CVD-registered officer requires the counter-signature of his supervising desk, and Gin's supervising desk has, as of the last roster, no supervisor listed — the chair sits vacant, a small administrative weather system, pending an appointment that keeps not happening. So you see, Sekine says, almost apologetic, there is no one with the standing to declare him gone. The sentence lands in the cold warm room and sits there. A man has been erased not by force but by procedure, by the careful removal of every official who would have the authority to notice.

You watch Sekine's face for the lie and you don't find one, and that is the worst of it — he is not lying, he is reciting, he is a decent man reading you the rules of a game whose rules were written, recently and quietly, by someone who wanted exactly this room to produce exactly this answer. When you ask who left the supervising chair empty, Sekine glances, just once, at the dying plant, as if it might know, and tells you the previous supervisor took a personal leave in late July. For health, he says. He'd been — Sekine searches for the word a kind man uses for a colleague who came apart — helped, I think, by someone. A consultation. He spoke very warmly of it. The word helped goes into you like cold water down a wall, because you have heard it before, in your brother's flat, in the cadence of a thing that does favours, and you know now that the briefing they kept quiet was never a briefing at all. It was a clearing of the room.

The choice it leaves you
Press Sekine for the former supervisor's name and where his 'consultation' came from.
Follow the word 'helped' and you walk straight up the chain of favours toward Yushiro's hand. But you'll be asking a kind man to name the thing that hollowed his colleague, and kind men who name it tend to be next.
Accept that the chair is the lock; ask how a new supervisor gets appointed — and by whom.
Whoever fills the vacant desk controls whether Gin is ever declared gone. Learning the appointment path tells you who benefits from the silence — and may reveal that the vacancy itself is the plan, a small rehearsal for a larger empty seat.
Thank Sekine, leave, and stop trusting the building entirely.
Walking out intact protects you from a system that administers absence for a living. But you surrender the only channel that could make Gin official, and you go back to facing the thing alone, with no one above you who will write his name on any board.
The corridor, and the badge that still works

You leave through the same turnstile you entered by, and on instinct — the bad, useful instinct of a person who has stopped believing the official story — you take out the visitor's lanyard they clipped to you at the door and look at it properly for the first time. It is not a visitor's pass. The clerk, in the long blank ritual of intake, made an error or was made to make one, and the card in your hand carries a low-grade access tier under a name that is not yours: it is a temporary credential issued, the small print says, on behalf of the supervising desk, K. Gin. For one impossible second you read it as a message from your brother, a hand reaching out of the machine, and then the colder reading arrives. The vacant chair is not empty. Something is sitting in it long enough to issue credentials, quietly, in your brother's authority, and it has just — by accident or by design — handed you the keys to the building that says he isn't missing.

The corridor outside intake runs long and fluorescent toward the lifts, and the cold that lives in Gin's flat is here too, you realise, thinner, dilute, a single thread of it laid along the floor like a tripwire of held breath. You can feel where it leads. It does not lead toward the exit. It leads deeper, toward the archive floors, toward the locked registry where the verification was born, and you stand at the seam between the way out and the way in with a credential in your fist that should not exist, issued by an authority that should be a dead man, warm under the lights of an institution that has decided, on excellent paper, that nothing is wrong.

Somewhere below you a printer is running. You can hear it through the floor, the soft mechanical patience of a document filing itself, certifying again, on schedule, that Kanzaki Gin is present and accounted for — a small machine keeping a large lie current while the city outside grinds out cicadas and lights its lanterns for the dead it is willing to admit are gone. You think of the word helped. You think of an empty chair that issues keys. And you understand, with the flat clarity that has replaced fear in you over these three days, that to find your brother you will have to walk in the one direction the building was built to prevent, carrying a borrowed authority that is really a borrowed wound.

The choice it leaves you
Use the credential now, while it works, and go down toward the registry.
The card is an accident no one has caught yet, and accidents get caught. Going now buys you the registry before someone reconciles the error — but you'd be walking into the system's hardest room wearing the authority of the thing you're hunting.
Pocket the card, leave, and come back when you understand what tier it really opens.
Caution keeps you alive past today, but the credential is live and logged; every hour it sits in your pocket is an hour the vacant chair has to notice it gave you a key, and to decide what a borrowed wound is worth taking back.
Photograph the card and report the error yourself, on the record.
Confessing the accident makes you the honest one — and erases your only way in. The instant the credential is voided, the building reseals, and the printer downstairs keeps certifying your brother alive to a system that will no longer let you near the floor where the lie is made.
Interlude — The Form That Files Itself written
Registry sub-level, the room where absence is kept

The registry floor is two levels below the dying plant and the kind man, past a door that reads your borrowed card and decides, after a pause long enough to make your scalp prickle, that you are allowed. Inside, the cold is no longer a thread; it is the weather of the room. Rows of grey cabinets stand in the half-light like a congregation that has been waiting a long time, and the only sound is the printer you heard through the floor, a single machine at the end of the central aisle, exhaling a page every few minutes into a tray already thick with its own patience. You walk toward it past drawers labelled in a hand that is too even to be a hand, and you understand that this is not a place where records are stored. It is a place where records are kept alive — fed, certified, renewed — so that the people they describe can be quietly held in the condition the building prefers them in.

The page in the tray is your brother. KANZAKI GIN — STATUS: ACTIVE — VERIFICATION: AUTOMATIC — NEXT: 06:00. Below it, the page beneath it, the same. Below that, the same, the same, a stack of identical certifications climbing back through the days like sediment, each one a small official act of looking at an empty chair and writing present. You lift the stack and the dates run backward under your thumb — three days, a week, two — and then the pattern breaks. The oldest pages in the tray are different. They are not automatic. They were signed, once, by a hand, before the machine took over, and the hand that started the lie wrote a single initialled note in the margin of the first one, a note no automatic system would generate, in ink that has gone the brown of old blood: per consultation — Y. He asked so little.

You stand in the cold congregation with the proof in your hands and you feel the shape of it close around you the way the cold closes around a stranger at Gin's threshold. Someone began this by hand. Someone sat at a desk in late July and made the first false certification himself, willingly, gratefully, because a consultation had asked so little of him and given, you can only assume, something he wanted very much. And then he taught the machine to do the rest, set absence to renew itself at six every morning, and walked away into a personal leave for his health — and the form has been filing itself ever since, a wound that has learned to bleed on schedule, keeping your brother present in the only ledger that could ever declare him gone.

The choice it leaves you
Take the signed originals — the hand-written pages — and leave the automatic ones.
The signed pages name a human author and the favour that bought him. They're the thread back to Yushiro. But they're also the only physical proof, and the room will know they're missing; you'd be pulling the first stone from a wall built to fall on whoever pulls it.
Stop the printer — break the six-o'clock renewal and let the status lapse.
Lapsing the certification could finally turn Gin from 'active' to a gap the system has to explain. It could also trip whatever watches this room, and a status that lapses is a status someone is alerted to defend — you don't yet know what arrives when the lie stops being current.
Photograph everything, touch nothing, and read the room for what else it keeps alive.
Other cabinets, other congregations — how many people are being kept 'present' down here? Knowing the scale tells you this was never about Gin alone. But the longer you read, the longer the borrowed card sits logged in a room it was never meant to open.
The other drawers

You should leave. You photograph the signed pages and you should turn for the door, and instead the cold pulls you sideways, down a row you had no reason to enter, the way grief pulls a hand toward a drawer it shouldn't open. The cabinets here are older, and the labels are names, and the names have small coloured tabs, and after the third one you understand the colours. Green is active. Green is the lie. There are more green tabs in this row than a building should need, a whole quiet harvest of people the registry is certifying present, each with a tray, each with a printer somewhere exhaling them alive at six in the morning, and beside each of the oldest, in the same brown ink, the same initialled note: per consultation — Y. The Healer has been busy. The Healer has been busy for longer than your brother's three days, asking so little of so many, and filing the favour in a room two floors below the kindest man you ever met.

One drawer is open already. You did not open it. It hangs out an inch into the aisle as if recently consulted, and the file inside is not green and not anyone's name you know — it is a registry of the registry, an index, and the entry the index is open to is not a person. It is a seat. DOMAIN XIII — STATUS: VACANT — VERIFICATION: PENDING APPOINTMENT.
