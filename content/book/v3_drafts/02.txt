#title Itsuki
#part Book One. The Archive

Rei did not sleep again. He sat at the kitchen table with the cold key in his fist until the window over the sink turned from black to dark blue to the grey of dirty dishwater, and at half past four, when he could not bear the quiet of the flat for one more minute, he put on his shoes and went out.

He took the first train of the morning south from Ōji. The carriage was almost empty and much too cold, the air conditioning roaring away as if it were the middle of the afternoon. A man in a creased white shirt was asleep across from him with his arms folded over his briefcase and his tie pulled loose, his head nodding forward a little further at every station and jerking back up again. At the far end of the carriage a young woman in a waitress uniform was eating a rice ball very slowly, holding it in both hands, taking tiny bites and looking out of the window, as if she wanted it to last the whole way to wherever she was going. The station names went past in a calm, kind, recorded voice. Tabata. Nippori. Uguisudani. Outside, over the rooftops and the tangles of electric wires, the sky was getting paler by the minute.

He kept his hand in his pocket around the key the whole way. It did not warm up. Not even a little.

[break The park]

At Ueno he got off and climbed the long stone steps up into the park.

Ueno park sits on a hill above the station, a great green island of old trees and museums and temples and ponds in the middle of the city. At five in the morning it belonged to the crows and to a handful of homeless men asleep on flattened cardboard under the trees, their shoes placed neatly side by side next to their heads. The crows were everywhere. Tokyo crows are enormous, the size of small dogs, glossy blue-black with thick heavy beaks like a pair of pliers, and they walk along the paths with a slow, rolling, unbothered strut, as if they own the place and are only letting the humans stay out of kindness. One of them was standing on a bench beside the path, and it turned its head as Rei went past and watched him with one bright black eye, following him all the way along the path, its head turning slowly, as if it were making a careful note of his face for later.

He walked faster.

On the far side of the park, behind a long wall and a row of tall ginkgo trees, stood Geidai. That is the short name everyone uses for Tokyo University of the Arts, the most famous art school in Japan. Every year thousands of young people try to get in, and only a few hundred do. Itsuki was one of them, and he talked about it, when he talked about it at all, as if he had won it in a raffle he had not meant to enter.

[break The studio]

[img scene_itsuki_dawn.png | Itsuki's studio at dawn]

Itsuki's studio was on the third floor of an old building at the back of the campus, up a staircase that smelled of turpentine and dust and instant noodles and, faintly, of somebody's burnt toast. The door was open. Itsuki always left it open when he worked through the night, because he said the air in there turned to glue after midnight and he needed the draught to keep from passing out.

It was a big, untidy, high-ceilinged room with tall windows, and it was full of paintings. They leaned against every wall, three and four deep, some finished and some barely started, some taller than Rei and some no bigger than a postcard. A laundromat at four in the morning, all white machines and one old man asleep on a plastic chair with his mouth open. A fish market under hard white lights, silver bodies piled on ice, a woman in rubber boots laughing at something out of the picture. A ginger cat asleep on top of a vending machine. A little girl in a yellow raincoat jumping into a puddle so big it reflected the whole sky. A crowd crossing the big intersection at Shibuya in the rain, a hundred umbrellas, and in the middle of them all, one person with no umbrella, looking straight out of the canvas. They were all completely different from each other, and they were all, somehow, unmistakably Itsuki's. There was always something in them that made you look twice. Something a little too quiet, a little too still. People who could not say why found them hard to stop looking at.

Itsuki was sitting on a stool in front of a canvas on an easel by the window, in the same enormous grey hoodie he had worn to the festival, with his knees up and his bare feet hooked over the rung of the stool. There was blue paint on his hands and on his wrist and in his hair, a smear of it across one cheekbone like a bruise, and there were black rims under his fingernails. He had a paintbrush clamped between his teeth like a cigarette, and a real cigarette, unlit, tucked behind his ear, as if he could not decide which one he needed more.

He heard Rei on the stairs. He did not turn round at once. He finished the line he was painting first, very carefully, leaning in close to the canvas with his eyes narrowed, and then he took the brush out of his mouth and said, still looking at the painting, "If you're the cleaner, I'm sorry about the sink, I'll do it later, I promise, there's a reason it's blue."

"It's me."

Itsuki swung round on the stool so fast he nearly went over.

He looked at Rei. He really looked, the way he looked at things he was going to paint, his quick tired eyes moving over him from top to bottom and missing nothing at all. Rei watched him see it. The same clothes as the night before, crumpled now. A dried smear of sweet squid sauce still on one sleeve. The grey face of someone who had not slept. The way his right hand was buried in his pocket, closed tight around something.

The teasing look went out of Itsuki's face as if someone had switched it off.

"Rei," he said quietly. He put the brush down on the edge of the easel without looking at where it went. "What happened? You look like you've been sitting up all night in the dark." He stood up. "Did he come home?"

Rei shook his head.

For a second Itsuki did not move. Then he pulled a second stool out from under a table covered in old paint tubes and coffee cans full of brushes, knocked a pile of sketches off the seat onto the floor without even looking at them, and pushed it across to Rei.

"Sit," he said. "Sit down before you fall down. Tell me."

[break The key]

Rei sat. He told him.

He told it badly, in the wrong order, starting with the plate of ginger pork and then going back to the hook and then forward to the phone, and Itsuki did not interrupt him once. He sat on his own stool with his elbows on his knees and his paint-stained hands hanging down between them and listened, his eyes never leaving Rei's face. When Rei got to the line of light under the door, Itsuki's hands went very still. When Rei got to the lamp, and the open book, and the chair pulled out as if someone had just got up from it, Itsuki let out a long slow breath through his nose.

"And this was in the drawer," said Rei, and took his hand out of his pocket, and opened it.

The key lay on his palm. A plain brass door key on a plain steel ring. It did not look like anything.

Itsuki reached out and picked it up between his finger and thumb. And then he made a small sharp sound, half a gasp and half a laugh, and nearly dropped it, catching it again clumsily against his chest.

"What the hell," he said. He held it out at arm's length and stared at it. "Why is it so *cold*? It's like you've had it in the freezer. Rei, it's thirty degrees outside, how is a key that cold?"

"It's been that cold since I found it. Since quarter to four." Rei held out his hand, and Itsuki gave it back to him at once, quickly, holding it only by the steel ring, the way you hand someone a cup that is too hot. "It doesn't warm up. I've been holding it for an hour on the train. It doesn't warm up at all."

Itsuki wiped his fingers slowly on the front of his hoodie, leaving four long blue streaks on the grey cotton, and did not seem to notice he was doing it. He was looking past Rei now, at something over his shoulder. His face had gone very strange.

Rei turned round on the stool to see what he was looking at.

It was the painting on the easel by the window. The one Itsuki had been working on when he came in.

It was a room at night. A dark, small, plain room, with the shape of a made bed in the shadows and the edge of a drawn curtain. And in the middle of it, a desk. And on the desk, a lamp with a green glass shade, bent low, making a circle of warm yellow light on the dark wood. And in the circle of light, lying open, a book.

The paint was still wet. It shone.

Neither of them said anything for what felt like a very long time. Somewhere down in the building a door banged, and someone laughed, and a radio came on playing the morning news.

"When did you paint that?" said Rei at last. His voice did not sound like his own.

Itsuki swallowed.

"Last night," he said. "When I got back from the festival. I couldn't sleep, so I came up here, and I didn't know what to paint, so I just started." He was still staring at it. "I didn't plan it. I don't plan them, mostly. I just wanted to paint a lamp. I don't know why. I just really, really wanted to paint a lamp." He laughed, a thin, cracked sound with nothing funny in it at all. "I did the light first. I remember that, because it was the hardest part. The light on the wood."

"What time?"

"I don't know. Three. A bit after three. The building was dead quiet, the last guard had gone round." Itsuki dragged his eyes away from the canvas and looked at Rei. "Rei. Is that... that's not..."

"That's Gin's lamp," said Rei. "That's Gin's desk."

Itsuki sat down very slowly on his stool, as if his legs had decided to do it without asking him.

[break Wednesday]

For a while neither of them could think of anything to say. Itsuki took the cigarette from behind his ear and turned it over and over in his fingers without lighting it, the way he always did when he was frightened and did not want anyone to know. Then, abruptly, he pulled his phone out of the pocket of his hoodie and unlocked it and held it out, screen first, to Rei.

It was his chat with Gin.

Gin's last message was on Tuesday afternoon. A photograph of a bowl of ramen with a soft-boiled egg cut in half on top, the yolk orange and shining, and underneath it, in Gin's usual short neat way, *Tell Rei this place is better than his.*

After that there were only Itsuki's messages, going down the screen, getting shorter. Wednesday. *you alive? finished the index thing u wanted, it's on the usb.* Thursday. *gin. answer me or I'm telling rei you eat konbini pudding at 2am standing in front of the fridge.* Friday morning. *ok seriously. are u ok.*

None of them said *Read*.

"He didn't answer me since Wednesday," said Itsuki. His voice was low and rough. "I thought he was just busy. It's Obon, you know, it's always mad for them in Obon. I thought he'd turn up with dinner on Saturday like always and laugh at me for worrying." He rubbed his face with the back of his wrist, smearing the blue paint across his cheek even further. "I should have said something to you. Last night. At the festival. I almost did. I was going to ask you if he'd been weird lately, and then the grandmother grabbed me, and then I forgot."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have guessed. I'm good at guessing. It's basically the only thing I'm good at apart from this." He waved a hand at the paintings round the walls, without looking at them. Then he stood up, all at once, because Itsuki had never in his life been able to sit still when he was upset, and went and stood at the window with his back to the room and his arms wrapped round himself. "Okay. Okay. What do you need? Tell me what you need, and I'll do it."

That was Itsuki. He made jokes about everything, all the time, without stopping, so that nobody could ever get close enough to ask him a serious question. But the moment something actually mattered, he dropped everything he was holding and came and stood next to you, and he did not leave. In eight years of friendship, Rei could not remember a single time Itsuki had left.

[break The drawings]

"He comes here," said Rei slowly. "Gin. He's always coming here. He brings you food. You draw things for him. You never tell me what they are."

Itsuki turned round from the window. For a moment he looked almost guilty, like a boy caught with his hand in the biscuit tin.

"That's because he never tells me what they are either," he said. He went over to a tall metal shelf in the corner, stood on tiptoe, and pulled down a fat cardboard folder held together with parcel tape, so stuffed with paper that it bulged in the middle. "He comes by maybe once a week. Brings dinner, which honestly, I'd be dead without, I'd have starved in this room months ago. And while I eat, he describes things to me, and I draw them. Rooms. Objects. Shapes. Sometimes a face. Very carefully. He makes me do them again if they're not exactly right." He dropped the folder on the table with a thump that made the brushes rattle in their cans. "He says it helps him think. He pays me in gyoza. It's honestly the best job I've ever had."

He opened the folder.

There were hundreds of drawings inside. Pencil and ink and charcoal, a few in watercolour, all in Itsuki's quick sure hand. A narrow staircase in an old block of flats, with a pale blue smudge on the fourth step, as if the air above it were colder than the rest. A car seen from above, with the driver's seat shaded darker than the rest of it, much darker, as if something very heavy were sitting there. A woman's hand holding a teacup, with no woman attached to it, just the hand, ending at the wrist in nothing. A circle of small marks arranged like the face of a clock, but with far too many numbers. A cat sitting very upright on a cushion, with a small brass tag hanging from its collar. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table in the middle, and nothing else in it at all, and somehow that one was the worst, though Rei could not have said why.

And on one page, all by itself, a thin column of white light rising out of a black space, drawn in white chalk on black paper. Underneath it, in Gin's small exact pencil handwriting, a single word.

*Don't.*

"What's this one?" said Rei.

"No idea." Itsuki leaned over his shoulder to look, and his hair, smelling of turpentine and cigarettes and festival smoke, brushed Rei's cheek. "He described it so carefully. It took me six tries to get it right. Six. And when I finally got it, he went completely quiet. Just sat there looking at it for, like, ten minutes, not saying anything. And then he took me out for yakiniku and wouldn't talk about it the whole meal." Itsuki pulled a face. "That's when I knew it was something bad. Gin doesn't buy yakiniku for good things. Gin buys yakiniku when he's apologising for something."

Rei turned the pages slowly. He felt the pressure begin behind his eyes, softly, the way it always did. Some of the drawings were only drawings, ink on paper. But some of them were not. When he looked at the staircase for too long, he could almost feel the cold rising off the fourth step against his shins. When he looked at the long white room, he felt a heavy, grey, soft kind of tiredness settle over him, like a wet blanket, a tiredness that had nothing to do with not sleeping. He closed the folder quickly.

"Itsuki," he said. "Last night. At the festival. The old woman in your sketchbook."

Itsuki did not answer straight away. He sat down on the edge of the table, and picked up a brush, and began to clean it on a rag, very slowly, round and round, although it was already clean.

"I told you," he said. "My hand just does that."

"Does it happen a lot?"

"Define *a lot*." Itsuki kept wiping the clean brush. "Sometimes I paint something, and later I find out it was real. That's all. That's the whole gift. A room I've never been in. A face I've never seen. Once I painted a little boy sitting on a swing in a park in Nerima, a park I've never set foot in, and two weeks later I saw his face on the news." He threw the rag down on the table. "I'm a Kuroda. We're all supposed to see things. My father puts his hand on a doorframe and the door opens onto somewhere else. Everyone in that house can do something. And me, I see what I paint, after I've painted it, when it's too late to be any use to anybody." He gave a short, sharp laugh. "My family think I'm a waste of a good name. I think they're a cult with a very nice garden. It works out."

The Kuroda were one of the Tower Families. Rei had grown up hearing the name the way other people grow up hearing the names of old banks or famous shrines, as something that had always been there and always would be. The Tower Families were old houses, very old, older than the CVD and older than most of the city, families of people who could touch the veil and had been touching it for hundreds of years. Itsuki was a Kuroda, and he hated it. He wore the family name like a heavy coat somebody had forced on him in winter, a coat that did not fit and never would, that he would have given back years ago if he could only have found the shop it came from.

He looked up at Rei sideways, from under his paint-streaked hair.

"Why?" he said. "Can you?"

Rei thought about the old woman dancing to a drum nobody else could hear. About the door in his chest with the iron latch that Gin had taught him to close at the kitchen table when he was eight. About the things that used to come to the kitchen window at night, when he was small, and stand there in the dark, looking in.

"Sometimes," he said.

Itsuki looked at him for a long time. He did not make a joke. He did not laugh. He just nodded, slowly, once, the way you nod when a picture you have been working on for years finally comes out right and you are almost afraid to touch it in case it goes wrong again.

"Okay," he said softly. "Okay. That explains a lot, actually." And then, after a moment, with a ghost of his usual crooked grin, "That explains why you always look at the kitchen window like it owes you money."

[break The plan]

They had canned coffee from the vending machine on the landing and two egg sandwiches from the konbini on the corner, and sat on the paint-spattered floor of the studio to eat them, with their backs against the wall under the windows and their legs stretched out in front of them. The sun came up properly over Ueno and poured in through the tall windows and lit up all the paintings round the walls, one after another, the laundromat and the fish market and the girl in the yellow raincoat, and last of all the lamp on the easel, which looked, in the clean morning light, like nothing but paint. The first students began to arrive and drift past the open door with their bags and their coffee, yawning, without looking in. Somewhere on the floor below, a cello began to play scales, slowly and badly, getting stuck on the same high note again and again.

"Okay," said Itsuki, licking egg off his thumb. "Plan."

"I go home. I look in his room properly. I read the book. I look at everything."

"And you call the CVD."

"And say what? *My brother didn't come home and his spare key is freezing cold?*"

"Yes. Exactly that. Word for word." Itsuki pointed the last corner of his sandwich at him. "He's one of theirs, Rei. One of their own officers. They'll look for him. That's what they do."

Rei did not answer. He did not know how to explain it, even to Itsuki. Gin had always said very little about his work. But in the last year he had said even less than very little. Some nights he came home and sat down at the kitchen table and did not take off his coat for an hour, just sat there in it, with his hands round a cup of tea he did not drink. Some nights he stood at the living-room window and looked out at the dark, as if he expected someone to be standing on the walkway outside, three floors up. Once, a few months ago, Rei had asked him straight out whether something was wrong at work. And Gin had looked at him and smiled, and the smile had been a good one, a real one, warm all the way up to his eyes, and he had said, *Nothing you need to carry.*

"I'll call them," said Rei. "Later. After I've looked."

Itsuki studied his face with narrowed eyes.

"You're not going to call them."

"I'll call them."

"You've got your liar face on. It's exactly the same as your normal face, except your left eyebrow goes up about half a millimetre, like it's trying to escape." He leaned in and peered. "There. It's doing it now."

"My eyebrow is not doing anything."

"It's halfway up your forehead. It's practically in your hair." Itsuki finished his coffee and crushed the can flat on the floor with the side of his fist, *crack*, the way he always did, as if empty cans had personally offended him. "Fine. Don't call them. Be stubborn. But I'm coming over tonight, and I'm bringing the laptop. I built Gin a search program for his notes last year. If he left anything at all on a computer, anything, I'll find it."

"You built him a search program?"

"For his archive. He had too much paper, he was drowning in it. I scanned about four hundred pages for him over the winter and made him a thing where you type a word and it finds every page with that word on." Itsuki grinned, for the first time that morning, a quick crooked grin that changed his whole face. "I'm a genius. I keep telling people this."

"You do keep telling people," Rei agreed.

Itsuki got to his feet, and held out his hand, and pulled Rei up off the floor. For a second he did not let go. His palm was rough with dried paint, and warm, warmer than the key, warmer than anything had been since three o'clock that morning.

"Listen to me," he said. "He's Gin. He's the most annoying, organised, careful, over-prepared person alive. He labels his spices. He irons his *socks*. Wherever he is, whatever's happened, he left you something to follow. He always leaves you something to follow."

"Yeah."

"So go home," said Itsuki, "and find it."

[break The sketch]

Rei went. But at the top of the stairs he stopped, and turned, and looked back through the open door of the studio, because something made him.

Itsuki had not gone back to his easel. He was standing at the tall metal shelf in the corner, with the fat folder of Gin's drawings under one arm, and he had pulled out a single loose sheet of paper from somewhere at the very back, and he was holding it up to the light from the window and looking at it.

From the stairs Rei could see only a little of it. A pencil portrait. A man's face, half-turned away, with long hair falling past the jaw. Gin's face. Rei would have known it anywhere, even from the stairs, even upside down.

But there was something wrong with the drawing. Not with the lines. The lines were perfect, the best Itsuki had ever done. It was the shading. All around Gin's eyes, and in the hollows of his cheeks, Itsuki had laid in a soft grey shadow, very gently, with the side of the pencil, the kind of shadow you would draw on someone who was ill, or very tired, or standing in a room where the light had gone out. Except that the shadow did not fall the way light falls. It seemed to come from inside the face, not from outside it.

Itsuki stood looking at it for a long moment, with his jaw tight. Then he folded it in half, and in half again, very small, and pushed it into the back pocket of his jeans, and turned back to the easel and picked up his brush.

He did not know Rei had seen. And Rei, standing on the stairs with the cold key in his fist, did not know yet what he had seen. He only knew that he would remember it.

He went down the stairs and out into the bright morning and across the park full of crows, toward the station, and home.
