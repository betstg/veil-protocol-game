#title Itsuki
#part Book One. The Archive

Rei did not sleep again. At half past four he gave up pretending and went out.

He took the first train of the morning south from Ōji. The carriage was nearly empty and much too cold. A man in a white shirt slept across from him with his arms folded over his briefcase and his tie loose. At the far end a young woman in a waitress uniform ate a rice ball very slowly, holding it in both hands like something that might escape. Station names went past in a calm recorded voice. Tabata. Nippori. Uguisudani. Outside, the sky over the rooftops was turning from black to the colour of dishwater.

He had his brother's key in his pocket. He kept his hand on it the whole way. It was still cold.

At Ueno he got off and climbed up through the park. Ueno park sits on a hill above the station, full of old trees, museums and temples. At this hour it belonged to the crows and to a few homeless men asleep on flattened cardboard under the trees. The crows were enormous. Tokyo crows are the size of small dogs, glossy and black, with thick beaks like pliers, and they walk on the paths as if they pay rent. One of them watched Rei go past with its head on one side. He had the strange feeling it was memorising his face.

On the far side of the park, behind a long wall, stood Geidai. Geidai is the short name for Tokyo University of the Arts, the most famous art school in Japan. Only a few hundred people get in each year. Kuroda Itsuki was one of them, and he behaved as if this was an accident he was slightly embarrassed about.

[img char_kuroda-itsuki.png | Kuroda Itsuki]

Itsuki's studio was on the third floor of an old building that smelled of turpentine, dust and instant noodles. The door was open. Itsuki was standing in front of a canvas taller than he was, in an enormous grey hoodie pulled over a white shirt that had clearly cost a lot of money. There was blue paint on his hands, on his wrist and in his hair. His fingernails were rimmed with black. He had a brush in his mouth like a cigarette and a real cigarette, unlit, behind one ear.

He did not turn around. "You look terrible," he said around the brush.

"You haven't looked at me."

"I don't need to. You walk differently when you look terrible." Itsuki took the brush out of his mouth and pointed it at the canvas. "Tell me this bridge is wrong."

It was a bridge over a dark river at night. Itsuki had been painting the same bridge for two years. Every version was a little different. The lights changed. The water changed. The bridge never did.

"The bridge is fine," Rei said.

"The bridge is never fine. That's the point of the bridge." Itsuki finally turned around. He had a narrow face and quick eyes that were always a little tired, as if they had been awake for longer than the rest of him. They went over Rei once, from his messy hair to his untied shoes. The joke went out of his face.

"Gin," he said.

It was not a question. Rei took the key out of his pocket and held it out on his palm.

Itsuki looked at the key for a long time. Then he put down his brush, wiped his hands on his hoodie, which made everything worse, and took the key. He closed his fist round it and then opened it again quickly, as if it had bitten him.

"It's freezing," he said.

"It's been freezing since three forty-seven this morning."

"That's a weirdly exact time."

"Someone knocked. Three times. When I opened the door there was nobody. Just that, on the walkway. And half a cigarette."

Itsuki gave the key back. He did it carefully, holding it by the ring, not touching the metal again. Then he sat down on a paint-spattered stool and pulled out his phone and showed Rei the screen.

It was a chat with Gin. The last message from Gin was on Tuesday. A photograph of a bowl of ramen with an egg cut in half, and the words *Tell Rei this place is better than his.* After that there were only messages from Itsuki. Wednesday. *you alive? I finished the index thing.* Thursday. *gin. answer me or I'm telling rei you eat convenience store pudding at 2am.* Friday. *ok seriously.*

"He didn't answer me since Wednesday," Itsuki said. "I thought he was just busy. Obon. You know." He was quiet for a second. "I should have told you."

"You couldn't know."

"I could have guessed. I'm good at guessing. It's basically my only skill." He stood up again because he could not sit still. "Okay. What do you need. Tell me what you need and I'll find it."

That was Itsuki. He made jokes about everything, all the time, so that nobody could ever ask him a serious question. But the moment something actually mattered, he dropped everything and stood next to you, and he did not leave. They had been friends since they were twelve. Rei could not remember a single time Itsuki had left.

[need ch2_itsuki_studio | Geidai studio at dawn, Itsuki in an oversized grey hoodie with blue paint on his hands, unlit cigarette behind his ear, in front of a tall canvas of a bridge over a dark river, Rei in a black hooded jacket beside him]

"Show me the archive work," Rei said.

Itsuki blinked. "What archive work?"

"Gin's always here. He brings you food. You draw things for him. You never tell me what."

"Because he never tells me what." Itsuki went to a shelf in the corner and pulled down a fat folder held together with tape. "He comes by maybe once a week. He brings dinner, which honestly I'd die without. And he asks me to draw things. He describes them and I draw them. Rooms. Objects. Shapes. Sometimes faces. He says it helps him think. He's paying me in gyoza. It's the best job I've ever had."

He opened the folder on the table.

There were hundreds of drawings. Pencil, ink, some in watercolour. A narrow staircase with a cold spot drawn as a pale blue smudge on the fourth step. A car seen from above, with the driver's seat shaded darker than the rest, as if something heavy sat in it. A woman's hand holding a cup with no woman attached. A circle of small marks, like a clock face with too many numbers. A cat sitting on a cushion with a little brass tag around its neck. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table, and nothing in it at all, and somehow that one was the worst.

And on one page, by itself, a thin column of light rising from a black space, drawn in white chalk on black paper. Under it, in Gin's small, exact handwriting, someone had written one word in pencil. *Don't.*

"What is this?" Rei said.

"No idea. He described it really carefully. It took me six tries. When I got it right he went completely quiet and then he took me out for yakiniku and wouldn't talk about it." Itsuki pulled a face. "That's when I knew it was something bad. Gin doesn't buy yakiniku for good things. He buys yakiniku for apologies."

Rei turned the pages slowly. He felt the pressure begin behind his eyes, softly. Some of the drawings were just drawings. Some were not. When he looked at the staircase too long, he could almost feel the cold on the fourth step. When he looked at the white room, he felt tired in a way that had nothing to do with not sleeping. He closed the folder.

"Itsuki," he said. "Can you see things?"

Itsuki laughed, a short sound with no fun in it. "I'm a Kuroda," he said. "We're all supposed to see things."

The Kuroda were one of the Tower Families. Rei had grown up hearing the name the way other people hear the name of a bank or an old shrine. The Tower Families were old houses of people who could touch the veil. Some of them were older than the CVD. Itsuki was a Kuroda. He hated it. He treated the family name like a coat someone had given him that did not fit, and that he would return if he could find the shop.

"But can you?"

"I see what I paint." Itsuki shrugged with one shoulder. "Sometimes I paint stuff and later I find out it was real. That's it. That's the whole gift. The family thinks I'm a waste. I think they're a cult with a nice garden." He looked at Rei sideways. "Why? Can you?"

Rei thought about the woman on the train who did not sway. About the door in his chest with the iron latch.

"Sometimes," he said.

Itsuki looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, as if a picture he had been painting for years had finally come out right. He did not make a joke. That was how Rei knew he had understood.

[break The plan]

They ate canned coffee and two egg sandwiches from the konbini on the corner, sitting on the floor of the studio with their backs against the wall. The sun came up over Ueno. The first students arrived and walked past the open door without looking in. Somewhere a cello started playing scales, badly.

"Okay," Itsuki said. "Plan."

"I go home. I look in his room. I look at everything."

"And you call the CVD."

"And say what? My brother didn't come home and his key is cold?"

"Yes. Exactly that. He's one of theirs. They'll look for him."

Rei did not answer. He did not know how to explain the feeling. Gin had always said very little about his work. But in the last year he had said even less. Sometimes he came home and sat at the kitchen table and did not take off his coat for an hour. Sometimes he looked at the window as if he expected someone to be standing outside it, on the third floor. Once, a few months ago, Rei had asked him straight out if something was wrong at work. Gin had smiled, and the smile had been a good smile, and he had said, *Nothing you need to carry.*

"I'll call them," Rei said. "Later. After I've looked."

Itsuki studied him. "You're not going to call them."

"I'll call them."

"You've got your liar face on. It's the same as your normal face but the left eye does a thing."

"My left eye does not do a thing."

"It's doing it right now." Itsuki finished his coffee and crushed the can flat on the floor with the side of his hand. "Fine. Don't call them. But I'm coming over tonight. I'll bring the laptop. I built Gin a search thing for his notes last year. If he left anything on a computer, I'll find it."

"You built him a search thing?"

"For his archive. He had too much paper. I scanned like four hundred pages for him." Itsuki grinned for the first time that morning, a quick crooked grin. "I'm a genius, I keep telling people."

"You keep telling people," Rei agreed.

Itsuki stood up and offered his hand, and pulled Rei to his feet. For a second he held on. His hand was rough with dried paint.

"He's Gin," Itsuki said. "He's the most annoying, organised, careful person alive. Wherever he is, he left you a map. He always leaves a map."

"Yeah."

"So go home and find the map."

Rei went. At the door he looked back. Itsuki was standing in front of the bridge again, very still, with the brush in his hand and not moving it. In the morning light the painted river looked darker than it had at dawn, and Rei could have sworn there was a figure on the bridge that had not been there before. A tall man with long hair, standing at the rail, looking down at the water.

He blinked, and it was only paint.
