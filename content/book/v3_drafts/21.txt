#title Ink
#part Book Two. The Families

The tattoo shop had no sign. It was on the second floor above a noodle shop in Nippori, up a staircase that smelled of pork broth, behind a door painted dark green.

Rei knew the door before he reached it. He knew the smell that came out when it opened. Antiseptic, and green tea, and something sharp and dark that he later learned was ink. He had been here before. Many times. He had been thirteen, and fifteen, and seventeen, sitting on a plastic stool in the corner with a schoolbook open on his knees, pretending to read.

[img scene_tattoo_stool.png | Gin in the tattoo chair, years ago]

The memory came back so suddenly that he had to stop in the doorway. Gin in the big black chair under the lamp, with his long hair falling over one shoulder and his left arm stretched out on the padded rest. A woman bent over the arm with the needle, humming. Gin's face very calm, eyes half closed, the way it was when he drank hōjicha. The buzz of the machine. The little cup of green tea on the side table going cold. And Rei on the stool in the corner, watching the needle, not reading a single word.

He had never asked what the marks were. He had thought they were just Gin's. The way a face is someone's.

"Well," said a voice. "Look who finally came in on his own feet."

[break Uruma]

The man who ran the shop was lying on his back on the tattoo chair with his feet up, reading a manga, eating rice crackers out of the bag.

He was very tall and very thin, all long arms and long legs, folded into the chair like a heron. His head was shaved, and the whole of his scalp was covered in tattoos, fine black lines and small shapes and tiny writing, so that it looked like a map of a country nobody had found yet. Both his ears were full of silver rings, from the top to the lobe. His fingernails were painted black, and chipped. He was perhaps forty. It was hard to tell. He had a long pale gentle face, and when he looked up from his manga at the two of them in the doorway, his eyes were very dark and very still and very amused, as if he had just remembered a joke about them and decided not to tell it yet.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, look. It's the little one."

Rei stood in the doorway.

"You used to sit on that stool," said the man, pointing with a rice cracker. "With your maths book. Upside down, half the time. I never said anything. I thought it was very brave of you to pretend that hard." He swung his long legs off the chair and sat up. "Uruma. You won't remember. You were thirteen and you only had eyes for the needle."

"I remember the needle," said Rei.

He remembered something else too, now. Gin on the train home, years ago, saying that the man in Nippori made seals for the Division by day and tattoos by night, upstairs from the noodle shop, because the Division paid him for the first and his heart paid him for the second. Gin had said it fondly. He had said, *he's my oldest friend in that building, and the strangest.*

"Everyone remembers the needle." Uruma giggled. It was a strange giggle, high and quiet, like a child hiding behind a curtain. "Nobody remembers the man." He looked past Rei at Itsuki, who had come up the stairs behind him with a cardboard tube under his arm, and his eyes went slowly over Itsuki from his paint-stained hands to the switches nobody had tattooed yet between his knuckles. "And this is the artist. You smell of linseed oil and nerves. Lovely."

Itsuki opened his mouth. Closed it again.

"Gin-kun's little brother," said Uruma, to nobody. "In my shop. On his own feet." He sighed happily. "Your brother, you know, is the worst client I ever had. I told him. I said, Gin-kun, you are not a person any more, you are a walking notebook. There's no room left on you. Next time you want a ward you'll have to get it on your eyelids." He giggled. "He said he'd think about it. He would have, too."

Behind a bead curtain at the back of the room a woman's voice said, "Is that the Uedera boy?"

"It is," Uruma called back. "Don't come out, Tomoe-san, you'll frighten him." He leaned toward Rei and said, in a loud whisper, "Tomoe. She makes seals at the Division too, same room as me. Up here she tattoos the women and I tattoo the men. Nobody planned it. It just happened that way years ago and nobody ever changed it." He giggled. "She's much better than me and I'm not allowed to say so."

"I heard that," said the curtain.

"Don't touch anything," said Uruma pleasantly. "Some of it bites." He held out one long black-nailed hand. "Show me what you drew."

[break The latch]

They had argued about it for a week. What Rei should have.

The archive was very clear. A tattoo is not like a chalk ward or ink on paper. It never fades. It never switches off. Whatever it does, it does every second of every day for the rest of your life. Gin had written a whole page about it. *Choose like you are choosing a bone. You will not get another one.* The Division did not like tattoos. A tattooed practitioner made noise in their assessments. Their instruments could not read past the marks properly. That was one of the reasons Gin had so many.

"That's a reason to do it," Itsuki had said. "If they ever get you in a room and measure you, it'll come out all wrong."

But that was not why Rei wanted it.

He wanted a latch.

For eleven years he had closed a door in his chest every time the veil came close. He had learned from Sōun to close it slowly. He had learned from the building in Higashi-Jūjō what happened when he could not close it at all. He wanted something on his body that would hold the door when he was too tired to hold it himself. A real latch. Iron. Something that would not let go.

Itsuki unrolled the cardboard tube on Uruma's counter.

It was a drawing in black ink on thick white paper. A latch. An old-fashioned iron door latch, the kind on the gates of old temples, a curved bar dropping into a hook. But Itsuki had drawn it so that the curve of the bar was also the curve of a small footbridge, his bridge, and the hook was also a key, Gin's key, with the small dent in the ring. And woven round it all, so finely you had to look twice to see it, were the lines of the old ward glyph from the archive. The one for *closed*. The one Gin had marked in the margin with a single word. *Mine.*

Uruma bent over it so close that his nose almost touched the paper. He looked at it for a long time without blinking.

"You're good," he said to Itsuki at last, very softly.

"I know."

"No, you don't. You think you're good. You're actually good. That's much more frightening." He straightened up and smiled at Rei. "Where?"

Rei held out his left wrist. The inside. Where the skin is thin and you can see the blue of the veins.

"Where I'll see it," he said.

[break The needle]

It took three hours.

It hurt more than he expected and less than he feared. A sharp, hot, scratching hurt, like being drawn on very hard with a pin. On the bones of the wrist it was worse. Uruma hummed while he worked, the same song he had hummed six years ago over Gin's arm, an old song from Okinawa about a boat, and every now and then he giggled at something only he could see. Itsuki sat on the plastic stool in the corner, where Rei used to sit, and drew. He drew Rei in the chair. He drew Uruma's long black-nailed hands. He drew the lamp. He did not pretend to do anything else.

"He sat there," said Uruma, without looking up, at about the second hour. "Your brother. Same chair. First time he was twenty-two. Just joined the Division. Wanted a ward on his shoulder. Very serious. Very quiet. Brought you along because there was nobody to leave you with. You were thirteen. You fell asleep on that stool over your maths and nearly fell off it."

"I don't remember that."

"You wouldn't. You were asleep." The needle buzzed. "After that he came twice a year. Sometimes three times. Always brought you. Always sat in that chair with his eyes half closed like it was a hot bath. The last time was in July." He wiped the wrist with a cloth that smelled sharply of alcohol. "He wanted a mark on his chest. Over the heart. I asked him what it was for. He wouldn't say. I'd never seen the shape before. He'd drawn it himself."

Rei turned his head. "What did it look like?"

Uruma stopped the needle. He thought about it. For once he did not giggle.

"Like a door," he said. "A door, closed, with a hand on it. From the inside." He started the needle again. "I said, Gin-kun, that's not a ward. That's a prayer. And he said, Uruma, it's the same thing, if you mean it enough." He was quiet for a while. "Then he paid, and he took you home, and that was the last time I saw him."

Rei did not say anything. He watched the ceiling. In the corner, Itsuki had stopped drawing.

[break What it did]

When it was finished, Uruma wrapped the wrist in clear film and told him how to wash it and not to scratch it and not to go in a bath for two weeks, which upset Rei more than anything else he said.

Then he told him to close his eyes and open the door in his chest.

He did. He did it the way Sōun had taught him. A finger's width. The cold came in, the faint everyday cold that was always there, in every building in Tokyo, the background hum of the veil.

And on his wrist, under the film, the new mark went cold.

Not painful. A clear, sharp cold, like a drop of water from a tap. He felt it. And then he felt something else. A weight. Somewhere in his chest, behind the breastbone, where the door was. A weight settling into place. A small iron bar, dropping into a hook, with a click he did not hear but felt in his teeth.

The door was still open a finger's width. But now it could not open any further. Not unless he chose. Not unless he lifted the latch himself.

He opened his eyes. He was breathing hard.

"It works," he said.

"Of course it works," said Uruma. "He drew it, and I put it in." He jerked his chin at Itsuki. "And you meant it. That's all a mark is." He started cleaning his needles. "It'll go cold whenever something's near. Every time. Forever. You'll get used to it. Your brother used to say his left arm was a better alarm than any instrument the Division ever owned."

"Does it have a cost?" said Rei. "The archive said they all have a cost."

Uruma stopped cleaning. He looked at Rei with his dark still amused eyes.

"You'll never be able to throw that door wide open in one go again," he said. "Not without lifting the latch first. It'll always take you one breath longer than it used to." He shrugged. "Most days that's a gift. One day, maybe, it'll be the breath you don't have." He went back to his needles, and giggled once, softly, at nothing. "Everything costs something. That's what costs are for."

[break The stairs]

Outside, on the stairs that smelled of pork broth, Itsuki stopped and took Rei's wrapped wrist in both hands and looked at it through the film. The latch. The bridge. The key with the dent in the ring.

"It's the best thing I ever drew," he said quietly. "And it's on you. Forever."

"Is that bad?"

"No." Itsuki let go of his wrist. "No, it's... no." He went down two steps. Then he stopped again, without turning round. "Rei. The mark Gin got in July. The door with a hand on it. From the inside."

"Yes."

"He knew," said Itsuki. "In July. He knew something was going to get in. And he put a door on his heart to keep a bit of himself on the inside when it did." He started down the stairs again. "That's what that is. It's not a ward. It's a room. He made himself a room to hide in."

Rei stood on the stairs with his new wrist cold under the film and thought about that for a long time.

*Hands that make tea.* He did not know why the words came into his head. He did not know where they came from. They felt like a memory that was not his.

At the bottom of the stairs he stopped, and looked back up at the green door.

"Itsuki."

"What."

"I'm going to come back."

Itsuki turned round. He looked at Rei's face, and then at the wrapped wrist, and then at Rei's face again.

"Oh no," he said. "Oh no. You liked it."

"I didn't like it. It hurt." Rei thought about Gin in the black chair with his eyes half closed. About the walking notebook. About a door on a heart. "But he had a mark for everything he was afraid of. I want that too. I want to be able to look at my arm and see what I've decided."

"You're going to turn into him."

"Maybe," said Rei.

Upstairs, behind the green door, Uruma began to giggle.
