#title Ink
#part Book Two. The Families

The tattoo shop had no sign. It was on the second floor above a noodle shop in Nippori, up a staircase that smelled of pork broth, behind a door painted dark green.

Rei knew the door before he reached it. He knew the smell that came out when it opened. Antiseptic, and green tea, and something sharp and dark that he later learned was ink. He had been here before. Many times. He had been thirteen, and fifteen, and seventeen, sitting on a plastic stool in the corner with a schoolbook open on his knees, pretending to read.

[img scene_tattoo_stool.png | Gin in the tattoo chair, years ago]

The memory came back so suddenly that he had to stop in the doorway. Gin in the big black chair under the lamp, with his long hair falling over one shoulder and his left arm stretched out on the padded rest. A woman bent over the arm with the needle, humming. Gin's face very calm, eyes half closed, the way it was when he drank hōjicha. The buzz of the machine. The little cup of green tea on the side table going cold. And Rei on the stool in the corner, watching the needle, not reading a single word.

He had never asked what the marks were. He had thought they were just Gin's. The way a face is someone's.

"Well," said a voice. "Look who finally came in on his own feet."

[break Tomoe]

The woman who ran the shop was about fifty. She was small and brown and strong, with grey-streaked hair tied up in a knot on top of her head and both arms covered from wrist to shoulder in tattoos. Waves. Carp. Chrysanthemums. And, here and there among the flowers, small sharp shapes that were not flowers. Rei recognised some of them now. He had seen them in the dictionary at the back of the archive.

She had a gold ring in one ear and a pair of reading glasses pushed up into her hair.

"Tomoe," she said. "You don't remember my name. You were too busy pretending to do your homework." She looked at him over the top of the counter. "You've grown. You've got his jaw."

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone's right." She looked past him at Itsuki, who had come up the stairs behind him with a cardboard tube under his arm. "And this is the artist."

"Kuroda Itsuki," said Itsuki. He looked round the shop with huge eyes. At the walls covered in drawings. At the big black chair under the lamp. At the trays of needles and the little pots of ink. "This is amazing. This is the best room I've ever been in."

"Don't touch anything," said Tomoe. "Show me what you drew."

[break The latch]

They had argued about it for a week. What Rei should have.

The archive was very clear. A tattoo is not like a chalk ward or ink on paper. It never fades. It never switches off. Whatever it does, it does every second of every day for the rest of your life. Gin had written a whole page about it. *Choose like you are choosing a bone. You will not get another one.* The Division did not like tattoos. A tattooed practitioner made noise in their assessments. Their instruments could not read past the marks properly. That was one of the reasons Gin had so many.

"That's a reason to do it," Itsuki had said. "If they ever get you in a room and measure you, it'll come out all wrong."

But that was not why Rei wanted it.

He wanted a latch.

For eleven years he had closed a door in his chest every time the veil came close. He had learned from Sōun to close it slowly. He had learned from the building in Higashi-Jūjō what happened when he could not close it at all. He wanted something on his body that would hold the door when he was too tired to hold it himself. A real latch. Iron. Something that would not let go.

Itsuki unrolled the cardboard tube on Tomoe's counter.

It was a drawing in black ink on thick white paper. A latch. An old-fashioned iron door latch, the kind on the gates of old temples, a curved bar dropping into a hook. But Itsuki had drawn it so that the curve of the bar was also the curve of a crow's wing, and the hook was also a key, Gin's key, with the small dent in the ring. And woven round it all, so finely you had to look twice to see it, were the lines of the old ward glyph from the archive. The one for *closed*. The one Gin had marked in the margin with a single word. *Mine.*

Tomoe looked at it for a long time with her glasses on.

"You're good," she said to Itsuki at last.

"I know."

"No, you don't. You think you're good. You're actually good." She took her glasses off. "Where?"

Rei held out his left wrist. The inside. Where the skin is thin and you can see the blue of the veins.

"Where I'll see it," he said.

[break The needle]

It took three hours.

It hurt more than he expected and less than he feared. A sharp, hot, scratching hurt, like being drawn on very hard with a pin. On the bones of the wrist it was worse. Tomoe hummed while she worked, the same song she had hummed six years ago over Gin's arm, an old song from Okinawa about a boat. Itsuki sat on the plastic stool in the corner, where Rei used to sit, and drew. He drew Rei in the chair. He drew Tomoe's hands. He drew the lamp. He did not pretend to do anything else.

"He sat there," said Tomoe, without looking up, at about the second hour. "Your brother. Same chair. First time he was twenty-two. Just joined the Division. Wanted a ward on his shoulder. Very serious. Very quiet. Brought you along because there was nobody to leave you with. You were thirteen. You fell asleep on that stool over your maths and nearly fell off it."

"I don't remember that."

"You wouldn't. You were asleep." The needle buzzed. "After that he came twice a year. Sometimes three times. Always brought you. Always sat in that chair with his eyes half closed like it was a hot bath. The last time was in July." She wiped the wrist with a cloth that smelled sharply of alcohol. "He wanted a mark on his chest. Over the heart. I asked him what it was for. He wouldn't say. I'd never seen the shape before. He'd drawn it himself."

Rei turned his head. "What did it look like?"

Tomoe stopped the needle. She thought about it.

"Like a door," she said. "A door, closed, with a hand on it. From the inside." She started the needle again. "I said, Gin, that's not a ward. That's a prayer. And he said, Tomoe, it's the same thing, if you mean it enough." She was quiet for a while. "Then he paid, and he took you home, and that was the last time I saw him."

Rei did not say anything. He watched the ceiling. In the corner, Itsuki had stopped drawing.

[break What it did]

When it was finished, Tomoe wrapped the wrist in clear film and told him how to wash it and not to scratch it and not to go in a bath for two weeks, which upset him more than anything else she said.

Then she told him to close his eyes and open the door in his chest.

He did. He did it the way Sōun had taught him. A finger's width. The cold came in, the faint everyday cold that was always there, in every building in Tokyo, the background hum of the veil.

And on his wrist, under the film, the new mark went cold.

Not painful. A clear, sharp cold, like a drop of water from a tap. He felt it. And then he felt something else. A weight. Somewhere in his chest, behind the breastbone, where the door was. A weight settling into place. A small iron bar, dropping into a hook, with a click he did not hear but felt in his teeth.

The door was still open a finger's width. But now it could not open any further. Not unless he chose. Not unless he lifted the latch himself.

He opened his eyes. He was breathing hard.

"It works," he said.

"Of course it works," said Tomoe. "He drew it." She jerked her chin at Itsuki. "And you meant it. That's all a mark is." She started cleaning her needles. "It'll go cold whenever something's near. Every time. Forever. You'll get used to it. Your brother used to say his left arm was a better alarm than any instrument the Division ever owned."

"Does it have a cost?" said Rei. "The archive said they all have a cost."

Tomoe stopped cleaning. She looked at him over her glasses.

"You'll never be able to throw that door wide open in one go again," she said. "Not without lifting the latch first. It'll always take you one breath longer than it used to." She shrugged. "Most days that's a gift. One day, maybe, it'll be the breath you don't have." She went back to her needles. "Everything costs something. That's what costs are for."

[break The stairs]

Outside, on the stairs that smelled of pork broth, Itsuki stopped and took Rei's wrapped wrist in both hands and looked at it through the film. The latch. The crow's wing. The key with the dent in the ring.

"It's the best thing I ever drew," he said quietly. "And it's on you. Forever."

"Is that bad?"

"No." Itsuki let go of his wrist. "No, it's... no." He went down two steps. Then he stopped again, without turning round. "Rei. The mark Gin got in July. The door with a hand on it. From the inside."

"Yes."

"He knew," said Itsuki. "In July. He knew something was going to get in. And he put a door on his heart to keep a bit of himself on the inside when it did." He started down the stairs again. "That's what that is. It's not a ward. It's a room. He made himself a room to hide in."

Rei stood on the stairs with his new wrist cold under the film and thought about that for a long time.

*Hands that make tea.* He did not know why the words came into his head. He did not know where they came from. They felt like a memory that was not his.
