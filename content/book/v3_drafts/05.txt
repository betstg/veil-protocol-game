#title Refusal
#part Book One. The Archive

Okada walked fast and did not look back to see if he was following. He followed.

She took him out of the station by the south exit, away from the blue suits and the lanterns, across the road and up a narrow lane between a pharmacy and a shuttered shoe shop. Halfway up the lane there was a coffee shop so small that Rei had walked past it for years without seeing it. It had four tables, a glass case of sandwiches and a radio playing old enka songs very quietly. A woman of about seventy stood behind the counter. She looked at Okada and put the kettle on without being asked.

They sat at the table furthest from the window. The woman brought Okada a pot of strong green tea and brought Rei a glass of iced barley tea he had not ordered. Okada poured her tea. She took the three notebooks out of her pocket, took off the rubber band, and laid them on the table in a row. She opened the middle one and read something in it, slowly, for almost a minute. Then she closed it and looked at him.

"Ask," she said.

Rei had a hundred questions. The first one out of his mouth surprised him. "Did that man have a family?"

Okada was quiet for a moment. "Yes. A wife. Haruna. Two daughters at high school. He was an accountant at a printing company in Akabane. He was going to his wife's parents' house for Obon." She said it flatly, like reading a list. "His name was Ogawa. Ogawa Tetsuya."

"What was in him?"

"Something that wanted to talk to you." She drank her tea. "That's not what they usually want."

"What do they usually want?"

"Food. A body that works. A door into somewhere warm." She put the cup down. "Most of what comes through the veil is small and lost. A few things are not. The things that are not, we call demons. A demon is a spirit that decided to feed and grow instead of fading away. It feeds on one feeling. It finds people who are full of that feeling and it lives in them. Sometimes it lives in them quietly for years. Sometimes it uses them up in an afternoon." She looked at the window. "Mr. Ogawa was used up in an afternoon."

Rei looked at his barley tea. There were small drops of water running down the outside of the glass. He watched one run all the way to the table.

"It said I had room in me," he said. "It said, just like the other one."

"I heard you."

"The other one is Gin."

Okada did not answer. That was an answer.

"The woman on the phone said he's on assignment."

"I know what she said."

"Is he?"

Okada picked up her cup again and held it without drinking. Her hands were small and very clean. "I'm going to tell you what I'm allowed to tell you," she said. "And then I'm going to tell you one thing I'm not allowed to tell you. And after that you're going to listen to me, because I have been doing this job for eight years and you have been doing it for one evening."

"Okay."

"What I'm allowed to tell you. Officer Uedera Gin has not reported to his supervisor since Wednesday evening. That is not unusual during Obon for a field officer in his position. The Division is aware of his status. You will be informed of any change." She said it like a woman reading off a card. Then she put the cup down. "What I'm not allowed to tell you. Nobody in the building knows where he is. And the people who should be looking for him are not looking very hard."

Rei felt the cold coin of the key against his chest. "Why not?"

"I don't know yet." She closed the notebook. "That's the truth. I don't know yet. I've been watching the numbers in this district for four years. Something has been wrong in them for four years. Your brother knew. I think he went to look at it on Wednesday. I think whatever he found is the reason people in nice suits are making very sure nobody asks."

[break The offer]

"Your brother asked me to watch for you," Okada said. "Four years ago. He came to my desk with two cans of coffee and he said, I have a younger brother. He sees more than he knows. If anything happens to me, he'll do something stupid. Please be there when he does."

Rei did not trust his voice. He drank his barley tea instead.

"You're about to do something stupid," Okada said. "I can see it on your face."

"My left eye does a thing," Rei said before he could stop himself.

She frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. A friend says it."

She studied him for another second. Then she took a card out of her pocket and put it on the table between them. It was a plain white card with the white owl at the top. *Okada Shizuka. Investigation. Kita District.* A phone number. On the back she had written another number by hand, in very neat small figures.

"The front is my desk. The back is me." She tapped the card. "Here is what I want you to do. Tomorrow, you come to the Division office in Akabane. You ask for an assessment. It takes half a day. They measure what you can see and how much of it. Then you are on file. Then you are registered. A registered practitioner has rights. Training. Protection. A salary, if you're good enough. And most important, people know where you are." She leaned forward a little. "What happened on that platform tonight happens to unregistered people. Things find them and nobody notices until the train comes in."

Rei looked at the card.

He thought of the woman on the phone. *I'll make a note of that for you.* He thought of the mother on the platform, her face changing slowly while a kind voice talked to her, until she believed she had seen a man faint in the heat. He thought of the old man looking at his chrysanthemums, unable to remember why he was holding them.

"If I go in tomorrow," he said, "they'll measure me. And then they'll know everything about me."

"Yes."

"Like they know everything about Gin."

Okada did not move.

"They know where he is," Rei said. "Or they know something. And they sent people in nice suits to make a whole platform forget what it saw. In twenty minutes." He pushed the card back across the table, gently. "I'm not walking into that building and letting them write down what I am."

"That's stupid."

"Probably."

"Your brother would tell you to go in."

"My brother taught me to close a door in my chest every time I see something," Rei said, "and told me it was a trick for sleeping. For eleven years." His voice was shaking now and he let it. "I don't think my brother wanted them to write down what I am either."

Okada sat back. For a long moment she looked at him, and he could not tell what she was thinking. Then she picked up the card, turned it over to the side with her own number, and put it back in front of him. This time she put her hand flat on top of it.

"Keep it anyway," she said. "When you change your mind, call the back. Not the front. Never the front."

He took it. She stood, put her notebooks back in her pocket one by one, snapped the rubber band round them, and paid for both drinks with exact change. At the door she stopped.

"Rei."

"Yes?"

"The thing on the platform. It didn't come for Mr. Ogawa. Mr. Ogawa was just the closest coat it could put on." She did not turn round. "It came for you. Go home, lock the door, and don't open it for anybody who knocks."

Then she was gone, and the bell over the door rang once behind her.

[break The ones worth knowing]

He did not sleep that night either. He sat at the kitchen table with every light in the flat switched on and read the archive.

Somewhere around three in the morning he found the pages at the back.

They were different from the rest. Not typed, not neat. They were torn from a small paper diary and glued in, and they were in Gin's handwriting, but looser, faster, as if he had written them for himself and never meant anyone else to read them. At the top of the first page Gin had written a heading and underlined it twice.

*The Ones Worth Knowing.*

Each page was one person. A name. A few lines about them. A phone number or a place. Rei read them all, slowly, the way you read the names on graves.

There was a woman called Nakamura Aoi with a ceramic bead bracelet. *She asks how I am doing and actually waits for the answer, which I did not ask for but have started depending on.* There was a man called Kato Ryusei who carried a white king from a chess set in his pocket. *Has said approximately four genuine things to me in two years and they were all correct.* There was a man called Tsukishiro Haruki who made the best coffee in the city. There was a bakery in Jūjō. There was a café in Shinjuku where a woman called Matsuda Keiko had Gin's order ready before he sat down.

And there was a man called Tanizaki Seiichi.

[doc The Ones Worth Knowing. Tanizaki Seiichi]
Retired from active work in the technical sense. Has been doing active work since retiring, in the same way I have been doing active work while pretending to be Channel rank. The tea ritual means something... drink it. Does not explain anything until the third cup and I genuinely cannot tell if this is strategic or if he just likes tea that much. Has seen everything. Will help with almost anything if you ask without performing urgency.
[/doc]

At the bottom of the last page Gin had written one more line.

*If I am not here and you are reading this: the people above are the ones I trust. In that order, approximately.*

Rei turned back to Tanizaki. There was no phone number on his page. There was a small hand-drawn map instead. A lane off the old shopping street below Ōji, past a shrine with two stone foxes, to a house with a garden. Gin had drawn the garden as a wild scribble of pencil and written beside it, *Gone to seed on purpose. Do not offer to weed it.*

It was fifteen minutes' walk from the flat.

He went at nine in the morning, because some instinct told him an old man would be up early and would think less of anyone who came late.

The house was old and wooden and small, with a tiled roof that dipped in the middle like the back of a tired horse. The garden in front of it was a wild green mess. Tall grass, weeds with yellow flowers, a fig tree that had grown through the fence, a stone lantern half buried in ivy. There was no bell. Rei knocked on the sliding door.

A voice inside said, "It's open."

The man who sat in the small front room was perhaps sixty. He was thin and brown from the sun, with short grey hair and a face that had settled into calm lines a long time ago. He wore a faded blue cotton jinbei, the loose summer clothes old men wear at home. He was kneeling at a low table. On the table was a tray with a teapot and four cups. Four, not one. He looked up at Rei without surprise, as if he had been expecting someone, and not specially him.

"Sit," he said.

Rei sat. He could not hold it in. "Mr. Tanizaki, my name is Uedera Rei. My brother is Uedera Gin. He's missing. He's been gone since Wednesday night, and the CVD is lying about it, and yesterday something possessed a man on Ōji station and it talked to me about Gin, and the man is dead, and I need you to help me find..."

Tanizaki poured tea into one cup. He pushed it across the table. "Drink," he said.

Rei drank. It was green tea, very hot, and bitter at the back of the tongue.

"Mr. Tanizaki, please, I don't have time to..."

Tanizaki poured a second cup. He pushed it across. Rei looked at it. He drank it, faster this time, and burned his mouth.

"Please," he said. "Gin wrote that you'd help. He wrote that you'd help with almost anything if..."

He stopped.

*If you ask without performing urgency.*

Tanizaki was looking at him with mild, patient interest, the way you watch a cat that has got its head stuck in a paper bag.

Rei put his cup down. He felt his face go hot. He was suddenly very aware of how he must look. A boy with red eyes and his shoes on the wrong way round, talking too fast, waving his hands, saying *please* every five words. Performing. Exactly what Gin had warned him not to do.

Tanizaki did not pour a third cup.

Instead he stood up, slowly, with one hand on the table, and slid open the door to the garden. The smell of grass and hot earth came in.

"Come back," he said, "when you have finished being in a hurry."

"My brother is..."

"Your brother has been in a hurry since he was nine years old," said Tanizaki. "It is the only thing about him I have never been able to fix." He stood aside from the door. "Go home. Sleep. Eat something. Come back when you can sit in this room for an hour without asking me for anything. Then we will talk about your brother."

Rei stood. His legs felt stiff. At the door he turned to argue, and Tanizaki was already kneeling at the table again, pouring tea into the third cup. He drank it himself, slowly, with his eyes half closed, looking out at his ruined garden. He did not look up again.

Rei walked home through the old shopping street with the heat on his neck and the key cold on his chest, and he was so angry that he could not see straight. He was angry at Tanizaki. He was angry at Okada and at the woman on the phone. He was angry at Gin most of all, for leaving, for lying, for teaching him a breathing trick and calling it a trick for sleep.

Under the anger, very small, there was another feeling. He did not have a name for it yet. It was the feeling of having been seen clearly by someone, all the way through, and sent away for his own good.

He did not like it at all.
