#title The Message
#part Book Two. The Families

The building was on Switch's list of seventeen. Number eleven. A five-storey block of flats in Higashi-Jūjō, empty since the spring, waiting to be knocked down. *Monitor only*, the Division had written. *Low activity.*

Two Grid people had gone in on Wednesday to check it. A woman who did wards and a man who read rooms. They had come out after forty minutes, white-faced, and refused to go back. They said the stairs went wrong. They said that on the fourth floor the corridor kept going after it should have stopped. They said they heard a child crying behind a door, and when they opened the door, there was the same corridor again, and the crying was behind the next door. Switch offered the job to anyone at forty thousand yen. Nobody took it.

Rei took it.

He did not tell Haruki. Haruki would have said no, or come with him, and Rei did not want either. He told himself it was because of the money, and some of it was. The rent for December would come round soon, and Gin's watch was in a pawn shop in Ueno, and the forty thousand would buy it back.

But some of it was the white room. Every night since Kabukichō he had closed his eyes and seen the teacher's empty smile. He had needed to do something with his hands. Something he could finish.

[break The stairs]

[need ch19_flats | An empty five-storey Tokyo apartment block at night waiting for demolition, outside walkways, broken windows, a single young man with a torch and a backpack climbing the outside stairs, a crow on the railing]

He went in on Saturday night at nine, after the bath. Gorō came with him as far as the outside stairs, walking up them one step at a time like a fat old man, and then stopped on the third-floor landing and would not go further. He fluffed his feathers and made the low kettle noise in his throat.

"I know," said Rei. "I'll be quick."

Gorō did not look as if he believed him.

The fourth floor was a long concrete walkway with doors along one side and a railing on the other, exactly like the building Rei lived in. The railing looked out over the street. He could see the lights of a konbini on the corner, and a man walking a dog. Ordinary. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

It was cold. Not the wet cold of the platform. A thin, sad, grey cold, like a room where the heating has been off for years. And under it, very faint, a sound. A child crying. Small, tired, hopeless crying, the kind a child does when it has been crying for a long time and does not expect anyone to come.

It came from behind the fourth door.

He did everything the archive said. He laid a salt line across the walkway behind him, so he had a way back. He wrote a ward in chalk on the concrete by his feet. He held the key on its cord in his left hand. Then he opened the fourth door.

There was a corridor behind it.

Not a flat. A corridor. The same concrete walkway, with the same doors along one side. But no railing on the other side. A wall. A blank grey wall where the street should have been. And the crying was coming from behind the fourth door along.

Rei turned round.

The door he had come through was there. He opened it. Behind it was the same corridor again. Doors. A blank grey wall. Crying behind the fourth door. His salt line was gone. His chalk ward was gone.

He had been in the building for nine minutes.

[break The corridor]

He tried for an hour.

He opened doors. Every one opened onto the same corridor. He walked to the end of the corridor and there was a staircase, going down, and he went down it, and at the bottom there was the corridor again, with the same doors and the same wall and the same crying. He went up. The same. He counted the doors. Twelve. He opened all twelve. Twelve times, the same corridor.

He opened the door in his chest wider, to read the place, and that was a mistake. The building opened up around him like a wound. He felt what it was. Not a demon. Not one spirit. Something worse and sadder than either. The archive called it a *suffering being*. Many small griefs, from many years, from many people who had lived in these flats and been unhappy in them, all poured into one place and stuck together like old chewing gum, until they made something that had a shape and a hunger. It did not want to kill him. It did not want anything. It just did not want to be alone. And it had folded the building round him, the way a child holds a toy, so he could not leave.

The crying went on and on behind the fourth door. He did not open it again.

By the end of the hour his legs were shaking. The tunnel had started at the edges of his eyes. He could feel himself running out, the way you feel a phone battery dying, the screen getting dimmer. Every door he opened cost him a little. Every minute he held the door in his chest open cost him more. He sat down on the concrete with his back against the blank grey wall and made himself breathe.

He took out his phone. One bar of signal. Then none. Then one.

He did not call Haruki. He did not call Okada. He knew what he needed, and it was not either of them. He needed a door. A real door. A door that went somewhere else.

He typed with his thumb, very slowly, because his hands would not keep still.

*itsuki. I'm stuck. higashi-jujo, the empty flats behind the shrine, 4th floor. the doors all go to the same place. I need a door that goes somewhere else.*

He looked at it. He added one more line.

*I'm sorry I shoved you.*

He pressed send. The bar of signal flickered. The message said *Sending*. It said *Sending* for a long time.

Then it said *Delivered*.

Rei closed his eyes. He was so tired. The crying behind the fourth door had gone softer. It sounded almost like a lullaby now. It would be so easy to stay. To sit here against the wall and let the building hold him. It was not trying to hurt him. It only wanted company.

His phone buzzed in his hand.

*Read.*

And then, a second later, in lowercase:

*you absolute fucking idiot. stay there. don't open any more doors. I'm coming.*

[break Itsuki]

It took Itsuki twenty-six minutes. Rei found out later that he had run from the Kuroda house in Nezu to Nishi-Nippori station, in slippers, and taken the Keihin-Tōhoku north, and run from Higashi-Jūjō station to the building, in slippers, with Mrs. Fujii's voice following him down the lane asking if the young master needed a coat.

Rei did not know any of that. He knew only that he was sitting against the blank wall with his eyes closing, and the crying had become a lullaby, and then his phone buzzed again.

*I'm outside. I can see the 4th floor. I can see you. you're sitting on the walkway with your back against the railing. you look like shit.*

Rei opened his eyes. He was sitting against a grey wall. There was no railing.

*I can't see you,* he typed. *there's a wall.*

*there's no wall. there's a railing. you're looking right at me and you can't see me.* A pause. *ok. ok. this is the thing. this is the thing my father does. ok.*

A long pause. Rei watched the screen. The battery was at four percent.

*I'm going to try something,* Itsuki wrote. *don't laugh.*

*I won't laugh.*

*you'll laugh. I've never done it. I've never done it all the way. if it goes wrong I'm going to be really embarrassed in front of you.*

*itsuki I'm going to die in a corridor. I promise I won't laugh.*

*don't say die. not this week.* A pause. *put your hand on the door. the fourth one. the crying one. and don't open it until I say.*

Rei got up. His legs nearly gave way. He walked to the fourth door and put his palm flat on it. It was cold and slightly damp, like the side of a fish tank. Behind it the lullaby went on.

He waited.

Outside, far away, on the street below a railing he could not see, Kuroda Itsuki stood in his slippers in front of the concrete entrance of an empty building, with his hand flat on the cold metal frame of the front door, and closed his eyes.

Rei felt it.

Through the door under his palm. Through the walls and the floor and the folded concrete. Something opening. Not slowly, the way Sōun opened the black frame in his room. Badly. Clumsily. Like a boy shouldering open a door that has been painted shut for twenty years, in one desperate shove. A wrench. A crack. And then air, real air, cold November air, coming from somewhere that was not this corridor. Smelling of river.

*now,* said the phone.

Rei opened the door.

[break The bridge]

He fell through it onto stone.

Cold stone, wet with dew. Above him, a dark sky. Beside him, a low iron railing painted green. Under the railing, a long way down, a black river moving slowly, with the lights of the city broken up on it. He was lying on a bridge. A pedestrian bridge, narrow, old, with green iron rails and old-fashioned lamps every ten metres. He knew it. He had never stood on it in his life. But he had seen it a hundred times. In oil paint, on a tall canvas, in a studio on the third floor of an old building in Ueno.

Itsuki's bridge.

Itsuki was on his knees beside him in the middle of the bridge in a pair of grey house slippers and a thin T-shirt, shaking from head to foot. His nose was bleeding. His eyes were enormous.

"It's real," he said. His voice was high and strange. "It's real. It's a real bridge. I thought I made it up. I've been painting it for two years and I thought I made it up and it's real. It's the Shirahige bridge. No. It's the little one next to it. The old footbridge. I've walked over it. I must have walked over it when I was a kid. I must have..."

"Itsuki."

"I opened it. Rei. I opened a door. I opened a door from Higashi-Jūjō to the Sumida river. That's four kilometres. That's four kilometres, that's a real door, I opened a real..."

"Itsuki."

Itsuki stopped. He looked at Rei. He was crying. He had not noticed.

"You're wearing slippers," said Rei.

Itsuki looked down at his feet. Then he looked back at Rei. And he laughed. It came out of him all at once, a huge, helpless, hiccupping laugh, and he could not stop it, and it set Rei off, and the two of them sat in the middle of the old footbridge over the Sumida at eleven at night, Itsuki in his slippers with blood on his lip and Rei with salt on his knees and four percent battery, and laughed until they could not breathe.

"You promised you wouldn't laugh," Itsuki gasped.

"You said I would."

"I hate that you listen to me."

When the laughter ran out, they sat with their backs against the green iron railing, side by side, looking at the river.

"I'm sorry I shoved you," said Rei.

"You said that in the message."

"I'm saying it with my mouth."

"Okay." Itsuki wiped his nose with the back of his hand and looked at the blood. "I'm sorry I said Gin was gone." He did not look at Rei. "I didn't mean it. I've never meant anything less in my life. I said it because I was scared and it was the worst thing I could think of."

"I know."

"Don't *I know* me. Let me be sorry."

"Okay. Be sorry."

"I'm being sorry." Itsuki pulled his knees up. "And you're still an idiot. You went into a folded building on your own. On your *own*, Rei. With chalk. You didn't tell Haruki. You didn't tell anyone."

"I told you."

"After! After you were stuck!" Itsuki turned and glared at him. "If you'd died in there I'd have had to explain to Gin, when we find him, that his brother died because he was too proud to send a text. And Gin would have killed me. And then I'd be dead too. And it would be your fault."

"That's a lot of people dead."

"It's a tragedy. It's a whole tragedy. I'd paint it." Itsuki put his head back against the railing. "Don't do it again."

"I won't."

"You will. But tell me first. Every time. Promise."

"I promise."

They sat for a while. A late train went over a bridge further down the river, a line of yellow windows, very small and far away.

"Rei," said Itsuki.

"Yeah."

"My father's going to see this on my face tomorrow. He's going to know I did it. And I didn't do it properly in front of him like I wanted. I did it in my slippers for an idiot in an empty building." He was smiling. "I'm so glad. I'm so glad I didn't do it for him."

Rei did not say anything. He reached over and put his hand on top of Itsuki's, on the cold stone of the bridge, and left it there.

Itsuki looked at their hands. Then he looked at the river.

"Tomorrow," he said, after a while, "you're taking me to your stupid Grid."
