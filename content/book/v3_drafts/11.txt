#title Rats
#part Book One. The Archive

The file came across Rei's desk at the police station on a Tuesday in the middle of September, in a pile of things nobody wanted.

It was a report from the week before. A woman of seventy-eight found dead in her flat in Akabane, near the Arakawa river. She lived alone. A neighbour noticed the smell. The doctor said heart failure, made worse by the heat. Case closed. It was the kind of report that came in every week in summer, and the detective who gave it to Rei to file did not even look at it.

But stapled to the back was a second page, a note from the uniformed officer who had found her. It said there had been dead rats in the flat. Eleven of them. Lying in a line from the gap under the front door to the foot of her futon, evenly spaced, each one about a metre from the next, as if someone had laid them out with a ruler.

Under that, in pen, the officer had written, *Neighbours report many dead rats along embankment road this month. Health department informed.*

Rei read the second page three times. Then he went to the filing room and looked for other reports from the river wards in the last month. He found two. A man of eighty-one in Kawaguchi, just across the river. A woman of sixty-six in Shimura. Both alone. Both heart failure. In both, somebody had mentioned rats.

He took photos of all three with his phone and put the files away where they belonged. Nobody saw him do it. Nobody would have cared.

[break The embankment]

[need ch11_arakawa_night | The Arakawa river embankment at night in Kita, a long raised grass bank with a straight footpath on top, the dark wide river, distant lit bridge, a line of small dead rats evenly spaced along the path under a street lamp, two young men with torches]

The Arakawa is the big river along the northern edge of Kita. It is wide and grey and slow, and on both sides of it there are high grass banks that keep the city from flooding when the typhoons come. Along the top of the bank on the Tokyo side runs a long straight footpath. In the day it is full of joggers and cyclists and old men with fishing rods and families flying kites. At night it is dark and empty, with the lights of the bridges far away on either side and the black water moving below.

Rei and Itsuki walked the bank at eleven at night with two torches and a backpack full of salt and iron.

"So we're looking for dead rats," said Itsuki.

"Yes."

"On a riverbank."

"Yes."

"At night."

"Yes."

"This is the worst date you've ever taken me on."

"It's not a date."

"Clearly. I'd have worn better shoes." Itsuki swung his torch along the grass. "Gin used to tell me rats were the second smartest animal in Tokyo. After crows. Before people."

"He told me that too."

They found the first one twenty minutes upriver from Akabane.

It lay in the middle of the footpath, on its side, with its legs stretched out stiffly in front and behind, as if it had died running. It was a big brown sewer rat, fat and glossy. There was no mark on it. No blood. Its eyes were open, and they were white. Not pink, not black. White all the way across, like boiled eggs.

A metre further on there was another. And a metre after that, another. Rei swung his torch along the path. In the yellow circle of light the rats lay one after another, perfectly spaced, heads all pointing the same way, inland, toward the houses. A line of small dead bodies leading off the bank and down a flight of concrete steps into the streets.

"Okay," said Itsuki very quietly. "Okay, I don't like that."

Rei opened the door in his chest a finger's width, slowly, the way Sōun had taught him.

The line of rats changed. Each little body had a faint stain over it in the air, like the smudge a thumb leaves on glass. And the stains were all the same colour, a dull, sick, grey-yellow, and they pulled at something in Rei's stomach. It was a feeling like hunger. Not hunger for food. A wider hunger. A wanting that had no bottom to it. Wanting someone to call. Wanting to be touched. Wanting the phone to ring. Wanting anything at all, so much that it hurt.

"It's using them," Rei said. "Something is using the rats. Like legs. It walks through them, and when it's finished with one, it drops it and takes the next." He followed the line with his torch, down the steps. "It's going somewhere."

"Where?"

"Somewhere lonely."

[break Tachibana Sumiko]

The line of rats went down the steps, across a road, along the side of a closed petrol station and into a street of old two-storey wooden apartment buildings with outside staircases and laundry hanging on the balconies. In front of the third building, the line turned and went up the outside stairs.

At the top of the stairs, outside the second door, the last rat lay on the doormat with its white eyes open.

The light inside was on. There was a name card in the slot by the door. *Tachibana.*

The door was not quite closed.

Rei felt it before he touched the handle. The cold. The same wet cold as the platform at Ōji, but thinner and closer and somehow hungrier, like the air in front of an open freezer in a dark room. The pressure behind his eyes hit hard. He did the breathing. In for four. Hold. Out for six. He opened the door in his chest a crack and kept it there, with his whole body, like holding a door against a wind.

He pushed the door open.

The flat was one room and a tiny kitchen, very clean and very full. Every shelf was covered with small things. China cats. Postcards. Plastic flowers. Photographs of a young man in a graduation gown, a young man at a wedding, a small child at a beach. The television was on with the sound off, showing a quiz show. On the low table there were six empty bowls from a takeout ramen shop, stacked neatly, each with a paper receipt tucked under it.

A woman sat on the floor in the middle of the room with her back to them.

She was about sixty. She wore a pink cotton nightdress. Her grey hair was in curlers. She was sitting with her legs folded under her, very straight, facing the wall. And she was eating.

Rei saw her right hand lift to her mouth. Lower. Lift again. There was a sound in the room, small and wet and constant. Chewing.

"Mrs. Tachibana?" said Rei.

She stopped.

Her head turned. Not her shoulders. Not her body. Only her head, slowly, round and round on her neck, much further than a neck should turn, until her face was looking at them over her own shoulder blade and her body was still facing the wall. Rei heard something in her neck make a small, dry, grinding sound, like a wooden spoon in a mortar.

Her face was smeared with something brown and grey. Her mouth was full. Her cheeks were stuffed so full that they bulged. She was still chewing, slowly, her jaw going round and round. Behind Rei, Itsuki made a sound and put his hand over his mouth.

She had been eating everything in the room. Paper. A postcard was half in her hand, torn, chewed at the corner. Cotton wool. Dry rice from a bag, scattered across the mat. The plastic petals of a plastic flower. There was blood on her lips where something sharp had cut them.

Her eyes were not white like the rats. They were black. The pupils had spread so wide that her eyes looked like two holes, and they took in the whole room at once. Rei felt them take him in. His face, his hands, the backpack, the iron, Itsuki behind him. All of it, in one second, with a hunger so strong he could feel it on his skin. No old woman had ever looked at anything like that. Nobody human had.

"Hungry," she said.

The voice was hers. That was the worst thing. It was not deep, not strange. It was a small, polite, ordinary voice, the voice of a woman who said good morning to her neighbours and thank you to the delivery man. But it came out of her full mouth with the food still in it, so it was thick and wet, and it did not stop. It went on and on, very fast, all on one note, like a radio left on between stations.

"Hungry hungry so hungry nobody calls nobody comes my son lives in Osaka he says next month next month the ramen boy comes on Thursdays he says thank you auntie he says it every time so hungry so hungry..."

[break What Rei did]

He did not think. He swung the backpack off his shoulder and tore open the jar of iron filings and threw them.

The archive said that iron disrupts things wearing a human body. It said it burns them. The filings went across the room in a black spray and hit Mrs. Tachibana on the side of the face and the shoulder.

She screamed.

Not with her own voice. Something screamed out of her mouth, high and thin and furious, like a kettle and a cat at the same time. Her head snapped round the right way with a crack. Her whole body jerked, as if she had touched a live wire. And from under her nightdress, from the sleeves and the collar and the hem, rats came pouring out.

Dozens of them. Brown and grey and fast, a river of small bodies with white eyes. They ran in every direction, over the mat, up the walls, over Rei's feet. Itsuki shouted and jumped backward and fell into the kitchen doorway. Rei felt claws on his ankles and a weight running up his trouser leg and he grabbed the salt bag and poured it in a circle round his own feet, blindly, the way the archive said, and the rats that touched the salt line squealed and turned back as if they had hit glass.

"Rei!" Itsuki was on the floor in the kitchen with rats running over his legs. "Rei, Rei, they're on me, oh God, they're..."

"Iron!" Rei threw him the second jar. "Throw it! Not at them! At the floor round you! A line!"

Itsuki caught the jar one-handed, which surprised them both, and tore the lid off with his teeth and poured. The rats on his legs leapt off as if he had burned them. One of them did not leap fast enough. It touched the iron, and it shrieked and went stiff, and fell over on its side with its legs straight out in front and behind. When Rei looked at it, its eyes were not white any more. They were small and black and ordinary. Just a dead rat.

The rest went out the open door in a single brown wave, down the outside stairs, and were gone.

The room was silent except for the television, still showing its quiz show with the sound off.

Mrs. Tachibana was lying on her side on the mat.

[break The last thing]

Rei knelt beside her. Her eyes were brown now. The black had gone back to two small ordinary dots. Ordinary brown eyes, wet and frightened. Her mouth was still full. She was trying to breathe and she could not. Her chest jerked. Her hands moved weakly at her throat.

"Itsuki, call an ambulance. Call now."

Itsuki was already dialling. His hands were shaking so badly he dropped the phone twice.

Rei turned her on her side and tried to clear her mouth with his fingers. Paper and rice and plastic and cotton wool. Some of it came. Most of it was too far down. She was looking at him the whole time. Her hand found his wrist and held it, very hard for a woman so small.

He did not know what else to do, so he opened the door in his chest all the way, and he looked at her.

He saw her. Not her past, like the cup in Lilac. Her now. A woman alone in a room full of small things. A son in Osaka who called on her birthday and on New Year. A husband who had died nine years ago. Nobody who touched her, ever, except the doctor once a year and the boy who brought the ramen on Thursdays and said *thank you, auntie*, and she ordered ramen every Thursday even when she was not hungry, just to hear him say it. That was what the thing had found in her. Not hunger for food. Hunger for one warm word. It had found it and climbed into it and eaten her from inside, using her own wanting to do it.

Her grip on his wrist went loose.

"I'm here," Rei said. He did not know if she could hear. "I'm here. Somebody's here."

Her eyes looked at him. Then they looked past him, at nothing. Then they did not look at anything.

The ambulance came in eleven minutes. By then she had been dead for eight.

[break The back of the card]

They sat on the bottom step of the outside staircase while the ambulance men went up. Rei's hands were covered in things he did not want to look at. Itsuki sat next to him with his arms round his knees, shaking. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Rei took out his wallet and found the white card with the owl on it. He turned it over to the side with the small neat handwriting.

*Call the back. Not the front. Never the front.*

He called the back.

It rang twice. "Okada."

"It's Rei," he said. "Uedera Rei. I'm in Akabane. There's a woman. She's dead. There were rats. It was in her. I couldn't..." His voice broke. He made it work again. "There are two others. I have the files. It's going along the river. It'll do it again."

There was a silence on the line. Then he heard, faintly, the sound of keys being picked up, and a door opening.

"Give me the address," said Okada. "Don't touch anything else. Don't talk to anyone in a nice suit. I'm coming."

She came in twenty minutes, in a taxi, in the same plain suit with shiny elbows. She looked at the stairs. She looked at the dead rat on the doormat, and at the iron filings scattered across the walkway, and at Rei's hands. She did not ask a single question. She took a packet of wet wipes out of her bag and gave it to him, and then she took out her phone and made a call, and Rei heard her say, in a flat bored voice, "Okada, Investigation. Natural death, Akabane, one occupant, elderly. I'm on scene. No veil signature. No need for Public Confidence. I'll file in the morning."

She hung up. Rei stared at her.

"You lied," he said.

"Yes." She sat down on the step on his other side, between him and the street, and took out her three notebooks and opened the middle one. "If I report what happened here, men in blue suits will be here in twenty minutes, and they will ask who threw the iron, and then they will want to know who you are. And then they'll know." She clicked her pen. "Now. Show me the files. All three. And tell me about the rats from the beginning, slowly, and don't leave anything out."

Itsuki laughed, suddenly, a high shaky laugh that was half a sob.

"Sorry," he said, wiping his face with his sleeve. "Sorry. It's just. I've been terrified for an hour and she's the scariest thing I've seen all night."

Okada did not look up from her notebook.

"Thank you," she said.
