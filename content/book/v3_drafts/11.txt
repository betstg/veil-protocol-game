#title Rats
#part Book One. The Archive

The file came across Rei's desk at the police station on a Tuesday in the middle of September, in a pile of things nobody wanted.

It was a report from the week before. A woman of seventy-eight found dead in her flat in Akabane, near the Arakawa river. She lived alone. A neighbour noticed the smell. The doctor said heart failure, made worse by the heat. Case closed. It was the kind of report that came in every week in summer, and the detective who gave it to Rei to file did not even look at it.

But stapled to the back was a second page, a note from the uniformed officer who had found her. The neighbours, it said, had complained about rats for a week before she died. Not rats running about. Rats sitting. A dozen of them, every evening, on the outside stairs and the walkway in front of her door, sitting very still on their back legs, all facing the door. When the neighbours chased them they went, all together, and came back the next evening and sat in the same places, facing the same way.

Under that, in pen, the officer had written, *Neighbours report many rats along embankment road this month. Behaving oddly. Health department informed.*

Rei read the second page three times. Then he went to the filing room and looked for other reports from the river wards in the last month. He found two. A man of eighty-one in Kawaguchi, just across the river. A woman of sixty-six in Shimura. Both alone. Both heart failure. In both, somebody had mentioned rats, sitting still, looking at the door.

He took photos of all three with his phone and put the files away where they belonged. Nobody saw him do it. Nobody would have cared.

That night he found the page in the archive. He had read it weeks before and not understood it.

[doc Personal Archive. Borrowed Senses]
A Class B or higher entity can divide a sliver of itself into ordinary neighbourhood animals and ride their senses. Rats most often, because nobody looks at rats. One entity, a hundred small eyes and ears distributed across a city. The tell is not the rats themselves but the pattern. Rats that move with too much purpose, that hold still together, that orient toward the same thing at the same time. A cluster of twelve rats all looking at the same corner means something is wearing them. A salt line does not stop borrowed senses. The rats are not the entity. They are its eyes.
[/doc]

*They are its eyes.* Something was walking the river with a hundred small eyes, looking at doors. Looking for the people behind them who were alone.

[break The embankment]

[need ch11_arakawa_night | The Arakawa river embankment at night in Kita, a long raised grass bank with a straight footpath on top, the dark wide river, a distant lit bridge, a line of live rats sitting perfectly still on their back legs along the path all facing the same way inland, two young men with torches stopped in front of them]

The Arakawa is the big river along the northern edge of Kita. It is wide and grey and slow, and on both sides of it there are high grass banks that keep the city from flooding when the typhoons come. Along the top of the bank on the Tokyo side runs a long straight footpath. In the day it is full of joggers and cyclists and old men with fishing rods and families flying kites. At night it is dark and empty, with the lights of the bridges far away on either side and the black water moving below.

Rei and Itsuki walked the bank at eleven at night with two torches and a backpack full of salt and iron.

"So we're looking for rats," said Itsuki.

"Yes."

"On a riverbank."

"Yes."

"At night."

"Yes."

"This is the worst date you've ever taken me on."

"It's not a date."

"Clearly. I'd have worn better shoes." Itsuki swung his torch along the grass. "Gin used to tell me rats were the second smartest animal in Tokyo. After crows. Before people."

"He told me that too."

They found the first one twenty minutes upriver from Akabane.

It was sitting in the middle of the footpath. A big brown sewer rat, fat and glossy, up on its back legs with its front paws held against its chest. It did not run when the torch hit it. It did not move at all. It was facing away from the river, inland, toward the houses, perfectly still, like a small brown statue.

A metre further on there was another. Sitting the same way. Facing the same way. And a metre after that, another. Rei swung his torch along the path. In the yellow circle of light the rats sat one after another, perfectly spaced, all on their back legs, all facing inland, all perfectly still. A line of small watchers leading off the bank and down a flight of concrete steps into the streets.

"Okay," said Itsuki very quietly. "Okay, I don't like that."

Rei opened the door in his chest a finger's width, slowly, the way Sōun had taught him.

And the rats were not many rats. That was the strange thing. With the door open, he did not feel thirty small animals. He felt one thing. One presence, stretched thin along the path like a thread through beads, looking out through thirty pairs of small black eyes at once. It was grey-yellow and sick, and it pulled at something in his stomach. It was a feeling like hunger. Not hunger for food. A wider hunger. A wanting that had no bottom to it. Wanting someone to call. Wanting to be touched. Wanting the phone to ring.

"It's not in them," Rei said. "It's looking through them. They're its eyes." He followed the line with his torch, down the steps. "It's watching for someone. Someone alone."

The nearest rat turned its head and looked at him.

Then the next. Then the next. All down the line, one after another, thirty small heads turning on thirty small necks, slowly, together, until every rat on the path was looking straight at Rei.

"*Oh,*" said Itsuki. "*Oh, I really don't like that.*"

The rats held still for one long second, looking. Then all of them dropped onto four feet at the same moment and ran. Not away. Down the steps. Fast. All together, in one brown stream, toward the houses.

"It's seen us," said Rei. "It's going to hurry."

He ran after them.

[break Tachibana Sumiko]

The rats went down the steps, across a road, along the side of a closed petrol station and into a street of old two-storey wooden apartment buildings with outside staircases and laundry hanging on the balconies. In front of the third building, the stream of rats went up the outside stairs and stopped. They sat down again, on every step and along the walkway at the top, on their back legs, still. All facing the second door.

The light inside was on. There was a name card in the slot by the door. *Tachibana.*

The door was not quite closed.

Rei felt it before he touched the handle. The cold. The same wet cold as the platform at Ōji, but thinner and closer and hungrier, like the air in front of an open freezer in a dark room. The pressure behind his eyes hit hard. He did the breathing. In for four. Hold. Out for six. He opened the door in his chest a crack and kept it there, with his whole body, like holding a door against a wind.

He pushed the door open.

The flat was one room and a tiny kitchen, very clean and very full. Every shelf was covered with small things. China cats. Postcards. Plastic flowers. Photographs of a young man in a graduation gown, a young man at a wedding, a small child at a beach. The television was on with the sound off, showing a quiz show. On the low table there were six empty bowls from a takeout ramen shop, stacked neatly, each with a paper receipt tucked under it.

Along the top of the wardrobe, along the windowsill, along the back of the sofa, rats sat in a row. Still. Watching.

A woman sat on the floor in the middle of the room with her back to them.

She was about sixty. She wore a pink cotton nightdress. Her grey hair was in curlers. She was sitting with her legs folded under her, very straight, facing the wall. And she was eating.

Rei saw her right hand lift to her mouth. Lower. Lift again. There was a sound in the room, small and wet and constant. Chewing.

"Mrs. Tachibana?" said Rei.

She stopped.

Her head turned. Not her shoulders. Not her body. Only her head, slowly, round and round on her neck, much further than a neck should turn, until her face was looking at them over her own shoulder blade and her body was still facing the wall. Rei heard something in her neck make a small, dry, grinding sound, like a wooden spoon in a mortar.

Her face was smeared with something brown and grey. Her mouth was full. Her cheeks were stuffed so full that they bulged. She was still chewing, slowly, her jaw going round and round. Behind Rei, Itsuki made a sound and put his hand over his mouth.

She had been eating everything in the room. Paper. A postcard was half in her hand, torn, chewed at the corner. Cotton wool. Dry rice from a bag, scattered across the mat. The plastic petals of a plastic flower. There was blood on her lips where something sharp had cut them.

Her eyes were black. The pupils had spread so wide that her eyes looked like two holes, and they took in the whole room at once. Rei felt them take him in. His face, his hands, the backpack, the iron, Itsuki behind him. All of it, in one second, with a hunger so strong he could feel it on his skin. No old woman had ever looked at anything like that. Nobody human had.

"Hungry," she said.

The voice was hers. That was the worst thing. It was not deep, not strange. It was a small, polite, ordinary voice, the voice of a woman who said good morning to her neighbours and thank you to the delivery man. But it came out of her full mouth with the food still in it, so it was thick and wet, and it did not stop. It went on and on, very fast, all on one note, like a radio left on between stations.

"Hungry hungry so hungry nobody calls nobody comes my son lives in Osaka he says next month next month the ramen boy comes on Thursdays he says thank you auntie he says it every time so hungry so hungry..."

[break What Rei did]

Rei tore open the salt and threw a line of it across the floor between the woman and the door. On the wardrobe and the windowsill and the sofa, the rats did not move. They did not care about salt. They were only eyes. They watched him do it.

So he did not think. He tore open the jar of iron filings and threw them at the woman.

The archive said that iron disrupts things wearing a human body. It said it burns them. The filings went across the room in a black spray and hit Mrs. Tachibana on the side of the face and the shoulder.

She screamed.

Not with her own voice. Something screamed out of her mouth, high and thin and furious, like a kettle and a cat at the same time. Her head snapped round the right way with a crack. Her whole body jerked, as if she had touched a live wire.

And every rat in the room screamed with her.

Thirty small throats, on the wardrobe and the sill and the sofa, all opening at the same moment, all shrieking the same thin shriek, the shriek of the thing that was looking out of them. Then they ran. All of them at once, off the furniture, across the floor, over Rei's feet, over Itsuki's legs where he had fallen back into the kitchen doorway, a brown wave of small bodies going for the door and the window, and out, and gone. The thing was pulling its eyes back. It was running.

"Rei!" Itsuki was on the floor in the kitchen doorway. "Rei, they're..."

"They're only eyes," said Rei. "They can't hurt you. Throw iron. At her."

Itsuki caught the second jar, one-handed, which surprised them both, and tore the lid off with his teeth and threw. The second spray of filings hit Mrs. Tachibana full in the chest.

The thing came out of her.

Rei saw it with the door open. A grey-yellow shape, thin and stretched, like smoke with too many elbows, pulling itself out of her open mouth and nose and ears. It hung over her for one second. It looked at Rei. He felt it look, with all its bottomless wanting. Then it went out of the window after its rats, into the dark, toward the river.

The room was silent except for the television, still showing its quiz show with the sound off.

Mrs. Tachibana was lying on her side on the mat.

[break The last thing]

Rei knelt beside her. Her eyes were brown now. The black had gone back to two small ordinary dots. Ordinary brown eyes, wet and frightened. Her mouth was still full. She was trying to breathe and she could not. Her chest jerked. Her hands moved weakly at her throat.

"Itsuki, call an ambulance. Call now."

Itsuki was already dialling. His hands were shaking so badly he dropped the phone twice.

Rei turned her on her side and tried to clear her mouth with his fingers. Paper and rice and plastic and cotton wool. Some of it came. Most of it was too far down. She was looking at him the whole time. Her hand found his wrist and held it, very hard for a woman so small.

He did not know what else to do, so he opened the door in his chest all the way, and he looked at her.

He saw her. Not her past, like the cup in Lilac. Her now. A woman alone in a room full of small things. A son in Osaka who called on her birthday and on New Year. A husband who had died nine years ago. Nobody who touched her, ever, except the doctor once a year and the boy who brought the ramen on Thursdays and said *thank you, auntie*, and she ordered ramen every Thursday even when she was not hungry, just to hear him say it. That was what the thing had found in her, with its rats sitting outside her door every evening, watching. Not hunger for food. Hunger for one warm word. It had found it and climbed into it and eaten her from inside, using her own wanting to do it.

Her grip on his wrist went loose.

"I'm here," Rei said. He did not know if she could hear. "I'm here. Somebody's here."

Her eyes looked at him. Then they looked past him, at nothing. Then they did not look at anything.

The ambulance came in eleven minutes. By then she had been dead for eight.

[break The back of the card]

They sat on the bottom step of the outside staircase while the ambulance men went up. Rei's hands were covered in things he did not want to look at. Itsuki sat next to him with his arms round his knees, shaking. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Rei took out his wallet and found the white card with the owl on it. He turned it over to the side with the small neat handwriting.

*Call the back. Not the front. Never the front.*

He called the back.

It rang twice. "Okada."

"It's Rei," he said. "Uedera Rei. I'm in Akabane. There's a woman. She's dead. There were rats. It was in her. I couldn't..." His voice broke. He made it work again. "There are two others. I have the files. It's going along the river. It'll do it again."

There was a silence on the line. Then he heard, faintly, the sound of keys being picked up, and a door opening.

"Give me the address," said Okada. "Don't touch anything else. Don't talk to anyone in a nice suit. I'm coming."

She came in twenty minutes, in a taxi, in the same plain suit with shiny elbows. She looked at the stairs. She looked at the iron filings scattered across the walkway, and at the salt on the floor, and at Rei's hands. She did not ask a single question. She took a packet of wet wipes out of her bag and gave it to him, and then she took out her phone and made a call, and Rei heard her say, in a flat bored voice, "Okada, Investigation. Natural death, Akabane, one occupant, elderly. I'm on scene. No veil signature. No need for Public Confidence. I'll file in the morning."

She hung up. Rei stared at her.

"You lied," he said.

"Yes." She sat down on the step on his other side, between him and the street, and took out her three notebooks and opened the middle one. "If I report what happened here, men in blue suits will be here in twenty minutes, and they will ask who threw the iron, and then they will want to know who you are. And then they'll know." She clicked her pen. "Now. Show me the files. All three. And tell me about the rats from the beginning, slowly, and don't leave anything out."

Itsuki laughed, suddenly, a high shaky laugh that was half a sob.

"Sorry," he said, wiping his face with his sleeve. "Sorry. It's just. I've been terrified for an hour and she's the scariest thing I've seen all night."

Okada did not look up from her notebook.

"Thank you," she said.
