#title The White House
#part Book Two. The Families

On the first Sunday of November, Rei stood at the corner of the cinema plaza in Kabukichō at seven in the evening with his hood up, next to Yua, who was eating a crêpe he had paid for.

"You're going to follow her," said Yua, with her mouth full. "Aren't you."

"Yes."

"On your own."

"Yes."

"That's really stupid." She licked cream off her thumb. "Pay me another five thousand and I'll tell you which train she gets."

He paid her. She told him.

[break Grey coat]

At half past seven, a woman in her thirties in a long soft grey coat sat down on the low wall by the cinema steps next to a girl of about fifteen who was sitting alone with her knees pulled up. The woman was smiling. She was speaking softly. She had a kind face, Rei thought. A gentle, patient, ordinary face, like a nursery school teacher. She took a small white card out of her pocket and put it in the girl's hand and closed the girl's fingers over it. Then she stood up and walked away through the crowd.

Rei closed the door in his chest. All the way. He had read about it in the archive, the week before, in the section on following. *If you are following a practitioner, or anything that has been near something from the far side, close yourself. Completely. An open practitioner in a crowd is a lit window on a dark street. They will feel you looking.*

It was horrible. Like walking into the crowd with his eyes shut. The city went flat and loud and grey. But he kept the grey coat in sight, ten metres ahead, and he walked.

She went down into Shinjuku station. He followed her through the gates, through the huge underground corridors full of people going home on a Sunday night, families with shopping bags, students, couples, a man asleep against a pillar. She went down to the platform for a slow train going west, and stood near the front, and he stood near the back of the same carriage with his hood up and his phone in his hand, pretending to read it.

[need ch18_train_west | A quiet Sunday night local train going west out of Shinjuku, half empty, a woman in a long soft grey coat standing calmly near the front, a young man in a black hood at the far end pretending to look at his phone]

The train went out through the lit towers of Shinjuku, and then the towers stopped, and it was houses. Low houses, endless, roof after roof, with small lit windows and laundry on balconies and cars parked tight in tiny drives. The carriage emptied a little at every station. A man in a tracksuit got off. Two girls with tennis rackets got off. The woman in the grey coat did not move. She stood very straight, looking out of the window at the dark, and she did not sway when the train swayed.

Rei felt the cold go up his back. He did not open the door in his chest. He did not need to.

Six stops. Seven. At the eighth, a small station with one exit and a konbini and a bicycle parking lot, she got off. He waited until the doors were almost closing and got off too.

[break The street]

She walked for ten minutes. He walked forty metres behind her. The streets were quiet. Old wooden houses, small gardens behind low walls, persimmon trees with the last orange fruit hanging on bare branches. A dog barked behind a gate and stopped. A woman was taking in her washing from a balcony and looked down at him and went back inside.

Then the grey coat turned into a narrow street, and stopped at a gate, and Rei stopped at the corner and looked.

It was white. Completely white. White walls, white gate, white blinds in every window. A clean modern house on a quiet street of old wooden ones, with a hedge clipped very square. The woman went in through the white gate. The door opened before she reached it. She went in. The door closed.

Rei stood at the corner by a vending machine for a long time.

Then, very slowly, he lifted the latch on his wrist. A finger's width. No more.

The cold hit him like a wave. Not the cold of the platform or the river. Something older and softer and much, much bigger. It came off the white house the way warmth comes off a stove, except it was the opposite of warmth. He felt the dead in it. Not ghosts. Not spirits. Something like what was left in a cup after someone has drunk all the tea. Many cups. Dozens. A house full of empty cups.

And under all of it, at the centre, something sitting very still and very patient and very full.

One of the blinds on the ground floor was not quite closed. A gap of two fingers at the bottom, where the light came out. From the corner, at an angle, Rei could see in.

A long white room. White floor cushions in neat rows. A low table. On the cushions, people sitting very still, with their hands in their laps and their eyes closed. Ten of them, maybe twelve. Young and old. Very still. Smiling. And at the head of the room, kneeling at the low table, a woman with long black hair and a gentle face, pouring tea.

He knew that room. He had seen it in pencil, in a folder in an art studio, drawn by his best friend from his brother's description. He had seen it on a screen in Gin's room. The last file Gin had opened before he disappeared.

The woman at the table stopped pouring.

She lifted her head and looked at the window. Through the gap. Down the street. Straight at the corner by the vending machine where Rei stood with his hood up and his latch lifted one finger's width.

She smiled. It was a lovely smile. Soft and warm and kind, the smile of a teacher with a slow child. And her eyes above it were black. The pupils were so wide that there was almost no colour left, and they did not look at the street the way a person looks at a street. They drank it in. The gate, the hedge, the dark, the vending machine, and Rei, all at once, with an intensity no human face can hold for more than a second. She held it. She went on holding it. Under the gentle smile, those eyes wanted. Rei could feel it from forty metres away. A wanting so deep and so patient it had no bottom.

He dropped the latch so hard his wrist hurt.

[break Running]

He did not remember the walk back to the station. He remembered running. He remembered the persimmon trees going past, and the barking dog, and the konbini lights. He remembered standing on the platform with his heart going so fast he thought he would be sick, looking back up the dark street, again and again, to see if a grey coat was coming.

Nothing came.

On the train back, in the empty carriage, he sat with his hands between his knees and the door in his chest shut tight and tried to stop shaking. He took out his phone and opened the map and found where he was, and saved the station, and the street, and the time. Then he sat and looked at the screen until the towers of Shinjuku came back up out of the dark.

When he got off, Yua was waiting at the gate. Eating another crêpe. She looked at his face.

"You saw her," she said.

"A white room," said Rei. "And a teacher."

Yua stopped eating. For one second she looked her age.

"Mina said that," she said quietly. "The only thing she ever said about it. *A white room and a teacher.*" She threw the rest of the crêpe in a bin. "Did she see you?"

Rei thought of the black eyes drinking him in across forty metres of dark street.

"Yes," he said.

Yua looked at him for a long time.

"Then don't go back alone," she said. "Ever." She pulled her hood up and turned away into the crowd. "That one's free."
