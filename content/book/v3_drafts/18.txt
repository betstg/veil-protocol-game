#title Gorō
#part Book Two. The Families

The crow had been following him for weeks before he noticed.

He noticed on a Saturday at the end of October, sitting in Tanizaki's front room with his second cup of tea, when Tanizaki lowered his newspaper and looked past him, out at the wild garden, and said, "Your friend is back."

Rei turned. On the stone lantern in the ivy, very close to the open door, sat a crow. A big one, glossy and black, with a heavy beak like a pair of pliers and a ragged feather sticking out of its left wing. It was looking at Rei with its head on one side.

He knew it. He realised it all at once. He had seen it on the path in Ueno park on the morning of the thirteenth of August, watching him go past as if memorising his face. He had seen it on the wire outside the police station. On the railing of the Arakawa bank. On the roof of the bath house, by the tall chimney. On the vending machine outside the Kuroda wall, the night Itsuki sat on the pavement and drank corn soup. He had seen it twenty times and never once thought, *that's the same bird.*

"That's Gorō," said Tanizaki. He went back to his newspaper. "He belonged to a medium in Ueno called Old Kawabata. She died in the spring. He has been looking for somebody since." He turned a page. "It seems he has decided."

"Decided what?"

"That you are interesting." Tanizaki did not look up. "Crows are very particular about who they find interesting. It took him four years to decide about Kawabata, and she fed him every day."

Rei looked at the crow. The crow looked at Rei. Then it opened its beak and said, very clearly, in a rough deep voice like an old man clearing his throat, "*Kaa.*"

"He wants an egg," said Tanizaki. "There are eggs in the kitchen. A boiled one, please. He does not eat raw. He is a snob."

[break The mirror]

He read the glyph pages that night.

In the back third of the archive Gin had a chapter he called *Symbols, Seals and Tattoos*. After it came a dictionary of shapes. Hundreds of them, drawn in ink, each one with a name and a few lines of notes. Rei had watched his brother draw these shapes at the kitchen table a hundred times and never asked what they were. Gin had noticed that. He had written it in the margin. *R. watched me draw for an hour tonight. Did not ask. One day he will.*

A symbol, Gin wrote, is not a prayer. It is a shape, and the shape does the work, the way a key opens a lock whether or not the person turning it believes in keys. You draw it, and you put a little of yourself into it, and it holds. Ink on paper holds for a few hours. Chalk on stone, for a night. A tattoo holds forever and never switches off.

Rei went slowly through the dictionary with a cup of cold tea. Wards. Locks. Seals. A shape for keeping a spirit in a room. A shape for keeping one out. And near the end, on a page with a pencil drawing of a bird in the corner, he found it.

*Shared sight. Two-eye glyph. Drawn on the back of a mirror. Practitioner and animal look into the glass together. If the animal consents, the practitioner can see what the animal sees, at a distance, while the glyph holds. Works best with birds. Works best of all with crows. Crows like mirrors. Cost: high. Headache, nausea, nosebleed. Do not hold the link for more than ten minutes. Do not try it with an animal that has not chosen you.*

Under it, in pencil, in the margin, Gin had written one more line.

*Kawabata does this with Gorō. Ask her to show you. Rei would love this.*

Rei read the line four times. Then he put his head down on the table on top of the archive and stayed there for a while.

[break The glass]

He bought a small round hand mirror from a hundred-yen shop. Plain plastic frame, cheap glass. On the back of it, on the grey plastic, he drew the two-eye glyph with a black ink pen, very slowly, copying it line by line from the archive. Two overlapping circles, each with a smaller circle inside like a pupil, and between them a shape like a bird's open wing. It took him nine tries on paper before he drew it on the mirror. His hand shook on the last line.

On Sunday morning he went to Tanizaki's garden with the mirror and two boiled eggs.

Gorō was on the lantern. He watched Rei come up the path. He watched him kneel down on the wet grass. He watched him peel an egg, very carefully, and put it on the flat top of a stone, and step back. Then he dropped down off the lantern, walked to the stone with his rolling crow walk, like a fat old man in a black coat, and ate the egg in four bites, looking at Rei the whole time.

Rei held out the mirror. Glass side toward the crow.

Gorō stopped eating. He tipped his head. He looked at himself in the glass. He hopped closer. He tapped the glass with his heavy beak, twice. *Tock. Tock.* Then he looked over the top of the mirror, at Rei, with one bright black eye.

Rei leaned in so that both their faces were in the little round glass together. A crow's black head. A tired young man's face with messy hair. Side by side.

He opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

"Will you show me?" he said. "Please?"

Gorō looked at the two of them in the mirror for a long moment.

"*Kaa,*" he said quietly.

And Rei was looking at himself.

From the side. From below. A young man kneeling on wet grass, holding a cheap plastic mirror, enormous, like a giant. Everything was wrong. The colours were wrong. The grass was a hundred shades of green he had never seen, and the sky was full of a colour he had no name for, a shining violet over everything. The world was very sharp and very wide. He could see almost all the way round himself without turning his head. He could see Tanizaki inside the house, through the open door, watching with his newspaper lowered. He could see a beetle on the fence three metres away, every leg.

Then Gorō spread his wings and jumped, and Rei's stomach dropped out of him.

[break Flying]

He had never been so frightened or so happy in his life.

The garden fell away beneath him. The wild green of it, the fig tree, the roof of Tanizaki's house like a tired horse. Then the lane. The old shopping street. The shrine with the two stone foxes. Kita spread out under him like a map, grey and green and brown, the railway lines shining, the trains crawling along them like green and blue caterpillars. The wind went over him. He felt it on wings he did not have. He felt the crow's body, small and hot and fast, with its heart going like a drum.

He could not steer. He did not try. Gorō flew where Gorō wanted, in long loops, over the station, over the river, round the tall chimney of Tsuru-no-yu, as if he were showing Rei his city. *Here. And here. And here. This is mine.*

On his knees on the wet grass in the garden, far away, Rei felt warm wet blood begin to run out of his nose. He did not care.

After six minutes Gorō turned and came home. He landed on the stone lantern with a bump, and folded his wings, and Rei was suddenly back in his own body, kneeling on the grass, with his head pounding like a hammer and blood all down his chin and his stomach turning over and over.

He threw up in Tanizaki's weeds.

When he could see again, Tanizaki was standing over him with a towel and a glass of water.

"Kawabata used to do that the first time too," said Tanizaki mildly. "She said it was worth it." He held out the towel. "Was it?"

Rei wiped his face. He looked up at the crow on the lantern, which was cleaning its ragged wing feather and ignoring them both.

"Yes," he said. "God, yes."

Tanizaki almost smiled. "Gin would have liked to see that," he said. And then, as if it had slipped out by mistake, he turned and went back into the house very quickly.

[break Sunday]

He used it on the first Sunday of November.

He stood at the corner of the cinema plaza in Kabukichō at seven in the evening with his hood up, next to Yua, who was eating a crêpe he had paid for. Gorō sat on the edge of a hotel roof above them, looking down at the crowd with total contempt.

"There," said Yua quietly, at half past seven. "Grey coat. By the steps."

A woman in her thirties in a long soft grey coat had sat down on the low wall by the cinema steps next to a girl of about fifteen who was sitting alone with her knees pulled up. The woman was smiling. She was speaking softly. She had a kind face, Rei thought. A gentle, patient, ordinary face, like a nursery school teacher. She took a small white card out of her pocket and put it in the girl's hand and closed the girl's fingers over it. Then she stood up and walked away through the crowd.

Rei took out the mirror. He held it up so that it caught the light from the signs. On the hotel roof, Gorō turned his head.

"*Kaa,*" Rei whispered.

And he was on the roof, looking down at a river of heads, and in the river of heads one grey coat was moving away toward the station.

He held the link for nine minutes. He watched through Gorō's eyes as the woman in the grey coat walked to Shinjuku station and went down the steps. Gorō could not follow her underground. He circled. He flew west over the tracks, over the long roofs, following the lines, and Rei felt the crow's annoyance and boredom and something else, a growing unease, a feeling like a bad smell coming closer. They came out over a quiet residential area, low houses and narrow streets and small gardens, and the grey coat came up out of a small station and walked. Gorō followed her above the rooftops.

She stopped at a house.

It was white. Completely white. White walls, white gate, white blinds in every window. A clean modern house on a quiet street of old wooden ones, with a hedge clipped very square. The woman went in through the white gate. The door opened before she reached it.

Gorō dropped lower. He landed on a telephone wire across the street, looking at the house. And Rei felt the crow's unease turn into something else. Feathers lifting. The urge to scream. Gorō was a crow who knew the dead. It was what he had been trained for. He could smell them on the wind the way a dog smells a fox. And from that white house came a smell of the dead so thick that his whole small body wanted to flee.

One of the blinds on the ground floor was not quite closed. Through the gap, Gorō looked in.

A long white room. White floor cushions in neat rows. A low table. On the cushions, people sitting very still, with their hands in their laps and their eyes closed. Ten of them, maybe twelve. Young and old. Very still. Smiling. And at the head of the room, kneeling at the low table, a woman with long black hair and a gentle face, pouring tea.

Rei knew that room. He had seen it in pencil, in a folder in an art studio, drawn by his best friend from his brother's description. He had seen it on a screen in Gin's room. The last file Gin had opened before he disappeared.

The woman at the table lifted her head and looked straight at the window.

Straight at the crow on the wire.

Straight through the crow, at Rei.

She smiled. It was a lovely smile. Soft and warm and kind, the smile of a teacher with a slow child. And her eyes above it were completely, perfectly empty. Not white like the rats. Not wild like the man on the platform. Empty like a house where everyone has moved away and left the lights on.

Gorō screamed and flew.

The link broke like a snapped wire. Rei was on his knees on the pavement in Kabukichō with blood pouring out of his nose and Yua holding his hood with both hands, shouting his name, and a crowd of strangers stepping round him without looking.

"What did you see?" Yua was saying. "Hey. Hey. What did you see?"

Rei wiped his face with his sleeve. His hand was shaking so hard he could not hold it still.

"A white room," he said. "And a teacher."

Above them, on the edge of the hotel roof, Gorō landed hard, and fluffed out every feather on his body, and sat there making a low furious noise in his throat, like a kettle about to boil. He did not look down at Rei for a long time.

When he did, he said "*Kaa,*" once, very quietly. And Rei understood him perfectly.

*Never again.*
