#title Nezu
#part Book One. The Archive

On Saturday Rei went back to Tanizaki's house and sat in the front room for an hour.

He did not ask for anything. It was the hardest hour of his life. He knelt at the low table with his hands on his knees. Tanizaki poured him one cup of tea, and later a second. He drank them slowly. Tanizaki read a newspaper. Outside in the wild garden a bush warbler sang the same three notes over and over, and the cicadas sawed in the fig tree, and once a cat walked along the top of the fence, looked in at them both with yellow eyes, and walked on.

Every few minutes Rei wanted to say *Gin*. The word sat in his mouth like a stone. He did not say it.

After an hour Tanizaki folded his newspaper and looked at the teapot. There was tea left in it. He did not pour a third cup.

"Better," he said. "Come back next Saturday."

That was all. Rei walked home with his knees aching and his mind full of a strange calm that lasted almost until the bottom of the hill.

[break Itsuki's offer]

Itsuki came over that night with a bag of oranges and two cans of beer and sat on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge.

"Tell me again," he said. "The arcade thing. The cup thing. All of it."

Rei told him. Itsuki listened with his head tipped back against the fridge door and his eyes closed. When Rei got to the mug in Lilac, and the dead bartender who never said sorry, Itsuki opened his eyes.

"You read a cup," he said.

"I held it and I saw what it saw."

"That's a real thing. That's a thing people train for years to do." Itsuki peeled an orange in one long spiral without looking at it. "My cousins train for years. My brother trained for years. They'd kill to be able to do that at nineteen with no teacher."

"I have a teacher. He gives me two cups of tea and reads the newspaper."

"That's not a teacher, that's a grandfather." Itsuki split the orange and gave him half. He was quiet for a while. When he spoke again his voice was different. Flatter. Like a boy reciting something he had been made to learn.

"The Kuroda have trained people like you for four hundred years," he said. "There's a hall in the house in Nezu with a floor so old it's gone soft and shiny from bare feet. There's a library of things the CVD doesn't have. There's people who know how to do what you did in Jūjō without falling over afterwards." He ate a piece of orange. "And there's my father."

Rei waited.

"I hate it there," said Itsuki. "I hate the house. I hate the way everyone talks quietly in the corridors like someone's dying. I hate that my whole life was decided before I could walk, and that I'm the son who paints pictures and doesn't count." He was looking at the floor. "But if you want to find Gin, you need to learn faster than an old man with a teapot is going to teach you. And my family are the fastest teachers in Tokyo."

"You'd take me there?"

"I'd ask my father to see you." Itsuki laughed, a short laugh with nothing in it. "I've never asked him for anything. Not once. Not since I was ten. So he'll probably say yes just from shock."

"Itsuki. You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to." Itsuki threw the orange peel at him. It hit him in the chest. "Shut up. I'm being noble. It doesn't happen often. Let me enjoy it."

[break The house]

They went on Monday evening. The last day of August. The heat had broken a little, and there was a wind coming off the hills that smelled of rain.

The Kuroda house was in Nezu, ten minutes' walk from the basement bar with the moon on its door. Rei had walked past its wall a dozen times on Friday nights without knowing what was behind it. It was a long, plain, old wall of grey plaster with a tiled roof, higher than a man, running the whole length of a lane. There was one gate in it. A heavy wooden gate, very old and very dark, with a small door cut into it for people to step through. There was no nameplate. There was no bell. There was a camera, small and black, set high in the corner of the roof tiles, that turned to look at them as they came up the lane.

Itsuki stopped in front of the gate. He had dressed for it. A dark shirt with a proper collar, ironed, black trousers, real shoes instead of his paint-covered trainers. He had tried to clean the paint off his hands and had not quite managed. He looked like a stranger. He looked like a son.

"Last chance to run away," he said.

"Are you talking to me or to you?"

"Both." He breathed in, and out, and put his palm flat on the wood of the small door. It opened inward before he pushed it.

Inside was another world.

[need ch9_kuroda_garden | Inside a high grey plaster wall in Nezu at dusk, an old Japanese estate garden with raked gravel, a dark pond, stone lanterns being lit, long low wooden houses with deep eaves, Itsuki in a dark ironed shirt and Rei in a black hooded jacket walking a stone path]

A garden, wide and dark and still. Raked white gravel in long lines like water. A pond with black water and the slow orange shapes of carp moving under the surface. Old pine trees trained into low, twisted shapes over centuries. Stone lanterns along a path of flat stones, and a man in a grey work kimono going from lantern to lantern, lighting them one by one with a long taper. Beyond the garden, long low wooden houses with deep roofs, joined to each other by covered walkways. Paper screens glowing yellow from inside. The sound of the city, which had been everywhere a moment before, was gone. Not quieter. Gone. As if the wall cut it off with a knife.

Rei felt the pressure behind his eyes start at once. Not hard. Soft and steady, like the pressure of deep water. The air here was thick with something. Old things. Patient things. He did the breathing and closed the door in his chest, and it closed, but it was harder than usual, as if the latch were stiff.

A woman in a dark kimono was waiting for them on the path. She bowed to Itsuki, very low.

"Young master," she said. "Your father is in the old wing."

Itsuki's jaw went tight. "Don't call me that, Mrs. Fujii."

"Yes, young master," she said, and turned and led the way.

[break Kuroda Sōun]

The old wing was the oldest of the houses, at the far end of the garden, with a roof of dark grey tiles grown over with green moss. Inside it smelled of wood and incense and very old paper. They walked down a long corridor of polished boards that were so old they had worn into soft curves, like the steps of a temple. At the end was a room with its screens slid open onto the garden.

A man knelt alone in the middle of the room.

[need ch9_kuroda_soun | Kuroda Sōun, head of the Kuroda family, late fifties, upright, severe silver hair, dark formal haori, kneeling alone in an empty old tatami room with screens open to a dark garden, a tall black lacquered free-standing doorframe behind him]

He was in his late fifties. He sat very upright, with his hands on his thighs. He wore a dark formal haori, the jacket that men wear over a kimono for serious occasions, with a small white family crest on each side of the chest. His hair was silver and cut short and severe. His face was long and still, with deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth. He did not look up when they came in. He was looking at the garden.

Behind him, in the empty room, stood a doorframe.

Only a doorframe. Two tall upright posts and a crossbeam of black lacquered wood, standing on its own in the middle of the tatami floor, with nothing on either side of it. No wall. No door. A thick rope of twisted white paper hung across the top of it. Through the frame Rei could see the back wall of the room, plain plaster. It was the strangest object he had ever seen. It made the back of his neck cold.

Itsuki knelt. Rei knelt beside him a beat late.

"Father," said Itsuki.

"Itsuki." The voice was low and dry. Each word was put down carefully, like a stone in a garden. "You have paint on your hands."

"I always have paint on my hands."

"Yes." Kuroda Sōun turned his head at last. He looked at his son for a long moment. Rei could not read the look at all. Then he looked at Rei, and Rei felt it. The look went into him, like cold water into a cup. Not unkind. Measuring. "And this is the Uedera boy."

"Uedera Rei," said Rei. He bowed, low, with his forehead almost to the mat.

"I know your name. I knew your brother." Sōun said it without any warmth, but without any coldness either. "He came to this house once, six years ago, to ask about a matter in Kita. He was very polite and very stubborn and he did not get what he came for. He did not get angry about it. I remember that." He paused. "The family has an opinion about your brother. It is not entirely negative."

From Kuroda Sōun, Rei understood, that was a great deal.

"Itsuki has asked me to train you," Sōun went on. "Itsuki has not asked me for anything in ten years." He did not look at his son as he said it. "So I wanted to see what kind of person could make him do it."

Rei did not know what to say. He said the only true thing he had. "My brother is missing. The CVD won't look for him. I can see things and I don't know how to use what I see. I need to learn quickly."

"Everybody needs to learn quickly," said Sōun. "Nobody can."

He stood up. He did it in one smooth movement, without using his hands, the way old men who have knelt all their lives can. He walked to the black doorframe and put his right hand flat against one of the posts.

"The Kuroda are a family of doors," he said. "Do you know what that means?"

"No, sir."

"Then watch."

He did not do anything Rei could see. He did not say anything. He stood with his hand on the post and his eyes half closed, and he breathed out slowly. And the plaster wall that Rei could see through the frame was not there any more.

Through the frame, now, was a garden at night. A different garden. Smaller, with a low hedge and a stone bench. Beyond the hedge was a long dark line, and over it a dark sky with a few stars, and a sound came through the frame into the room. A soft rushing sound, rising and falling. A wind came through with it, cool and wet and smelling of salt.

The sea.

"Kamakura," said Sōun. "Fifty kilometres. Our house by the sea." He took his hand away from the post, and the sea was gone, and there was only plaster again. The salt smell stayed in the room for a moment, then faded. "Every Kuroda is born with a door inside them. Most of them never open it. My son has a door inside him that he has kept shut for twenty years out of pure stubbornness." He did not look at Itsuki. "You have something else inside you. I can see that from here. I do not know what yet."

"Will you teach me?" said Rei.

Sōun looked at him for a long time. Then he looked, at last, at his son.

"I will teach him," he said to Itsuki, "on one condition. You train with him. Every day that he trains, you train. You open the door you have kept shut."

Itsuki went white. Rei saw his hands close into fists on his thighs. For a moment Rei was sure he would stand up and walk out, and Rei would not have blamed him.

Itsuki did not stand up.

"Fine," he said. His voice was thin. "Fine. Every day."

Sōun nodded once. He turned back to the garden, as if they had already left. "Tomorrow at six," he said. "The training hall. Wear clothes you can move in. Do not eat breakfast."

Mrs. Fujii was waiting at the door to lead them out.

[break The lane]

Outside the gate, in the lane, the city came back all at once. A car horn. A television. A woman calling a child in for dinner. Itsuki walked fast, ahead of Rei, with his hands jammed in his pockets, until they were round the corner and out of sight of the camera on the wall. Then he stopped and leaned against a vending machine and slid down it until he was sitting on the pavement.

Rei sat down next to him.

"You okay?"

"No." Itsuki laughed, shakily. "No. That was horrible. Did you see his face? *You have paint on your hands.* Twenty years. Twenty years and that's the first thing."

"Itsuki. You don't have to train. I'll tell him..."

"Shut up. I said I'd do it." Itsuki put his head back against the glowing machine. The red and white light of it fell across his face. "You know what's stupid? I want to. Just a bit. I want to know what's behind the door." He closed his eyes. "Don't tell anyone I said that."

"I won't."

"Especially don't tell my father."

"I'll take it to my grave."

Itsuki opened one eye. "Don't say grave. Not this week."

They sat on the pavement for a while. Then Rei stood up and put coins in the vending machine and bought two cans of hot corn soup, because he had learned that from Haruki, and gave one to Itsuki. Itsuki looked at the can, and at him, and something in his face went soft and strange for a second, a look Rei had seen before and never understood. Then he grinned his crooked grin and pulled the ring.

"Six in the morning," he said. "No breakfast. I'm going to die."

"We're going to die together."

"Romantic," said Itsuki, and drank his soup.
