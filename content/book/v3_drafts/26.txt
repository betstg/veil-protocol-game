#title The First One
#part Book Two. The Families

His training officer was a woman called Shimizu Ren, and the first thing she ever said to him was, "That tie is wrong."

She was standing in the trainee room on the second floor at eight in the morning on the seventeenth of December, with her arms folded, looking at him the way you look at a car that is parked badly. She was about thirty, lean and still, in a plain dark suit with no jewellery at all. Her hair was cut short and practical. She had a face that did not move much, and a look on it that Rei thought, at first, was irritation. A small line between the eyebrows. A slight flatness to the mouth. As if he had already done something annoying, and she was waiting to see what he would do next.

"Sorry," he said, and reached for the tie.

"Don't fix it now. You'll make it worse." She looked at him a moment longer. "Three things," she said. "One, you're going to ask me about your brother before lunch. Don't. I won't answer in this building. Two, you're going to try to do everything faster than I tell you, because he did. Don't. Three, you're going to want to go places alone. Don't." She turned and walked to the door. "Come on. Field."

Rei followed her. Behind him, Kato Ryusei, at a desk by the window with a tower of photocopies, made a small choking noise into his coffee.

"What?" Rei said, under his breath, as he passed.

"Nothing," said Kato. "She likes you. I've never heard her say that many words to a trainee in two years."

[break What she saw]

Here is something Rei would learn about Shimizu Ren over the next months. The look on her face was not irritation.

She was a clairsentient, like him. But she did not read objects, or rooms, or the past. She read the shape of things. Situations. People. The way a building stood, the way a crowd moved, the way a conversation was going. She saw where the weight was and where the cracks were. And so, whenever she walked into anything, a room, a street, a case, she saw, almost at once, all the ways it could go wrong. Three, usually. Sometimes five. The small line between her eyebrows was her choosing which one to deal with first.

She had been doing it for ten years. She was almost never wrong. Gin had written about her in the archive. *If she says something won't work, it won't work. I have tested this. It was expensive.*

[break The telephone]

The case was an old public telephone in Jūjō, on the corner by the station, a green box with a glass door that almost nobody used any more. For two months, people waiting for the bus had heard a girl's voice coming from it in the evenings. Very quiet. Saying the same thing over and over. *Mum? Mum, it's me. Can you hear me? Mum?*

"Class E," said Shimizu, as they walked from the station. "Fragment. A tiny piece of someone. One feeling, one habit, left behind. No will. No danger. Perfect for your first capture." She glanced at him. "Three ways it goes wrong. You open your door too wide and it gets frightened and goes into the wires. You try to talk to it before it's in the box and it tells you its whole life story and you start crying on a street corner. Or the bus comes and someone steps into the box to make a call." She did not slow down. "Number three's the most likely. Take out your Capturer."

Rei took out the small black box. He had read the training sheet three times last night. He held it the way the sheet said. Screen toward him. Thumb over the button marked CAPTURE.

"Open a finger's width," said Shimizu. "Find it. When you can feel it clearly, point the top of the box at it and press. Don't think. Don't ask it anything. Just press."

He opened the door in his chest. The latch on his wrist held it at a finger's width. The cold came in.

The phone box was full of a small sad weight. Like a child's coat left on a hook. He could feel her. Not see her. A girl, maybe ten. A Wednesday, a long time ago. A coin in her hand, warm from holding it. A number she knew by heart. *Mum, it's me. I'll be late. Don't worry.* And then something. He did not let himself see what. Something that meant she never got home to say it to her face.

A bus was turning into the road at the end of the street.

He pointed the top of the box at the phone. He pressed.

The row of five small lights across the top of the Capturer lit up, one after another, left to right, amber. A soft high whine came from inside the box, like a mosquito. On his wrist the latch went very cold. In the phone box the small sad weight lifted, turned, like a leaf on water, and went toward him. Toward the box in his hand. He felt it go in. It was like feeling a bird land in your cupped hands, very light, very frightened.

The five amber lights went out. On the narrow green screen, three letters appeared, in small square old-fashioned type.

*SEALED.*

The bus pulled up at the stop. A salaryman got off, walked straight to the green phone box, opened the glass door, stepped in and picked up the receiver.

Shimizu looked at Rei.

"Number three," she said.

[break The light]

Under the screen, the single larger light came on.

It glowed. Soft green, like the light of a firefly. Not bright. Steady.

"Green," said Shimizu. "Good. Kind. Lost." She took the box out of his hand and looked at it, then gave it back. "Most things you'll catch will be green. Small dead things that don't know where to go. A few will be yellow. Confused. Angry. Stuck. And once in a while," she said, "it'll be red. When it's red, you don't talk to it. You don't open the box. You bring it straight to me, and I take it downstairs to Reintegration, and you go and eat something hot and forget you ever held it." She looked at him. "Understood?"

"Understood."

"Now you can talk to it," she said. "Press TALK. Not too loud."

He pressed the small rubber button. The screen went blank for a moment. Then, slowly, letters appeared. One at a time. As if someone very small were pressing very hard on a very old typewriter.

*hello*

Rei looked at it. He did not know what to say. He typed back with the button, the way the sheet said, one letter at a time, clumsy. *Hello. My name is Rei.*

A long pause.

*is mum there*

He looked at Shimizu. She was looking at the bus stop, not at him.

*No,* he typed. *I'm sorry. Not here.*

*oh*

Another pause.

*its cold*

*I know,* typed Rei. *I'm sorry. I'll keep you warm. What's your name?*

The screen stayed empty for a long time. The salaryman in the phone box finished his call and came out and walked away. The bus left. Shimizu stood with her hands in her pockets. Rei held the little box in both hands, and the green light glowed softly between his fingers, and the latch on his wrist was cold, and he waited.

*suzu,* said the screen.

And on the front of the Capturer, the little plastic flap over the third button, the one marked HOST, made a small click, and the tape across it split down the middle by itself.

Rei stared at it.

"That's what happens," said Shimizu quietly, "when you learn the name." She looked at the split tape. "Now it could ride you. If you let it. If you pressed that button and said yes." She took her hand out of her pocket and put one finger on the flap and pressed it closed again. "Don't. Not with this one. Not with any of them, until you know what you're doing. Your brother pressed that button exactly four times in six years. He said each time cost him a month."

*Suzu,* Rei thought. A small girl's name. A bell.

He typed. *Hello, Suzu.*

*hello rei*

[break What demons are]

They walked back to the station in the cold. It was getting dark. The shops along the street were lit up, and someone had already put out a small Christmas tree in front of the pharmacy, with blinking coloured lights and a plastic Santa.

"You'll catch a lot of Suzus," said Shimizu. "Small dead things. Ghosts. They're the easy part." She did not look at him. "One day you'll meet a demon. And you need to understand what a demon is before that day, because if you don't, it will kill you."

"Okada said a demon is a spirit that decided to feed and grow instead of fading away."

"That's what it is. That's not what it's like." Shimizu stopped at the crossing and waited for the light. "A spirit is a person, a little bit. Something left over from someone. You can talk to it. Reason with it. Help it. A demon is not a person. Not any more. A demon is weather."

"Weather?"

"A typhoon. An earthquake. Something that just happens." She watched the red light. "It's not evil, the way a man is evil. It doesn't hate you. It's impulse. A demon wants something, and it does it, the same second it wants it. No thinking. No reasons. No weighing it up. And when it's wearing a person, it makes that person do things that aren't in them at all. A gentle grandmother bites a stranger. A shy boy walks off a platform smiling. A kind man stands still while someone dies." The light went green. She did not move. "That's how you know one is there. Not by the cold. By the person doing something that isn't them. Some demons can be... kind, maybe. In their way. For a while. And the next second, on a whim, they're not." She started across the road. "Never trust one. Never. Not even the kind ones."

"Then how do you deal with them?"

Shimizu looked at him then. For the first time, the small line between her eyebrows went away.

"Contracts," she said.

They crossed the road.

"A demon never lies about a promise," said Shimizu. "Never. Not once in all the history of anything. They lie about everything else. What they are. What they want. What they'll do to you. But if a demon makes you a promise, it keeps it. If it signs a contract, it keeps every word of it, to the letter, forever, even if keeping it destroys it." She was walking fast now. "It's the one law they can't break. It's in what they are. A demon that breaks its word stops being a demon. It just comes apart."

"So you make contracts with them?"

"*They* don't make contracts with *us*," said Shimizu. "That's the point. Because they keep them, they almost never make them. A demon will do anything to you, but it will almost never *promise* you anything. A promise is a chain. They hate chains." She stopped at the top of the station steps. "The Vessel Program is built on that. People who agree to carry a demon, and the demon agrees, in a contract, what it will and won't do. And the demon keeps the contract. Always. To the letter." Her face was very flat. "The trouble is, the letter is never what the person thought it meant."

Rei thought of the man in the expensive suit in the empty swimming pool. *They said it was cohabitation. A partnership.* His sister in a clean room with soft toys on the bed, calling him by the wrong name.

"So if a demon ever offers me a contract," he said slowly.

"Run," said Shimizu. "And if you can't run," she said, going down the steps into the station, "read every single word. Twice. And then read what it didn't write."

[break Suzu]

That night he did not take the Capturer down to Reintegration. He was supposed to. The sheet said all captured entities must be logged and transferred within twenty-four hours. He had twenty-two hours left.

He sat on the sofa in the flat with the little black box on his knee and the green light glowing in the dark, and he typed, one slow letter at a time.

*Suzu. Do you remember where you lived?*

A long wait.

*near the big river. green roof. a dog called pochi*

*Do you remember your mum's name?*

Another wait. Longer.

*mum*

That was all she had. She was only a piece. One phone call. One Wednesday. One worry. *Don't worry, Mum. I'll be late.*

In the morning he took her to the Division and logged her in the register and handed her to a tired woman in Reintegration, who looked at the green light and smiled and said she would go to a quiet room and then, in a few weeks, to the people whose job was to help small things like her go on. Rei did not know where *on* was. He found he did not want to ask.

Before he handed over the box, he typed one more time.

*Goodbye, Suzu. Your mum isn't worried. I promise.*

The green light flickered.

*ok*

He walked up the stairs to the second floor, to the trainee room, where Shimizu Ren was standing by the window with a cup of coffee looking at the railway lines. She did not turn round.

"You kept it overnight," she said.

"Yes."

"You talked to it."

"Yes."

"You made it a promise."

Rei stopped.

"I heard you think it, all the way down the stairs," said Kato, from his desk, behind his tower of photocopies. "Sorry. Really loud."

Shimizu turned round. The small line was back between her eyebrows.

"Be careful with promises, Uedera," she said. "Even to the small ones. Especially to the small ones." She drank her coffee. "One day you'll make one to something that remembers."
