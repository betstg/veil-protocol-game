#title Itsuki at the Grid
#part Book Two. The Families

The November Gathering was in an old bowling alley in Itabashi that had closed in 2008.

It still had its lanes. Twenty-four of them, long and pale and dusty under the camping lanterns, with the pins still standing at the far ends in neat white triangles, as if the last game had been called off in the middle. The scoring screens hung dead above each lane. There was a counter where you used to rent shoes, with the shoes still on the shelves behind it, rows and rows of red and blue bowling shoes, cracked and stiff, waiting for feet that were never coming. People sat on the plastic seats by the lanes and on the floor and on the counter. The air smelled of dust and old carpet and the tea in Switch's flask.

[need ch20_bowling | A closed old bowling alley lit by camping lanterns, dusty lanes with pins still standing, rows of old red and blue bowling shoes on shelves behind a counter, a crowd of ordinary people of all ages sitting on plastic seats, two young men standing near the entrance]

Itsuki stood in the doorway and looked at it all for a long time.

"This is it?" he said. "This is the dangerous criminal underground?"

"Last month it was a swimming pool."

"I thought there'd be more leather." Itsuki looked at an old woman knitting by lane seven. "That lady looks like my piano teacher."

"Maybe she is your piano teacher."

"My piano teacher was terrifying. If that's her I'm leaving." But he did not leave. He walked in slowly, with his hands in the pockets of his huge grey hoodie, looking at everything. Rei watched him look. He had seen Itsuki look at a painting like that. Carefully. All the way through. Trying to see what was really there.

Haruki came over from the shoe counter with two paper cups of tea. He looked at Itsuki, and then at Rei, and smiled the crooked smile.

"Good," he said, and gave them each a cup. That was all he said. Rei thought it was exactly the right amount.

[break The meeting]

The meeting was the same as before. Switch on the counter now instead of a diving board, hands in pockets. *Nobody records. Nobody photographs.* Business. A man needed help with a house in Saitama. A woman had a list of three names of people who had gone to the Far Shore tea house and not come back, and wanted to know if anyone knew more. Nobody did. Rei held his cup very tightly while she talked, and Itsuki, beside him, felt it and moved a little closer, so that their shoulders touched.

Then Switch said, "Seventeen."

The room went quiet.

"Somebody brought me a list last month," said Switch. "Seventeen thin spots in Kita and Itabashi that the Division saw and decided to leave alone. We've closed nine of them." A small sound went round the room, a murmur. "Four were nothing. Three were bad. Two were very bad. One of them was the empty flats in Higashi-Jūjō." Switch looked straight at Rei across the room. "Somebody went in on their own and nearly didn't come out. And somebody else got them out in a way I've only seen done by one kind of family in this city."

Every head in the bowling alley turned. Not to Rei. To Itsuki.

[break Kuroda]

Itsuki went very still.

"What kind of family?" said a voice from the back. A man's voice, hard.

"I'll tell you what kind," said someone else. A woman by lane twelve, standing up. "Somebody opened a door in Higashi-Jūjō last Saturday night and stepped four kilometres to the Sumida in one step. I felt it from Akabane. Everybody who reads the city felt it." She pointed at Itsuki. "That's a gate. That's a Tower gate. That's Kuroda."

The murmur went round the room again. Different now. Colder.

Rei felt Itsuki's shoulder go stiff against his. He felt the room change. People who had been sitting relaxed were sitting up. The man in the expensive suit, the one whose sister was in the Vessel Program, had stood. The old woman by lane seven had stopped knitting.

"A Kuroda," said the man at the back. "At the Gathering. Who brought a Kuroda to the Gathering?"

"I did," said Rei.

"I vouched for both of them," said Haruki, very calmly, from the shoe counter.

"You would, Tsukishiro," said the man. "You'd vouch for anything with an old name." He was coming forward now, between the lanes. He was about forty, thick-armed, in a work jacket. "The Tower Families sit in their big houses and their gardens and they take money from the Division and they look down at us. And when the Division wants someone found, who do they ask? They ask the families. Who do you think tells them where we live?"

"I don't tell anybody anything," said Itsuki. His voice came out thin. "I don't even talk to my family."

"That's what they all say."

"It's true." Another voice. Quiet, from the dark at the end of lane twenty-four.

Everyone turned.

[break Sei]

A young man was sitting on the floor at the end of the far lane, with his back against the wall below the dead scoring screen. Rei had not seen him. Nobody seemed to have seen him. He was slight and narrow, about twenty-two, with his hair buzzed short at the sides and left longer on top. His clothes were dark and plain and clearly very expensive. On his right hand he wore an old silver ring with a seal on it. He was sitting with his knees up and his arms resting on them, and he was looking down the length of the dusty lane at Itsuki.

He had Itsuki's face. Older. Thinner. Harder. But the same narrow shape, the same quick tired eyes.

"He doesn't talk to the family," said the young man. "He's very good at not talking to the family. He's been doing it since he was ten." He did not get up. "I should know. I taught him."

Itsuki had gone so white that Rei put a hand on his arm, in case he fell.

"Sei," said Itsuki.

Kuroda Sei looked at his younger brother down twenty metres of bowling lane, with the white pins standing between them like an audience.

"You opened your gate," he said. "Finally. After twenty years." His voice was very flat. "For a friend. In your slippers. Father must be so proud."

"Father doesn't know."

"Father always knows." Sei's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "He'll pretend he doesn't. He'll wait for you to tell him. And then he'll be disappointed you didn't do it in front of him. Trust me." He looked at the man in the work jacket. "Leave him alone, Hamada. He's not a spy. He's a painter. He doesn't care about any of us. He doesn't even care about the family. Isn't that right, Itsuki?"

It was a test. Rei could feel it in the room. The whole Grid was watching.

Itsuki stood very still. Then he took his hands out of his pockets.

"I don't love my family," he said. His voice was shaking, but it was loud enough for everyone to hear. "My father talks to me like I'm a bad investment. My brother sells our house's secrets for a couch to sleep on and hasn't called me in four years." He did not look at Sei when he said it. "I didn't come here for them. I came here for him." He tilted his head at Rei. "His brother's missing. The Division isn't looking. You're the only people who are. So I'm here."

The room was quiet.

"Then promise," said Switch, from the counter.

Itsuki turned.

"Promise," said Switch, "that nothing you see here goes back to Nezu. Not a name. Not a face. Not where we met. Not that we exist. Not to your father. Not to anyone with your name." Their square face showed nothing. "You break that, and you're done. And so is he." A small nod at Rei. "And so is the Tsukishiro who vouched for you."

Itsuki looked at Rei. Rei could not tell him what to do. He did not try.

"I promise," said Itsuki.

"Say it to him," said Switch, and nodded down the lane at Sei.

Itsuki turned and looked at his brother. For a long moment neither of them said anything.

"I promise," said Itsuki. "I won't tell them anything. I won't be like you."

Something moved across Sei's face. Very fast. Rei saw it only because he had spent nine weeks learning to see things. It looked like something being hit.

Then it was gone, and Sei almost smiled.

"Good," he said. "Don't be."

He got up off the floor, in one smooth movement, the way his father did, without using his hands. He walked to the side door of the bowling alley, behind the dead lanes. At the door he stopped, with his back to the room.

"Itsuki," he said. "The bridge. You've been painting it for two years."

Itsuki did not answer.

"It's the one by Shirahige," said Sei. "The old footbridge. We used to go there when you were five, with Mother. You used to throw bread to the ducks and then cry because they ate it." He opened the door. "That's your door. It always was. I'm glad you found it."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the door swung shut behind him with a soft click.

[break After]

Afterward, in the street, Itsuki walked very fast with his hands in his pockets and his hood up and did not say anything for ten minutes. Rei walked beside him and did not say anything either. Haruki walked a little behind them, and did not say anything, which Rei was learning was his special skill.

At the station, Itsuki stopped under the lights.

"He remembered," he said. "The ducks. I didn't even remember the ducks."

"Yeah."

"Four years. Four years he didn't call. And he remembered the stupid ducks."

"Yeah."

Itsuki turned and looked at Rei. His face was wet under the hood. He was not trying to hide it.

"I said *I won't be like you,*" he said. "In front of everyone. I meant it to hurt him."

"I know."

"It did. I saw it." He wiped his face hard with his sleeve. "I've wanted to hurt him for four years and I finally did it and it feels horrible."

Haruki stepped forward then, and put a hand on Itsuki's shoulder, lightly, for one second.

"That's how you know you're not like him," he said. "He's done it so often he doesn't feel it any more." He took his hand away. "Or he pretends not to. With Kuroda it's hard to tell." He looked at the station clock. "Last train. Go home. Both of you. Same home, if you like. I'll see you Friday."

He walked off into the night, toward the taxi rank, with his collar up.

Itsuki stood under the station lights and looked at Rei.

"Can I sleep on your sofa?" he said. "I can't go back to Nezu tonight. I'll see my father's face and he'll know."

"You'll have to fight me for it. It's my sofa."

"I'll sleep on the floor. I don't care." Itsuki pulled his hood down. His eyes were red and his nose was red and he looked about fourteen. "I'm not in the Grid, Rei. And I'm not in the family. I don't know what I'm in."

"You're in the trio," said Rei. "Me, you, and a crow."

Itsuki stared at him. Then he laughed, wetly, and shoved him, gently, on the shoulder. The opposite of the shove in the training hall.

"That's the worst trio in the history of Tokyo," he said.

"I know."

"Okay," said Itsuki. "Okay. I'm in."

On the roof of the station, above them, a crow said *kaa* once, as if it had been asked.
