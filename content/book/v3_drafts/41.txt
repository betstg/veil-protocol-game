#title The Snake at the Door
#part Book Three. The Thirteen

It was not the Circle. That was the first thing Switch said.

"Different people. Different thing. Smaller. Newer." They were standing in the doorway of a closed launderette in Akabane, the Grid's message point that week, at two in the morning, with the rain coming down outside. "It calls itself the Hall of the Bright Sky. A man in Itabashi. Rents a hall above a pachinko parlour. Started a year ago with five people. Now there's forty." Switch's square face did not move. "Nine of them haven't been seen outside in a month. Their families come to the door. They're told their children are *in purification*. In the basement."

"Why us?" said Rei. "Why not the Division?"

"The Division went," said Switch. "In April. Two officers. They came out smiling and filed a report that said *legitimate religious organisation, no veil activity*." They looked at him. "One of the officers was from the third floor. You might know him. He's in Reintegration now. He still smiles. He hasn't stopped."

[break The hall]

[need ch41_bright_sky | A shabby upstairs hall above a neon pachinko parlour in Itabashi at night, rows of followers in white kneeling on cheap carpet facing a stage where a smiling middle-aged man in white stands under a painted blue sky on the ceiling, a narrow blue door at the back with a brown rat snake coiled on dark grave earth across its threshold]

The Hall of the Bright Sky was on the second floor of a building in Itabashi, above a pachinko parlour so loud that the floor shook. A pachinko parlour is a kind of gambling hall, full of noisy upright machines like pinball, where people sit for hours feeding steel balls into the machines and watching them bounce. The noise of ten thousand steel balls came up through the floorboards like rain on a tin roof.

The hall itself was one big room with cheap blue carpet and folding chairs pushed against the walls. Someone had painted the whole ceiling sky blue, with white clouds, badly. At the front was a low stage. On the stage stood a man.

He was about fifty. Ordinary. A little heavy, with thinning hair and a round, pleasant, smiling face, in a white robe like a bathrobe tied at the waist. He was talking. Forty people in white clothes knelt on the blue carpet in front of him, in rows, looking up at him with their faces shining.

"The sky," he was saying. "Look up. Look up at the sky. Isn't it beautiful? Isn't it the most beautiful thing you've ever seen?"

Forty faces looked up at the badly painted ceiling. Forty mouths opened. Forty pairs of eyes filled with tears.

Rei, at the back of the room in a borrowed white shirt, kneeling between Itsuki and Hana, opened the door in his chest one finger.

He felt it at once. A great wash of something, coming off the man on the stage like heat off a fire. Not fear. Not hunger. Something huge and bright and overwhelming. The feeling you have standing at the edge of a cliff over the sea. Looking at the stars on a clear night in the mountains. Seeing something so big and so beautiful that your mind cannot hold it, and you forget your own name.

*Awe.*

And the man on the stage was pouring it out. Into them. Forty people kneeling on blue carpet, filled to the brim with awe at a painted ceiling, so full of it that there was no room left in them for anything else. Not their families. Not their jobs. Not food. Not sleep. Not the nine people in the basement. Only the sky.

The man smiled. His pupils were huge. Black. Wet. Shining. Taking in all forty of them at once, like a farmer looking at a field.

"*He's riding it,*" whispered Hana, beside Rei. She had her sleeves pulled down over her hands. Her eyes were narrow and fast and moving round the room, reading it, the way she read everything. "*A demon of Awe. In the man. It's not even hiding. It doesn't need to. They'd die for it. Some of them already have.*"

Rei looked at her.

"*There,*" said Hana. "*Back of the room. The door.*"

[break The door]

At the back of the hall, behind the last row of kneeling people, there was a narrow door. Plain, wooden, painted the same blue as the ceiling. It led down. Rei could feel it. Stairs going down to a basement under the pachinko parlour, under the noise. Nine people down there. Nine small, faint, flickering lights, very weak, very cold.

The sill of the door was dusted with dark earth. Grave earth. And lying across the threshold, coiled on the earth, was a snake.

It was an ordinary snake. A Japanese rat snake, long and brown and olive, as thick as Rei's wrist, the kind that lives in old farmhouses and eats mice. It lay with its head raised a little off its coils and its tongue flickering, and it was not looking at any of them. It was watching the empty air in the doorway, very closely, as if the empty air were the most interesting thing in Tokyo.

Rei knew what it was. He had read about it in the archive. *Set a snake at the threshold. It bites what it cannot see.* A snake that feels the veil. Laid on grave earth across a door, it lets ordinary people walk past. A man in socks. A woman with a tray. It does not care about them. It strikes at anything that crosses which the eye cannot see. A spirit. A thing from the far side. A practitioner with the door in his chest open, pushing his sight ahead of him into the dark.

The thing on the stage had not put it there to stop the police. It had put it there to stop people like him.

"*Your gate,*" breathed Hana, beside him. "*Don't even think about it. You open a door through that doorway and it'll have your hand.*"

"*I know,*" whispered Itsuki.

"*And you,*" said Hana, to Rei. "*You've got your door open. I can feel it from here. You walk past that thing like that and it'll bite you in the leg, and whatever's on the stage will feel it like a bell.*"

Rei looked at the snake. At its small black ordinary eyes, watching the air.

"Then I'll close it," he said.

[break Blind]

On the stage, the man raised his arms.

"Look up!" he cried. "Look up at the sky!"

Forty people looked up. Forty people wept.

Rei put his right hand on his left wrist and dropped the latch.

Not to a finger's width. All the way. Shut. The door in his chest swung closed and the iron bar fell into the hook, and for the first time since August he was not open to the veil at all. Not even a crack.

It was like going deaf. Like going blind. The hall went flat and ordinary and grey. The great bright wash of awe from the stage was gone. The nine faint lights in the basement were gone. He could not feel Hana beside him or Itsuki behind him or the thing on the stage. He was a boy in a borrowed white shirt in a shabby room above a pachinko parlour, and that was all he was, and he did not know where anything was.

He had not realised how much he had come to rely on it. How frightening the world was without it.

Hana walked first. She did it the way you do it on a crowded train, quietly, with her shoulders down, a girl in white going to the toilet. She stepped over the sill. The snake did not move. Its tongue flickered at the air beside her ankle and found nothing it cared about.

Rei followed.

He did not look at the snake. He looked at the back of Hana's head. He kept the latch shut so hard that his wrist ached. He walked like a man walking on ice, one foot, then the other, and he felt his heel come down on the grave earth on the sill, soft and gritty, and he did not breathe.

Something brushed his ankle. Cool and dry. The snake's tongue.

He kept walking. He was through. He was on the stairs. The blue door was behind him.

Behind him, in the hall, the snake lowered its head onto its coils and went on watching the empty air.

[break The basement]

The stairs went down into concrete and cold. At the bottom there was one bare bulb. The noise of the pachinko parlour was very loud down here, pounding through the ceiling. On the floor, in a row, on thin white mats, lay nine people.

Rei lifted the latch.

It came back all at once, the way hearing comes back after a loud noise. The cold. The thin place under the floor, worn through, like a hole in a sock. And the nine. Eight small, weak, flickering lights. And one that was not flickering any more.

They were thin. Very thin. Weeks thin. They wore white clothes gone grey with dirt. They lay on their backs with their eyes open, looking up at the concrete ceiling, at nothing. All of them were smiling. Soft, sweet, peaceful smiles. Awe. The demon had filled them with awe and then left them down here in the dark, over the thin place, to be drunk from, slowly, like the old people on the Arakawa.

Hana knelt beside the one who was not breathing. A young man, about twenty, with long hair. She put two fingers on his neck. She stayed there a long time. Then she took her fingers away and pulled her sleeve down over her hand and closed his eyes with it. She did not say anything. Her face did not change.

"Eight," she said. "Get them up."

Rei took out his phone and sent one word to the number upstairs.

*now*

The basement had one small high window, at street level, with bars on it and the wet black alley behind. In the alley, Itsuki was waiting. Rei felt him put his hand on the outside wall. Felt him think of a bridge. Felt the wall under the window go soft, like a road in the heat, and open.

Not through the threshold. Not past the snake. Through the side of the building, from the alley straight into the basement, with nothing in between.

Cold wet air came in, smelling of rain and petrol.

They carried them out through Itsuki's door. One at a time. Rei and Hana with an arm each, walking them through, into the alley, where Itsuki stood with his palm flat on the wall and his face white and his nose starting to bleed, and did not let go until the eighth one was through.

[break The sky]

Upstairs, the man on the stage was screaming. Rei heard it through the ceiling, over the pachinko noise.

Not in his own voice. In the demon's. A high, wild, joyful shriek, like a child on a rollercoaster. It had felt the cold air from the alley. It had felt the door in the wall. It had felt eight of its herd go out of its reach at once. And it did what demons do. It did not think. It wanted, and it moved.

It wanted Rei.

It came down the stairs. Rei heard the blue door bang open and the man's heavy body come down the concrete steps three at a time, past the snake, which did not care about a man in a white robe. It came out of the stairwell into the basement in one leap, white robe flying, arms out, mouth open. Rei saw it coming. He saw the black eyes. He felt the awe pour out of it toward him like a wave, huge and bright and overwhelming, *look up, look up, look at the sky, forget everything, forget your brother, forget your name*.

He put his hand on his wrist.

He did not lift the latch. He held it. He felt the wave hit the closed door in his chest and break on it, like the sea on a wall.

And Gorō came through the wall.

Through Itsuki's door, from the wet alley, a big black and white crow with one pale blue eye came in at full speed, low over the mats, and hit the man in the face with both feet and his heavy beak, screaming *kaa kaa kaa* like an alarm. The man fell. He hit the concrete hard, on his back, with the crow on his chest, pecking.

Hana was there. She had a handful of iron filings. She pressed them flat against the man's forehead with her palm and held them there.

The demon came out of him like steam out of a kettle. A shriek. A rush of something bright and enormous and empty, going up, up, through the concrete and the pachinko parlour and the badly painted sky in the hall above, and away.

The man lay on the concrete with a crow on his chest and iron on his forehead and looked up at the bare bulb. His eyes were small and brown and ordinary.

"Oh," he said, in a thin, ordinary, frightened voice. "Oh, no. What have I done. What have I done."

[break After]

The Hall of the Bright Sky ended that night. Not with the Division. With forty people in white clothes standing in a shabby room above a pachinko parlour, looking at each other, looking out of the windows at the eight thin people sitting in the wet alley below, looking at the young man with long hair lying very still on a mat by the stage door. Waking up.

Some of them cried. Some of them walked out without a word and went home. Some of them stayed, and sat with the eight, and held their hands, and called ambulances, and called families.

The man in the white robe sat on the basement stairs with his head in his hands until the police came. He did not try to run. He kept saying the same thing. *I only wanted to show them something beautiful. I only wanted to show them something beautiful.*

The rat snake was still coiled on its grave earth across the blue door, guarding nothing now. Hana put it in a cardboard box to take to someone in the Grid who kept animals. "It did its job," she said. "It's not its fault the job was stupid."

Outside, in the rain, Itsuki sat on the kerb with a towel against his bleeding nose. Hana stood beside him, smoking.

"You held that door for nine minutes," she said. "With nine people going through it."

"Eight," said Itsuki.

"Eight." She looked at the cigarette. "You're good. For a Kuroda."

"Thank you," said Itsuki. "That's the nicest thing a Grid person has ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

Rei sat on the kerb on Itsuki's other side. Gorō landed on his shoulder, wet and furious and covered in tiny cuts from the glass, and pressed his head against Rei's ear.

"Kaa," he said quietly.

"I know," said Rei. "I know. Thank you."

The crow did not say *never again*. He did not say anything else at all. He just stayed there, on Rei's shoulder, in the rain, until the ambulances had gone.
