#title The Snake at the Door
#part Book Three. The Thirteen

It was not the Circle. That was the first thing Switch said.

"Different people. Different thing. Smaller. Newer." They were standing in the doorway of a closed launderette in Akabane, the Grid's message point that week, at two in the morning, with the rain coming down outside. "It calls itself the Hall of the Bright Sky. A man in Itabashi. Rents a hall above a pachinko parlour. Started a year ago with five people. Now there's forty." Switch's square face did not move. "Nine of them haven't been seen outside in a month. Their families come to the door. They're told their children are *in purification*. In the basement."

"Why us?" said Rei. "Why not the Division?"

"The Division went," said Switch. "In April. Two officers. They came out smiling and filed a report that said *legitimate religious organisation, no veil activity*." They looked at him. "One of the officers was from the third floor. You might know him. He's in Reintegration now. He still smiles. He hasn't stopped."

[break The keeper]

The animals came from a shop under the railway tracks in Akabane, a narrow dark place full of tanks and cages and the smell of sawdust and damp, with a sign over the door that said only PETS. The old woman who ran it was the Grid's animal keeper. She had birds and cats and dogs and a tank of goldfish that, she said, were very good at finding drowned things. She did not tell them her name.

She gave them two animals in a cloth bag.

The first was a snake. Long and thin and silvery green, as long as Rei's arm, with small bright black eyes and a flickering forked tongue. It wound itself round Rei's wrist the moment he held out his hand, over the latch, and lay there, cool and dry and still.

"Mizuchi," said the old woman. "She finds thin places. Better than any instrument. Where the veil wears thin, she goes still. And she has a trick." She almost smiled. "When she's frightened, or when she's told to, she dies."

"Dies?"

"Pretends to. Goes stiff. Rolls over. Tongue out. Heart so slow you can't find it. Smells dead, even." The old woman stroked the snake's head with one finger. "Most things leave a dead snake alone. Even other snakes."

The second animal was a frog. A big, fat, brown and green frog, the size of Rei's fist, with gold eyes and a throat that went in and out, in and out, very slowly. It sat in the palm of Itsuki's hand, looking up at him with an expression of total calm.

"Kawazu," said the old woman. "He eases things. Heavy minds. People who've seen too much. Sit with him for an hour and the noise in your head goes quieter." She looked at Itsuki for a long moment, and at the switches between his knuckles, and nodded. "And he's very loud when he wants to be. Loud in the veil, I mean. When he calls, everything that's watching turns to look at him. Even things that are watching through other eyes."

Itsuki looked down at the frog. The frog looked up at Itsuki.

"I'm not using you as bait," Itsuki told it. "That's horrible. I won't do it."

"Kaa," said Gorō, from the top of a birdcage, with contempt.

[break The hall]

[need ch41_bright_sky | A shabby upstairs hall above a neon pachinko parlour in Itabashi at night, rows of followers in white kneeling on cheap carpet facing a stage where a smiling middle-aged man in white stands under a painted blue sky on the ceiling, a narrow blue door at the back with a brown rat snake coiled on dark grave earth across its threshold, rats sitting still along the top of the walls]

The Hall of the Bright Sky was on the second floor of a building in Itabashi, above a pachinko parlour so loud that the floor shook. A pachinko parlour is a kind of gambling hall, full of noisy upright machines like pinball, where people sit for hours feeding steel balls into the machines and watching them bounce. The noise of ten thousand steel balls came up through the floorboards like rain on a tin roof.

The hall itself was one big room with cheap blue carpet and folding chairs pushed against the walls. Someone had painted the whole ceiling sky blue, with white clouds, badly. At the front was a low stage. On the stage stood a man.

He was about fifty. Ordinary. A little heavy, with thinning hair and a round, pleasant, smiling face, in a white robe like a bathrobe tied at the waist. He was talking. Forty people in white clothes knelt on the blue carpet in front of him, in rows, looking up at him with their faces shining.

"The sky," he was saying. "Look up. Look up at the sky. Isn't it beautiful? Isn't it the most beautiful thing you've ever seen?"

Forty faces looked up at the badly painted ceiling. Forty mouths opened. Forty pairs of eyes filled with tears.

Rei, at the back of the room in a borrowed white shirt, kneeling between Itsuki and Hana, opened the door in his chest one finger.

He felt it at once. A great wash of something, coming off the man on the stage like heat off a fire. Not fear. Not hunger. Something huge and bright and overwhelming. The feeling you have standing at the edge of a cliff over the sea. Looking at the stars on a clear night in the mountains. Seeing something so big and so beautiful that your mind cannot hold it, and you forget your own name.

*Awe.*

And the man on the stage was pouring it out. Into them. Forty people kneeling on blue carpet, filled to the brim with awe at a painted ceiling, so full of it that there was no room left in them for anything else. Not their families. Not their jobs. Not food. Not sleep. Not the nine people in the basement. Only the sky.

The man smiled. His pupils were huge. Black. Wet. Shining. Taking in all forty of them at once, like a farmer looking at a field.

"*He's riding it,*" whispered Hana, beside Rei. She had her sleeves pulled down over her hands. Her eyes were narrow and fast and moving round the room, reading it, the way she read everything. "*A demon of Awe. In the man. It's not even hiding. It doesn't need to. They'd die for it. Some of them already have.*"

Rei looked at her.

"*There,*" said Hana. "*Back of the room. The door.*"

[break The door]

At the back of the hall, behind the last row of kneeling people, there was a narrow door. Plain, wooden, painted the same blue as the ceiling. It led down. Rei could feel it. Stairs going down to a basement under the pachinko parlour, under the noise. Nine people down there. Nine small, faint, flickering lights, very weak, very cold.

The sill of the door was dusted with dark earth. Grave earth. And lying across the threshold, coiled on the earth, was a snake.

It was an ordinary snake. A Japanese rat snake, long and brown and olive, as thick as Rei's wrist, the kind that lives in old farmhouses and eats mice. It lay with its head raised a little off its coils and its tongue flickering, and it was not looking at any of them. It was watching the empty air in the doorway, very closely, as if the empty air were the most interesting thing in Tokyo.

Rei knew what it was. He had read about it in the archive. *Set a snake at the threshold. It bites what it cannot see.* A snake that feels the veil. Laid on grave earth across a door, it strikes at anything that crosses which the eye cannot. Spirits. Things from the far side. Anything that comes through a door without walking.

"*Oh, no,*" whispered Itsuki.

"*Yeah,*" breathed Hana. "*Your gate. You open a door through that doorway and that snake bites your hand off.*" She was reading the room, fast. "*And that's not all. Look at the walls.*"

Rei looked. Along the tops of the walls, along the edge of the painted sky, in the gaps where the cheap ceiling tiles did not quite meet, there were rats. A dozen. Two dozen. Sitting very still. All facing the room. All watching the kneeling people, and the stage, and the door.

"*It's got eyes everywhere,*" said Hana. "*If we move, it sees.*"

On the stage, the man raised his arms.

"Look up!" he cried. "Look up at the sky!"

Forty people looked up. Forty people wept.

"Okay," said Rei quietly. "The frog takes the eyes. The snake takes the bite. Itsuki takes the door."

[break The trick]

Itsuki crouched at the end of the last row and put his hand down on the carpet. He had promised not to use Kawazu as bait. He had sat in the car outside for twenty minutes with the frog in his palm, talking to him, very quietly, very seriously, and Rei had not heard what he said. Now Kawazu hopped off his hand, once, twice, toward the far side of the hall, away from the door, toward the stage.

Then the frog sat down on the blue carpet, and filled his throat, and croaked.

It was the loudest sound Rei had ever heard come out of anything so small. Deep and booming and wet, like a drum in a cave. And it was not only a sound. Rei felt it, with the door in his chest open a finger's width. Something went out of the frog with the croak, a pulse, a smell, a signature, bright and loud and alive, like a firework in the veil.

Every rat on every wall turned to look at it.

All of them. At once. Two dozen small heads swinging round toward the frog by the stage. The thing behind them, the thing riding the man, looked where its eyes looked. For a moment, the back of the hall was not being watched at all.

"Now," said Rei.

He slid back his sleeve. Mizuchi uncoiled from his wrist and slid down his hand onto the carpet, silvery green, low and fast, to the foot of the blue door, and stopped at the edge of the grave earth.

Itsuki put his palm flat on the wall beside the door, not on the door itself, and closed his eyes, and opened his gate. Just a crack. Just enough. A door through the doorway into the basement, with nothing in between.

Mizuchi went through it first.

The rat snake struck. Fast as a whip. Its head shot forward at the invisible crossing, at the thing coming through a door without walking, and its jaws closed on Mizuchi's silver-green body in the gap.

Mizuchi died.

She went stiff in its jaws. She rolled. Her pale belly showed. Her mouth fell open and her tongue hung out, limp. Her heart slowed until there was nothing to feel. She smelled, suddenly, of old dead snake.

The rat snake let go. It drew back onto its coils, slowly. It had struck. It had killed. Its tongue flickered over the limp silver body on the sill, tasted death, and lost interest. It lowered its head onto its coils and went still, the way a snake goes still after a strike, satisfied, finished, for a few long seconds not watching anything at all.

"*Go,*" said Hana.

Itsuki threw his gate wide.

[break The basement]

Itsuki was already at it. He put his palm flat on the blue door, and closed his eyes, and the door did not open onto stairs. It opened onto the basement itself. Straight in. No stairs at all. A door from the back of the hall to the bottom of the building, with nothing in between.

Rei and Hana went through. Itsuki held it.

The basement was concrete and very cold and lit by one bare bulb. The noise of the pachinko parlour was very loud down here, pounding through the ceiling. On the floor, in a row, on thin white mats, lay nine people.

They were thin. Very thin. Weeks thin. They wore white clothes gone grey with dirt. They lay on their backs with their eyes open, looking up at the concrete ceiling, at nothing. All nine of them were smiling. Soft, sweet, peaceful smiles. Awe. The demon had filled them with awe and then left them down here in the dark, under the thin place, to be drunk from, slowly, like the old people on the Arakawa.

One of them was not breathing.

Hana knelt beside that one. A young man, about twenty, with long hair. She put two fingers on his neck. She stayed there a long time. Then she took her fingers away and pulled her sleeve down over her hand and closed his eyes with it. She did not say anything. Her face did not change.

"Eight," she said. "Get them up."

They carried them out through Itsuki's door. One at a time. Rei and Hana with an arm each, walking them through, into the back of the hall, where the kneeling people had begun to stir and murmur and look round, confused, like people waking up in a cinema when the lights come on. Itsuki held the door with his hand flat on the frame and his face white and his nose starting to bleed, and did not let go until the eighth one was through.

[break The sky]

The man on the stage was screaming.

Not in his own voice. In the demon's. A high, wild, joyful shriek, like a child on a rollercoaster. It had seen them. It had seen the people coming out of the basement. It had seen the frog by the stage, and turned back too late. It had seen everything at once, with its huge black eyes, and it did what demons do. It did not think. It wanted, and it moved.

It wanted Rei.

It came off the stage in one leap. The man's heavy body went through the air like something thrown, white robe flying, arms out, mouth open. Forty people screamed and scattered. Rei saw it coming. He saw the black eyes. He felt the awe pour out of it toward him like a wave, huge and bright and overwhelming, *look up, look up, look at the sky, forget everything, forget your brother, forget your name*.

He put his hand on his wrist.

He did not lift the latch. He held it. He felt the wave hit the closed door in his chest and break on it, like the sea on a wall.

And Gorō came through the window.

Rei never knew how. The window was shut. There was glass everywhere suddenly, falling like rain onto the blue carpet, and a big black crow with a ragged wing feather came through it at full speed, and hit the man in the face with both feet and his heavy beak, screaming *kaa kaa kaa* like an alarm. The man fell. He hit the carpet hard, on his back, with the crow on his chest, pecking.

Hana was there. She had a handful of iron filings. She pressed them flat against the man's forehead with her palm and held them there.

The demon came out of him like steam out of a kettle. A shriek. A rush of something bright and enormous and empty, going up, up, through the badly painted sky on the ceiling, through the roof, and away.

The man lay on the blue carpet with a crow on his chest and iron on his forehead and looked up at the ceiling. At the painted sky. His eyes were small and brown and ordinary.

"Oh," he said, in a thin, ordinary, frightened voice. "Oh, no. What have I done. What have I done."

[break After]

The Hall of the Bright Sky ended that night. Not with the Division. With forty people in white clothes standing in a shabby room above a pachinko parlour, looking at each other, looking at the eight thin people sitting against the wall, looking at the young man with long hair lying very still on a mat by the stage door. Waking up.

Some of them cried. Some of them walked out without a word and went home. Some of them stayed, and sat with the eight, and held their hands, and called ambulances, and called families.

The man in the white robe sat on the edge of his stage with his head in his hands until the police came. He did not try to run. He kept saying the same thing. *I only wanted to show them something beautiful. I only wanted to show them something beautiful.*

Kawazu came out from under the stage when it was over and sat on Itsuki's shoe. The rat snake was still coiled on its grave earth across the blue door, guarding nothing now. Hana put it in a cardboard box to take to the keeper under the tracks. Mizuchi, who had lain dead on the sill through the whole thing, uncoiled herself, very much alive, and slid back up Rei's arm and round his wrist, over the latch, and lay still.

Outside, in the rain, Itsuki sat on the kerb with a towel against his bleeding nose and the frog in his lap. Hana stood beside him, smoking.

"You held that door for nine minutes," she said. "With nine people going through it."

"Eight," said Itsuki.

"Eight." She looked at the cigarette. "You're good. For a Kuroda."

"Thank you," said Itsuki. "That's the nicest thing a Grid person has ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

Rei sat on the kerb on Itsuki's other side. Gorō landed on his shoulder, wet and furious and covered in tiny cuts from the glass, and pressed his head against Rei's ear.

"Kaa," he said quietly.

"I know," said Rei. "I know. Thank you."

The crow did not say *never again*. He did not say anything else at all. He just stayed there, on Rei's shoulder, in the rain, until the ambulances had gone.
