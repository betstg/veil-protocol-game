#title Inside the Temple
#part Book Three. The Thirteen

Every candle in the hall lit at once.

Not just lit. Flared. A hundred small flames jumped up together, each one as tall as a hand, bright yellow, roaring softly, so that the whole hall was suddenly full of hot gold light and leaping shadows. The gold Buddha on the altar blazed. The black tablets with the names of the dead shone. And in the middle of it all, by the door, Tendo Akira sat with his legs crossed and his fingers still raised from the snap, smiling.

"Lights," said Tendo, a little out of breath. "As requested. Sorry about the eyebrows, Reverend."

Rei did not have time to think about it.

[break The thing in the priest]

Reverend Egami was kneeling where he had been kneeling, by the brazier, with his hands round his can of beer. But he was not Egami any more.

His face had gone soft. All of it. Every line in it, the deep lines of forty years of listening to other people's sorrow, had smoothed out, the way a sheet smooths when you pull it tight. He looked younger. He looked peaceful. His mouth was turned up very slightly at the corners in a small, sweet, sleepy smile, the smile of a child who has just been given something warm to drink. And his eyes were huge and black and wet, and they moved over the five of them slowly, one after another, the way a man at a buffet looks over the dishes.

"*So much,*" it said. In the priest's voice, but slow and soft and dreamy, with long pauses between the words, as if it were speaking from inside a deep sleep. "*So much grief. In all of you. You've all brought me so much.*" The black eyes stopped on Aoi. "*You. The little one with the beads. You lost someone. A grandmother. Last spring. You never cried for her. You were too busy being good.*" They moved to Kato. "*You. So lonely. All those voices and nobody to talk to.*" They moved to Rei. And the sweet sleepy smile grew wider.

"*And you,*" it said. "*Oh. You. You're nothing but a hole where your brother should be.*"

"Salt," said Shimizu. Very quiet. Very fast. "Uedera. Ring him. Now. Nakamura, seals on the doors. Kato, where will it go?"

"Out of him," said Kato instantly. "It's thinking about the tablets. All of them. Hundreds of doors. It wants to go into the names."

Rei was already moving. He tore open the salt and ran it in a circle round the kneeling priest, on the old tatami, fast, the way he had done it in Mrs. Tachibana's flat. The thing in Egami watched him do it. It did not move. It only smiled, sleepily, and when the circle closed, it laughed. The low breathless laugh.

"*Salt,*" it said. "*Salt is for little ghosts. Salt is for grandmothers in shopping arcades.*"

And it went out of him.

[break The names]

Rei saw it go. A grey shape like smoke, like ash blown off a fire, poured up out of Egami's open mouth and over the salt line as if the line were not there. Egami fell sideways onto the mat and lay still. The grey shape went across the hall in a long low rush, toward the altar, toward the rows of black tablets with the names of the dead in gold.

Aoi was there first.

She was on her knees by the altar with her hands full of white paper seals, the long thin strips covered in black writing that she had carried in her bag. She did not throw them. She opened her hands, and the seals rose up out of them on their own, a dozen strips of paper lifting into the air like birds, and flew. They flew to the rows of tablets and slapped flat against them, one after another, across the front of every row, *slap slap slap*, pinned there by nothing Rei could see. Aoi's face was white with effort. Her bead bracelet had broken and the beads were rolling across the floor.

The grey shape hit the seals and stopped. It hung in the air in front of the altar, swirling, like smoke against glass.

"*Clever,*" it said, from everywhere at once. "*Clever little one. Hiding how strong you are. Just like him.*"

And outside, in the dark yard, the bell stopped ringing.

The silence was terrible.

Shimizu's head turned toward the door. Rei saw her face. He saw the line between her eyebrows go suddenly very deep, deeper than he had ever seen it. He saw her see something. Five things. Six. And choose.

"Nakamura," she said. "*Move.*"

Aoi looked up.

The roof of the main hall came in.

[break The bell]

It was not the roof. Rei understood that later. It was the bell.

The thing had gone out of the hall through the wall and into the bell tower, into the great bronze bell it had been ringing every dusk for four months, the bell that a hundred years of grief had soaked into. It filled the bell. And it pulled. And the thick old beam the bell hung from, which had held it for a hundred years, split down the middle with a crack like a gunshot.

The bell came through the side wall of the hall. A tonne of bronze, swinging on its broken rope, tearing through the old wooden boards and the paper screens like a fist through a paper bag. It swung across the end of the hall in a great low arc, exactly where Aoi was kneeling by the altar.

Aoi moved. Not fast enough. She threw up both hands, and Rei felt it, the whole hall felt it, a huge invisible push, Aoi's whole strength, everything she had hidden for two years from every instrument in the building, thrown against a tonne of swinging bronze.

It slowed the bell. It did not stop it.

The rim of the bell caught her across the legs.

The sound it made was the worst sound Rei had ever heard. Worse than the train on the platform. Worse than Itsuki's ribs. A deep, wet, crunching sound, and then the bell crashing down on its side on the floor beside the altar with a boom that shook the whole building, and rolling, slowly, to a stop, rocking.

Aoi was lying on the floor. Her left leg was wrong. Very wrong. Bent at the knee in a direction knees do not go, and below the knee, through the torn cloth of her trousers, something white was showing. And blood. A lot of blood. Very fast.

"*Femoral,*" said Kato.

He said it in a strange flat voice. Rei looked at him. Kato was already moving, already sliding across the floor on his knees through the scattered beads, already at Aoi's side. He did not hesitate. He put his knee into the top of her thigh, high up, near the hip, with all his weight, and pressed.

Aoi screamed.

"I know," said Kato. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm not stopping." His face was white and his voice was shaking and his knee did not move. "I'm not stopping. I'm not stopping. Aoi. Look at me. Look at me. Think something at me. Anything. Think at me."

Aoi stared up at him with huge wet eyes. Her lips moved. Kato laughed, a cracked high laugh with tears in it.

"Yes, I know my jokes are bad," he said. "Keep thinking. Keep thinking at me."

[break The true ring]

The bell lay on its side by the altar, rocking slowly. The grey thing was inside it. Rei could feel it in there, coiled in the bronze, furious, hungry, drinking the grief that had soaked into the metal for a hundred years. Drinking Aoi's blood on the floor. Drinking the fear in the room.

"It's in the bell," he said. "It lives in the grief in the bell. That's its door."

"Then we take the door away," said Shimizu. She was kneeling by Egami, who was stirring, groaning, his face lined again, his own. "Tendo. Can you burn it?"

"Burn a tonne of bronze?" Tendo was standing in the middle of the hall with the hundred candle flames roaring round him. He was not smiling now. His face was white with effort and set and serious. "I can melt it. Give me a minute. It'll be loud."

"No," said Rei.

They all looked at him.

He was looking at Egami. The old priest had pushed himself up on one elbow, on the tatami, inside the broken salt ring. He was staring at the bell. At Aoi on the floor. At the blood. At the hole in the wall of his temple. His face was grey with understanding.

"It was me," Egami whispered. "It was in me. All winter. It was in me while I prayed."

"Yes," said Rei. "It came in through your guilt. Through the boy. It's been drinking from you and everyone who prayed beside you. That's how it lives." He knelt down in front of the priest, inside the salt. "If Tendo melts the bell, it'll go somewhere else. Into the tablets. Into the ground. Into you again. It'll find the guilt and go back in. It's an impulse, Reverend. It wants, and it takes. It doesn't care what door."

"Then what..."

"You have to close it. The door." Rei swallowed. "The bell's for sending. That's what temple bells are for, isn't it? At New Year. At funerals. At Obon. To send things on. You ring it to say goodbye." He looked at the old man. "You've been praying for the boy every evening for four months. Asking him to forgive you. Holding on. You haven't sent him."

Egami stared at him.

"Ring it," said Rei. "Properly. Yourself. And let him go."

[break The sending]

The wooden log for striking the bell had fallen with it. It lay on the floor among the broken boards, a thick, heavy, smooth beam on two broken ropes. Egami got to his feet. He swayed. Shimizu caught his arm. He shook her off, gently, and walked to the log, and bent, and picked up one end of it. It was too heavy for an old man. He lifted it anyway.

Rei took the other end.

They stood together by the bell lying on its side, the old priest and the boy in the cheap suit, holding the heavy wooden beam between them. In the bell, the grey thing went still. Rei felt it go still. Felt it understand, the way an animal understands when someone picks up a stick.

Egami looked at the black tablet at the end of the third row. A new one. The gold of the name was still bright.

"Shōta," he said. His voice was steady now. "Shōta. I am sorry I didn't call someone. I am sorry I thought I was enough. I wasn't enough. Nobody is enough on their own." He took a breath. "You're not mine to hold any more. Go home."

They swung the log together.

It hit the rim of the bell, and the bell rang.

Not like before. Not the deep endless booming of the thing inside it. A clean, clear, ringing note, high and pure and very loud, like a bell is supposed to ring. It filled the hall. It went out through the hole in the wall and over the graveyard and the roofs of Takinogawa and up into the dark sky. Rei felt it go through him. Through the floor and the walls and the candles and the tablets. Felt it go through the bronze, through every year of grief that had soaked into it, and shake it loose, the way you shake dust out of a rug.

The grey thing came out of the bell screaming.

It came out in a long grey shriek of ash and smoke, shaken out of its door, with nowhere to go. It went for the tablets, and hit Aoi's seals, and bounced off. It went for Egami, and Egami was standing straight with the log in his hands and his face wet and his eyes his own, and there was no door in him any more. It went for Rei.

Rei put his hand on his wrist. On the latch.

And did not open it.

"Tendo," said Shimizu.

Tendo raised his hand and closed his fist.

Every one of the hundred candle flames in the hall left its candle at the same time. They flew across the hall like a flock of burning birds and hit the grey thing from every side at once, and it burned. Not yellow. White. A white so bright that Rei had to shut his eyes, and still saw it through his eyelids. A sound like a sheet of paper torn in half, very slowly. A smell like burnt hair and incense.

Then dark. Real dark. Every candle out.

And silence.

[break After]

The Division medics came in twenty minutes. Kato did not take his knee off Aoi's leg until they made him. When they lifted him away, he could not stand up. He sat on the floor of the temple with Aoi's blood on his trousers to the knee and shook from head to foot, and Rei sat next to him, and Kuma came in from the yard and lay down across both their feet.

Aoi would live. She would keep the leg. Probably. The surgeons would know in a week. Three months before she walked properly. Maybe more.

"She thought at me the whole time," said Kato, in the car, much later. "The whole time I was pressing. Do you know what she was thinking?" He laughed, shakily. "*Your jokes are terrible. Your jokes are terrible. Your jokes are terrible.* Over and over. For eighteen minutes." He put his head back against the seat. "Best thing anyone ever thought at me."

In the front, Tendo was driving with one hand. The jazz was off. He had carried Aoi down the temple steps himself, to the ambulance, very carefully, talking to her the whole way, and there was still blood on his beautiful suit. He had not said very much since. When they stopped at a red light, Rei looked at him in the mirror.

Tendo was looking back at him. His face was tired and his eyes were red.

"You didn't open your door," he said quietly. "When it came at you. Most people would have panicked. You held it shut." He looked back at the road. "I've never been able to do that. Hold anything back. I just burn." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm glad you're on the team, Rei. I mean it. I'm glad it's you."

The light went green.

Beside Rei, Kato was asleep against the window, with Aoi's broken bracelet beads in his fist. He had picked up every one.
