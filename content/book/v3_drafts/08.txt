#title Grid Money
#part Book One. The Archive

On Tuesday the twenty-fifth of August, Gin's salary did not arrive.

Rei checked the bank app at six in the morning, and at nine, and at noon. At one o'clock he walked to the bank branch by Ōji station and stood in a queue behind a grandmother paying her gas bill in coins. When he got to the window, a young woman in a neat uniform typed for a long time and then turned her screen a little so he could not see it.

"I'm sorry," she said. "This account is under administrative review."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the account holder's employer has asked us to hold incoming payments until a review is finished." She smiled the way people smile when they are not allowed to say any more. "It's usually a matter of paperwork."

"Who asked? The CVD?"

"I'm sorry, I can't say." She slid a printed paper across the counter. It had a phone number for an office called *Personnel Administration, Civil Veil Division.* "You could try this number."

He did not try the number. He knew what the voice would say. He walked out into the rain with the paper in his hand and stood under the awning of a shoe shop and did the sums again on the back of it.

He had thirty-one thousand yen in his own account. His internship would pay him eighteen thousand at the end of the month. The rent was eighty-four thousand, due on Thursday.

It was not even close.

[break Ni-chōme]

Shinjuku is the busiest part of Tokyo. Its station is the busiest station in the world, with more than three million people passing through it every day, more than the whole population of some countries. To the east of the station is a grid of narrow streets called Ni-chōme. In the daytime it is quiet, just small offices and closed bars and a few cafés. At night it fills up. Ni-chōme is where gay men and women in Tokyo have gone for fifty years to find bars where nobody will look twice at them, where they can be who they are for an evening. There are more than four hundred bars in a few streets, most of them so small that eight people make a crowd.

Rei came up out of the subway at seven in the evening. The rain had stopped. The streets were wet and shining, full of red and pink and green reflections. Two men in office suits walked past holding hands and laughing. A tall woman in a sequined dress stood smoking in a doorway, talking on her phone in a voice like gravel. Somewhere upstairs someone was singing karaoke, a sad old love song, very loudly and very well.

Café Asahi was on a corner, on the ground floor of a narrow building with a staircase up the side. It was not a bar. It was an old-fashioned coffee shop, the kind called a kissaten, with red leather seats, a glass case of plastic models of the food, net curtains, and a counter of dark wood. It smelled of toast and coffee and cigarette smoke. Most of the seats were empty.

A woman in her fifties stood behind the counter in a white apron. She had short grey hair, steady hands and a quiet, square face. She looked up when Rei came in, and he had the strangest feeling. It was as if she had looked at him and read him all the way through in one second, the way you read the front page of a newspaper, and then folded him up and put him down.

He sat at the counter. He did not know what to say. "The usual," he said. "Please."

She did not ask what usual. She turned, and made a coffee, a dark one in a thick white cup, and put it in front of him with a small plate. On the plate was a slice of thick white toast with butter and a little dish of red bean paste. Next to the plate she put a folded square of paper.

"He came on Tuesdays," she said. Her voice was low and slow. "For three years. He never ordered. I never asked. He paid for a coffee he didn't order, every Tuesday." She looked at the empty stool beside Rei. "I was starting to think it was the most efficient arrangement either of us had ever had."

"I'm his brother."

"I know." She went back to drying a glass. "You have his way of sitting. Like you're ready to leave."

Rei unfolded the square of paper. It was an address in Ni-chōme, two streets away. A bar called Lilac. A name, Mama Sachi. And a number. 40,000.

"Work," said Matsuda Keiko, without looking up from the glass. "Sachi has a problem. She'll pay cash. She doesn't care who you are as long as you fix it." She put the glass down and picked up another. "Haruki told me about the account. The rent. He thought you'd be too proud to say."

"I would have been," Rei admitted.

"Your brother was too proud as well." She almost smiled. "This is how the Grid works. Someone knows someone who has a problem. Someone else can fix it. Money changes hands. Nobody writes anything down. Nobody gives anybody a number on a file." She looked at him properly. "Do it well, and there'll be more. Do it badly, and there won't. That's all."

He ate the toast. It was the best toast he had ever eaten, thick and soft with crunchy edges, the butter melting into the red bean. He had not realised how hungry he was.

When he went to pay, she shook her head. "It's paid," she said. "He paid for this Tuesday last week. He always paid one week ahead."

[break The list]

Before he left, she gave him one more piece of paper. It was a short list in pencil, in handwriting he knew.

*Basic kit. Coarse salt, one kilo. Iron filings, cast iron only, NOT steel, a jar. White chalk, the soft kind. Red cotton thread. A small torch. Something to eat that is warm.*

"He left that with me two years ago," said Keiko. "He said, if Rei ever comes in here, give him this. I asked him why you would ever come in here." She shrugged. "He didn't answer."

Rei folded the list very carefully and put it in his wallet next to Okada's card.

He bought the kit on the way to the bar. The salt and the chalk and the torch from a big discount store that sold everything from toilet paper to party costumes, with music blaring and shelves up to the ceiling. The thread from a tiny sewing shop run by an old man who was closing up and sold it to him with the shutter half down. The iron filings were harder. He found them in the end in a hardware store under the train tracks, in a small jar meant for science experiments, for four hundred yen. The archive said why it had to be cast iron. Steel is made in a way that burns the old thing out of the metal. Cast iron still has it. Rei did not understand what *it* was. He bought two jars.

His backpack was heavy now. It rattled a little when he walked. It was the first time since Gin left that he had felt like he was carrying something useful, and not just carrying something.

[break Lilac]

Lilac was on the second floor of a narrow building, up a staircase so steep it was almost a ladder. It was one room with a counter and seven stools and a karaoke machine in the corner. The walls were painted dark purple. Behind the counter stood a large woman of about sixty in a silk blouse with enormous earrings and a lot of make-up. She looked at Rei and sighed.

"Keiko sent a child," she said.

"I'm nineteen."

"That's what I said." She lit a cigarette. "Sit. You want a drink? No? Good, you can't afford one." She blew smoke at the ceiling. "Every night, two in the morning, my customers start fighting. Every night. Good people, my regulars, twenty years some of them. Two in the morning comes and they start shouting at each other about nothing. Last week one of them threw a glass. Last night two of them were crying and couldn't say why." She tapped ash into a tray. "And there's a cup that won't go away."

"A cup?"

She pointed with her cigarette. On the top shelf behind her, among the bottles, there was a plain white coffee mug with a small chip in the rim. It looked like nothing.

"It belonged to Ken-chan," she said. "He worked this counter with me for eleven years. He died last spring. His heart. In the street outside, on his way in to work." Her voice did not change, but her hand did, a little. "I threw that cup away in April. I put it in the rubbish myself. The next night it was back on the shelf. I threw it away again. It came back again. I smashed it with a hammer in June, I swear to you. Next night it was back on the shelf. Chip in the same place."

Rei looked at the mug. He let the door in his chest open a crack.

The mug changed.

It did not move. It did not glow. But it went heavy, the way a room goes heavy after people have been shouting in it. When he looked at it he felt something that was not his. Shame. A hot, sick, stomach-twisting shame. And under the shame, something smaller and sharper. A word that had not been said.

He opened the door a little more and reached up and took the mug down from the shelf.

[break What the cup knew]

The archive called it an object read. You hold a thing, and if enough feeling has soaked into it, it shows you what it remembers. Gin had written that most people who can do it think they are imagining things. *You are not imagining things.*

Rei held the mug in both hands, and the bar went away.

He was standing behind this same counter. It was night. It was the same purple room. But it was a year ago, maybe more, and he was not himself. He was taller and heavier and older. His knees hurt. His hands were drying this same mug with a cloth. And Mama Sachi was across the counter from him, younger, with smaller earrings, shouting. She was shouting about money. About the till. Someone had taken money from the till. And he, the man he was being, Ken-chan, was shouting back that it wasn't him, that how could she think it was him, after all these years.

Except that it was him.

Rei felt it, in the hot sick place under his ribs. Ken-chan had taken the money. He had needed it for something. His mother, a hospital, Rei could not see the details, only the shame of it. And he had lied, and Sachi had believed the lie, in the end, and they had made up, and he had gone on working at her counter for another year. And every night, at two in the morning, when the bar was loud and warm, he had looked at her and opened his mouth to tell her. And every night he had closed it again.

And then one spring evening his heart stopped in the street outside, on his way to work, on his way to finally telling her.

Rei came back. He was standing in the purple room with the mug in his hands and his cheeks were wet. His legs were shaking. The tunnel had started at the edges of his eyes again.

Mama Sachi was staring at him. Her cigarette had burned down to her fingers and she had not noticed.

"What," she said. "What did you see."

He told her. He told her slowly and plainly, the way Gin would have, because there was no gentle way to say it. When he finished, she did not say anything for a long time. She took the mug out of his hands. She held it the way you hold something that might break.

"Eighty thousand yen," she said at last. "Two years ago. I knew. Of course I knew. I always knew." She laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob. "I didn't care, you stupid man. I didn't care. I'd have given it to you."

She put the mug down on the counter. Not on the shelf. On the counter, in front of the empty stool where her customers sat.

"I forgive you, Ken-chan," she said. "You idiot. You complete idiot. I forgive you."

Rei felt it go. The heavy feeling went out of the room like air out of a balloon. The mug was just a mug. A white cup with a chip in it.

Mama Sachi wiped her face with the back of her hand and smeared her make-up and did not care. She opened the till and counted out forty thousand yen in notes and pushed them across the counter. Then she counted out another ten thousand and put them on top.

"That's for crying," she said. "Nobody cries for my customers but me." She waved him away. "Go home, child. Eat something. Tell Keiko you did good."

[break Thursday]

On Thursday Rei paid the rent. Not all of it. He took fifty thousand yen in cash and thirty thousand from his own account to the office of the property company in Akabane, and asked very politely if he could bring the last four thousand next week. A tired man in a short-sleeved shirt looked at him, and at the envelope of notes, and wrote something on a form and said yes, just this once.

Rei walked out with eleven hundred yen in his wallet and a backpack full of salt and iron. He felt rich. He felt terrified. He felt, for the first time, a little bit like his brother.

That night, at the kitchen table, he made a list on the back of the rent receipt. He wrote down everything he owned that might be useful. The key. The archive. Okada's card. The kit. Itsuki. Haruki. A café in Ni-chōme. A bakery in Jūjō. An old man with a garden full of weeds who would not give him a third cup of tea.

It was not much. It was more than he had had last week.

At the bottom he wrote a new line.

*Go back to Tanizaki on Saturday. Don't ask him for anything.*
