#title The Platform
#part Book One. The Archive

The first day of Obon was the thirteenth of August. Rei went back to work because he did not know what else to do.

He was an intern in the criminal investigation section of the police station in Ōji. He was nineteen and studying for the investigator exam, and three days a week he sorted files, made copies, carried boxes and sometimes sat in a car outside a building with a detective who ate rice crackers and did not talk. He was good at it. Better than the other interns. He noticed things. A drawer that had been opened and closed again. A wife who said "we" too often about a husband who was missing. The detectives called it luck. Gin had always called it something else, and changed the subject.

That day he noticed nothing. He sat at a desk and moved papers from one pile to another. Every hour he took out his phone. *Delivered.* Never *Read.*

At six in the evening he walked down the hill to Ōji station to go home the long way, round through Akabane, because he did not want to be in the flat yet.

[img place_oji_station_dusk.png | Ōji station at dusk]

The platform was crowded. It was the evening rush, and it was the first night of Obon, and half the city seemed to be going somewhere with a bag. A family with two small children and a box of sweets wrapped in paper for a grandmother. A group of office women in summer dresses going out to drink. A university student with a guitar case. An old man in a dark suit, much too warm for August, holding a bunch of white chrysanthemums. Chrysanthemums are the flowers for graves in Japan. He was going to visit someone.

The heat on the platform was thick and still. The lanterns along the roof swung a little. A recorded voice announced the next Keihin-Tōhoku line train in three minutes.

Rei stood near the middle of the platform, by a vending machine. And next to the vending machine, a man was buying a coffee.

He was about forty-five. Grey suit, the jacket over his arm. A white shirt with a soft collar. A wedding ring. A clear plastic umbrella from a konbini hooked over his wrist, though it had not rained in a week. He pressed the button for a hot canned coffee, not a cold one, and the machine dropped it with a clunk. He bent to take it out of the tray.

He stopped.

He stopped in the middle of the movement, bent over, with his hand in the tray. Not the way a man stops when he remembers something. The way a video stops when it freezes. His body stayed exactly where it was, and the rest of the platform went on moving around him.

The cold reached Rei a second later.

It came off the man the way heat comes off a road. It rolled over Rei's arms and the back of his neck and the hair rose on his skin. The pressure behind his eyes did not begin softly this time. It hit him, hard, like a hand pressed flat over both eyes. He breathed in for four. He held for four. He started to breathe out, and the door in his chest would not close. Something was leaning on it from the other side.

[break What it looked like]

The man straightened up.

He did it slowly, in the wrong order. A person stands up with the back first and the head last. This man stood up head first. His head lifted and tilted back, and back, until his face was pointing at the roof of the platform and the skin of his throat was stretched tight over the bones inside it. Then the rest of his body followed, one part at a time, like a puppet being lifted by a string from the top of the skull.

The canned coffee was still in his hand. Rei could see the steam coming off it.

The man's head came down again and turned toward Rei.

His face had changed. It was still his face. The same nose, the same small mole by the left eye, the same tired lines. But it hung differently. The skin had gone slack, as if every small muscle that holds a face together had let go at the same time. His cheeks had dropped. His lower lip hung away from his teeth. For one second it was the face of a man who had died sitting up. Then the skin tightened again. It tightened from the inside, all at once, the way a glove tightens when someone pushes a hand into it. Rei saw the shape of the cheekbones come up under the skin. He saw the jaw set. Something was wearing him now. And it was a different size from him.

The eyes were the worst part.

They were open very wide. The whites showed all the way round the dark centres. And they did not move together. The left eye was looking straight at Rei. The right eye was looking somewhere past him, at the tracks, and slowly sliding, as if it were looking for something on the ground. They moved separately, like the eyes of a lizard. They never blinked.

Then the man smiled.

The corners of his mouth went up. They kept going up, much further than a smile should go, toward his ears, until the lips were pulled thin and pale against the teeth. It was not one smile. It was the bottom half of a smile with nothing above it. The skin around his eyes did not move at all. There were no lines at the corners, no crinkle, no warmth. The cheeks did not lift. Only the mouth stretched, wider, and wider, until Rei could see the lower teeth and the pink of the gums under them, and a thin line of spit running from the corner of the lip down the chin.

"There you are," said the man.

The voice did not belong to the throat it came out of. It was too low. Much too low, a deep flat sound like someone speaking into a metal bucket. And it did not breathe. A person speaking takes small breaths between words without noticing. This voice did not. It came out in one long even line, every word at exactly the same height, like a recording played back too slowly. And at the end of each word there was a small wet click, the sound a tongue makes when it comes off the roof of a dry mouth.

"There. You. Are." Click. Click. Click.

Around them, people had started to notice. Not the way Rei noticed. They noticed that a man was standing too still and smiling at a teenager by a vending machine, and that something about it was wrong. The office women had gone quiet. The mother had pulled her two children behind her legs without seeming to know she was doing it. The old man with the chrysanthemums was staring, and his flowers were shaking in his hand.

"Sir?" said the university student with the guitar. "Sir, are you okay?"

The man's right eye stopped sliding along the ground. It swung up and fixed on the student. The left eye stayed on Rei.

"So much room in you," the voice said to Rei. "So much empty room. Just like the other one."

Rei could not move. He knew he could not move because he was trying very hard and nothing was happening. His legs were someone else's legs. The pressure on the door in his chest was enormous now. Through the gap, he could feel the thing in the man looking in at him. It was like standing in a dark room and knowing, without seeing, that someone is in the corner. It was curious. It was hungry. It was very, very pleased.

*The other one.*

"Where is he," Rei said. His own voice came out small and cracked. "Where is my brother."

The smile stretched one more millimetre. A thin line of blood appeared at the corner of the man's mouth, where the lip had split.

The recorded voice announced that the train was arriving. Rei heard it come. He felt the wind that pushes ahead of a train in a tunnel, warm and smelling of hot metal and brakes. The lights of the train came round the curve.

The man walked backward.

He did not turn. He did not look. He took one step back, and another, toward the edge of the platform, with the hot coffee still in his hand and the umbrella swinging from his wrist and his eyes still on Rei, the left one, wide and white and never blinking. The yellow line on the platform edge passed under his heels.

"Stop," said Rei. Or he thought he said it. He was not sure afterward.

The man said one more word. It was not in the deep voice. It was in a different voice, a normal man's voice, high and frightened and very quiet. The voice of a man who has just woken up and does not know where he is.

"Haruna?"

Then he stepped back off the edge.

[break Personal injury accident]

Rei did not see the train hit him. He had closed his eyes. He heard it. He heard the scream of the brakes, long and high and metal, and under it a sound he would hear for months afterward when he tried to sleep. A short, heavy, final sound. Then people screaming. Then the train, still sliding, still braking, the whole length of it going past him with its windows full of faces turning to look.

When he opened his eyes, the clear plastic umbrella was lying on the platform in front of him, spinning slowly on its side.

The cold had gone. The pressure behind his eyes had gone. The door in his chest swung loose, unlatched, and nothing was leaning on it any more. Whatever had been wearing the man had left the moment the man stopped being useful to it.

The canned coffee had rolled to the vending machine and stopped against it. It was still steaming.

A voice came over the speakers, calm and recorded. *Due to a personal injury accident at Ōji station, Keihin-Tōhoku line services are suspended. We apologise for the inconvenience.*

Here is something else you should know about Tokyo. When a person falls or jumps in front of a train, the announcement on the speakers calls it a *personal injury accident*. It happens often. Often enough that the city has a phrase for it, and the phrase is gentle, and it does not say anything at all. People on other platforms across the city would hear that announcement tonight and sigh, and check their phones, and look up another way home.

[break The cleaning]

The CVD arrived in eleven minutes.

Rei counted. He was sitting on a bench by then. He did not remember sitting down. The first ones were field officers, three of them, in plain grey clothes with a small white owl badge on the chest. They moved fast and spoke little. One walked the length of the platform with her eyes half closed and her hand held flat in front of her, as if feeling for heat from a stove. One went down onto the tracks. One stood very still by the vending machine where the man had stood, and after a moment he looked straight at Rei, and then away.

The second group arrived at nineteen minutes. They were different.

They wore good suits, dark blue and grey, well cut, and they carried tablets and clipboards. They smiled. They were the most calm and pleasant people Rei had ever seen in his life. They went to each witness one at a time and crouched down to their level and spoke softly. They gave each person a bottle of cold water and a small card. Rei could read one of the cards on the bench next to him. It had a phone number for counselling and the words *Office of Public Confidence* in small letters at the bottom, under the smiling white owl.

He watched them talk to the mother with the two children.

At first the mother was crying and shaking her head and pointing at the edge of the platform. Rei heard her say *smiling, he was smiling*. The woman in the blue suit nodded and nodded and held her hand. She spoke very gently for a long time. Rei could not hear the words. The mother's face changed while he watched. The fear went out of it slowly, like water going out of a bath. When the woman in the blue suit stood up, the mother was nodding too, and wiping her eyes, and saying something that Rei could hear clearly.

"The heat. Yes. He must have felt faint. The poor man."

They did the same with the office women. They did the same with the student with the guitar, who argued for longer, and then stopped arguing. They did the same with the old man with the chrysanthemums. He had dropped his flowers on the platform, and when they finished talking to him, he picked them up again and looked at them as if he could not remember why he had them.

Twenty minutes. That was all it took. Twenty minutes, and a man had stood at a vending machine and fainted in the heat and fallen. A terrible accident. The kind of thing that happens in August.

One of the blue suits was walking toward Rei. A young man with a kind face and a tablet held against his chest. He was smiling already.

And then someone sat down on the bench next to Rei, between him and the young man, and said quietly, "Don't talk to him."

[img char_okada-shizuka.png | Okada Shizuka]

It was a woman of about thirty. She wore a plain dark suit, not as good as the blue suits, a little shiny at the elbows. Her hair was tied back tight. She had three small notebooks in her jacket pocket, held together with a rubber band. She did not smile. She had the flat, unimpressed look of someone who has read everyone's file and was not surprised by any of them.

She held up a hand toward the young man in the blue suit. Two fingers. He stopped. He looked at her for a moment, still smiling, and then he turned and went to talk to someone else.

"Who are you?" Rei said.

"Okada. Investigation. Kita district." She did not look at him. She was watching the blue suits. "You saw it."

"I saw a man walk backwards off a platform. Smiling."

"Yes."

"Something was in him."

"Yes."

"It talked to me." His voice cracked and he hated it. "It said there you are. It said I had room in me. It said just like the other one."

Okada turned and looked at him properly for the first time. Her eyes were dark brown and very steady. She looked at his face, and then down, at the cord round his neck, where the key lay under his shirt. Something moved in her face. Not much. A small thing, at the corner of the mouth. Then it was gone.

"Uedera Rei," she said.

He stared at her. "How do you know my name?"

"Your brother told me to watch for you." She stood up and put her hands in her pockets. "I've been watching for four years. Get up. We're not doing this here."
