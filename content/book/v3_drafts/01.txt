#title Obon
#part Book One. The Archive

[img place_keihin_train_night.png | The Keihin-Tōhoku line at night]

The train was full of people going home, and some of them were dead.

Uedera Rei stood by the door of the third carriage with his bag between his feet and his hand on the steel pole. It was a little after eight in the evening, the twelfth of August, 2015. The train was a Keihin-Tōhoku line train, silver with a sky-blue stripe down its side. The line runs north to south through the middle of Tokyo, and every evening it carries thousands of tired people out of the offices of the city and back to the wards where they sleep. Rei rode it twice a day. He knew its sounds the way you know the sounds of your own kitchen.

The air conditioning was too cold, the way it always is on Tokyo trains in August. Outside the window the city went past in strips of light. Office towers. A row of small bars with red lanterns. A school with its gate shut for the summer. A pachinko hall so bright it hurt to look at.

Rei looked at the people instead. He always did. It was a habit he could not break, and his brother had stopped asking him to.

A salaryman sat across from him with his jacket folded on his knees. His shirt was damp under the arms. He was asleep with his mouth slightly open, and his phone was sliding out of his hand a millimetre at a time. Beside him two schoolgirls in summer uniforms shared one pair of earphones and laughed at something on a screen. A grandmother in a grey cardigan held a paper bag from a department store against her chest with both arms, as if someone might try to take it. A young man in a delivery company polo shirt stood with his eyes closed and his lips moving. He was counting something. Addresses, maybe. Or money.

And at the far end of the carriage, by the connecting door, a woman in a white blouse stood very straight and did not sway.

That was how Rei knew.

Everyone on a moving train sways. The floor tips a little at every curve, and every body tips with it. The salaryman's head rolled. The schoolgirls leaned into each other. The grandmother's bag shifted against her chest. The woman in the white blouse stayed exactly upright, like a picture of a woman pinned to the air. Her hair was black and cut at the jaw, and it did not move in the cold wind from the vent above her. Her face was turned toward the window. On the glass, where her reflection should have been, there was only the dark city going past.

Nobody else looked at her. Nobody else could see her.

Rei felt the familiar pressure start behind his eyes. It always began there, a soft weight, like the feeling before a headache. If he let it grow, the carriage would change. The colours would go thin. The sounds would come from further away. And the woman would turn her head and see that he could see her.

So he did what his brother had taught him to do when he was small.

He breathed in for four counts. He held it for four. He breathed out for six, slowly, through his nose. On the out-breath he imagined closing a door inside his chest, a heavy wooden door with an iron latch, and he heard the latch drop. Gin had taught him this at the kitchen table when Rei was eight and could not sleep. *It's a trick for bad nights,* Gin had said. *Close the door. Nothing comes in that you don't invite.* Rei had believed for years that it was only a trick for sleeping.

He knew better now. He did not know exactly what he knew. He only knew that the door worked.

The pressure behind his eyes eased. The colours came back. When he looked again toward the end of the carriage, there was a woman-shaped patch of cold air by the connecting door, and nothing else. The salaryman's phone finally slid off his knee and fell to the floor with a smack. He woke with a jerk, picked it up, and looked around to see if anyone had noticed. Nobody had. The train ran on.

[break What everyone knows]

Here is something you need to know about Tokyo in 2015. Everyone knew that the dead were real.

Not in the way of ghost stories. In the way of weather. Between the world of the living and whatever lies on the other side, there is a thin boundary. People called it the veil. Most of the time the veil holds, the way skin holds. Sometimes it grows thin in one place, the way skin grows thin over an old scar. And sometimes things come through.

Most of what comes through is small and sad. A grandmother who keeps walking into the kitchen of a flat she died in. A cold spot on a staircase where a boy fell. A smell of a particular cigarette in a car that was sold three times. Some of it is not small. Some of it is hungry, and patient, and very old.

The government had an office for this, the way it had an office for fires and floods. It was called the Civil Veil Division. People called it the CVD. Its officers went into the houses and streets where the veil had gone wrong and put things right, or tried to. There were posters about it in train stations. *Felt something strange? Call the CVD. Do not investigate alone.* There was a cartoon mascot, a smiling white owl in a police cap. Children drew it at school.

Everybody knew all of that. What most people did not know was how much more there was. The CVD made sure of it.

Rei's older brother, Uedera Gin, was a field officer for the CVD. He had been one for six years.

[break Ōji]

The train slowed for Ōji. Rei stepped off with the evening crowd onto the platform.

Ōji station sits low between two hills in Kita, the north ward of Tokyo. Kita was not a famous part of the city. Nobody came here on holiday. It was a place of narrow streets, old apartment blocks, small family shops and railway lines, with the wide grey Arakawa river along its northern edge. Most people who lived in Kita had lived there a long time. Rei had lived there all his life, in a two-room flat on the hill above this station, with his brother.

Obon would begin in a day. Obon is the festival in August when, people believe, the dead come home. For three or four days families clean their graves, light small fires at the door to show the spirits the way, hang paper lanterns and leave food on the family altar. Then they send the dead back again with more fires and lanterns floating on the rivers. In 2015 it was a little more than a custom. Everyone knew the veil grew thinner in that week. The CVD put extra officers on the streets. People who had lost someone that year stayed home with the doors open and the lights on.

Somebody had already hung white paper lanterns along the station exit. They swung a little in the hot wind from the tracks. Rei walked under them and out into the evening.

The heat hit him like a wet towel. It was still thirty degrees, even after dark. The cicadas were screaming in the trees on Asukayama, the park on the hill beside the station. An old man in a vest and shorts sat outside a closed shop on a plastic stool, fanning himself with a paper fan from a bank. A girl on a bicycle went past with a bag of groceries swinging from the handlebar. Somewhere a television was showing a baseball game, and every few seconds a crowd on the television cheered.

Rei's phone buzzed. It was Daiki.

Sasaki Daiki was seventeen and had been Rei's friend since school, the loud kind of friend who sends nine messages where one would do. He knew nothing about the veil except what was on the posters. He knew that Rei's brother worked for the CVD and he thought this was the coolest thing in the world.

*bro*
*summer festival in jujo saturday. youre coming*
*not a question*
*bring gin he can scare the drunk guys*

Rei typed back with his thumb as he climbed the hill. *Maybe. Gin's working all week. Obon.*

*lame,* Daiki wrote. A second later another message came. *tell him hi anyway.*

Rei smiled at the screen and put the phone away.

[break The kitchen]

[img res_apartmentempty.png | The kitchen of the Uedera flat]

The flat was on the third floor of an old concrete building near the top of the hill. Like most small apartment buildings in Tokyo, it had no inside hallway. Each front door opened onto a long concrete walkway along the outside of the building, with a metal railing on one side and the street three floors below. Rei took off his shoes in the genkan. In a Japanese home the genkan is the small entrance just inside the front door, a step lower than the rest of the floor, where everyone leaves their shoes before stepping up into the house.

On the wall of the genkan there was a brass hook. It was empty. That meant Gin was not home yet. Gin hung his keys on that hook every time he came in, before he took off his coat, before anything. The brass was worn bright where his thumb went.

The flat was two small rooms and a kitchen. Gin had the bedroom with the window. Rei had the other room, but he slept on the living-room sofa most nights, with his feet hanging off the end, because he liked to hear the trains. The kitchen was the heart of the place. A small table with two chairs. A gas stove with two rings. A rice cooker with a crack in its lid that Gin kept saying he would replace.

Rei made dinner. It was his job on the nights Gin worked late, and in the summer that meant most nights. He made ginger pork, because Gin liked it. He sliced the pork thin and fried it with soy sauce, mirin, sugar and a lot of fresh ginger, until the kitchen smelled sweet and sharp and the window steamed up. He ate his half standing at the counter. He put Gin's half on a plate with rice and turned a second plate upside down over it, to keep it warm, the way their mother used to do before she died. He left it on the counter by the stove.

At nine o'clock he sent Gin a message. *Dinner's on the counter. Ginger pork.*

There was no answer. That was normal. During Obon week Gin sometimes worked until two or three in the morning and came home smelling of incense and salt. But he always answered, in the end. Sometimes it was one word. *Thanks.* Sometimes it was a photograph of something strange he had seen, with no explanation. In nineteen years Gin had never once let a night end without some word.

Rei lay on the sofa with the television on and the sound off. He watched a cooking show without seeing it. At some point he closed his eyes.

[break 3:47]

He woke because someone knocked on the door.

Three knocks. Not loud. Even and patient, with the same space between each one, the way a doctor knocks before coming into a room.

Rei sat up. The television had gone to a blue screen. The room was dark except for that blue. His neck hurt from the arm of the sofa. He looked at the clock on the stove, because it was the only clock he could see from there. The green numbers said 3:47.

He waited for the knock again. It did not come.

*Gin,* he thought. Gin had forgotten his keys. That had never happened, not once, but it was the only thing that made sense. Rei got up and crossed the dark living room. The floor was warm under his bare feet. He stepped down into the genkan.

The hook on the wall was still empty.

He put his hand on the door handle. And then he stopped, because the metal was cold.

It was August. The handle should have been warm. It had been warm when he came home. Now it was cold the way a can from a vending machine is cold, so cold it almost hurt. Rei felt the pressure begin behind his eyes, much faster than on the train. He did the breathing. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. He heard the iron latch drop on the door in his chest.

Then he opened the front door.

The walkway was empty.

The orange street lamp lit the metal railing, the bicycles parked below, and the row of closed doors, exactly as it did every night. Two doors down, the neighbour's futon was still hung over the rail to air. A moth was beating itself against the lamp. Nothing else moved. Far away, a truck changed gears on the main road. The city went on being large and ordinary and asleep.

Something glinted on the concrete just past the doorstep.

It was a key. A single brass door key on a plain steel ring. Rei crouched and picked it up. It was cold, properly cold, and wet with a thin film of water, as if it had been lying in a fridge. He knew the key. He had known it all his life. It was the key to this door, and the ring was the ring Gin had carried since he was a teenager. There was a small dent in the ring where Gin had once caught it in a car door.

Beside the key lay half a cigarette. Gin's brand. Smoked exactly halfway and put out against the concrete. Gin smoked half a cigarette on the step every morning and then put it out, because Rei complained about the smell inside. He never smoked at night.

Rei stood up. He looked left along the walkway, then right, then down at the street. There was no one. There was no sound of footsteps on the stairs. There was no car pulling away. There was nothing at all, and later that was the part that stayed with him. How completely nothing else there was.

He stood there until the moth fell from the lamp and lay on the concrete, still moving its legs.

Then he went inside, closed the door and locked it, and stood in the dark kitchen holding his brother's key. The key did not get warm in his hand. It stayed cold. And the strange thing, the thing he would not understand for months, was that holding it made the floor feel steadier under his feet. As if the key were a nail, and he were a picture, and he would not fall off the wall while he held it.

On the counter by the stove, the plate was still upside down over the other plate. Nobody had lifted it.

Rei took out his phone and wrote to his brother.

*Where are you?*

He watched the screen for a long time. The message said *Delivered*. It never said *Read*.
