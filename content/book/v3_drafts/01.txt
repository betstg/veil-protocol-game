#title Obon
#part Book One. The Archive

[img place_kita_obon_lane.png | Obon lanterns in a lane in Kita]

On the first night of Obon, the dead came home to Kita, and Uedera Rei went to see them dance.

It was the twelfth of August, 2015, and all day the narrow lanes of the ward had been filling up with paper lanterns. White ones, mostly, hung outside the houses of families who had lost someone that year, so that the new dead would know which door was theirs. Round red ones strung along the fronts of the shops. Small square ones on bamboo poles beside the gates of the old wooden houses, swinging a little in the hot wind that came up from the railway lines and never cooled anything down. By evening the whole hill smelled of incense and grilled corn and the green, bitter smoke of the little fires people lit on their doorsteps.

Obon is the festival in August when, people believe, the dead come back to visit the living. For three or four days families clean the graves and leave food on the family altar, and light small fires at the door so that the spirits can find their way home in the dark. Then, at the end, they send the dead back again with more fires, and with lanterns set floating down the rivers to carry them away.

In most of Japan that is a custom. A lovely one, an old one, but a custom. In Tokyo, in 2015, it was a little more than that.

[break What everyone knows]

Here is something you need to know about this city, before anything else. Everyone knew that the dead were real.

Not the way people know ghost stories, with a shiver and a laugh afterward. The way people know about weather. Between the world of the living and whatever lies on the other side, there is a thin boundary. People called it the veil. Most of the time the veil holds, the way skin holds. Sometimes it grows thin in one place, the way skin grows thin and shiny over an old scar. And sometimes, through the thin places, things come through.

Most of what comes through is small and sad. A grandmother who keeps walking into the kitchen of the flat where she died, looking for her reading glasses. A cold patch on a staircase where a boy fell, forty years ago, that every tenant learns to step around without knowing why. The smell of one particular brand of cigarette in a car that has been sold three times and cleaned by every owner. Some of what comes through is not small at all. Some of it is hungry, and patient, and very old, and it does not want to go back.

The government had an office for this, the way it had offices for fires and for floods. It was called the Civil Veil Division, and everybody called it the CVD. Its officers went into the houses and the streets where the veil had gone wrong and put things right, or tried to. There were posters in every train station in Tokyo. *Felt something strange? Call the CVD. Do not investigate alone.* There was even a cartoon mascot, a round white owl in a little police cap, smiling, and children drew it at school and stuck it on the fridge.

And everybody knew that in Obon week the veil grew thinner than at any other time of the year. The CVD put extra officers out on the streets. People who had lost someone stayed home with all the lights on and the front door unlocked, just in case.

Rei's older brother, Uedera Gin, was one of those officers. He had worked in the field for the CVD for six years. In Obon week he hardly came home at all.

[img Rei_Gin_Together.png | Rei and Gin]

[break The dance]

Itsuki was waiting for him at the bottom of the hill, sitting on the low wall outside the shrine with a stick of grilled squid in one hand and a sketchbook open on his knee, drawing the crowd as if they were all posing for him and had simply forgotten.

Kuroda Itsuki was twenty, a year older than Rei, and he had been Rei's best friend since they were twelve, which was long enough that neither of them could remember a version of their life without the other one in it. He studied oil painting at the most famous art school in the country and behaved as if this were a clerical error he was too polite to point out. He was wearing an enormous grey hoodie over a white shirt that had obviously cost more than Rei's whole wardrobe, a pair of headphones round his neck that he never seemed to actually listen to, and there was blue paint dried in the creases of his knuckles, because there was always paint somewhere on Itsuki. He had a pencil stuck through the knot of hair at the back of his head and an unlit cigarette tucked behind one ear, and when he saw Rei coming down the lane under the lanterns he held the squid up in the air like a torch and shouted, "*Finally.* I've been here forty minutes. I've drawn every grandmother in Kita. Twice."

[img char_kuroda-itsuki.png | Kuroda Itsuki]

"You said half past seven."

"I said half past seven and I meant seven, because I know you, and you're always late, so I tell you a later time so you'll be on time." He frowned at his own logic, then waved it away with the squid. "It made sense in my head. Here. I saved you the end. The end's the best part, everyone knows that."

Rei took the squid. It was cold and chewy and covered in sweet sauce, and he ate it anyway, standing at the shrine gate, while Itsuki shut the sketchbook and stood up and stretched with his arms over his head until his back cracked.

The Bon dance was in the shrine grounds, the way it was every year. In the middle stood a tall wooden tower hung all over with lanterns, red and white, with a big drum on top and an old man in a twisted cotton headband beating it with two sticks, slowly, steadily, like a heart. Round the tower, in a wide slow circle, people danced. Grandmothers in light cotton summer kimonos printed with fish and fireworks and flowers. Small children in miniature ones, copying the hand movements one beat too late and very seriously. A group of high school boys pretending they were only there to laugh at it and then forgetting to laugh. Two young policemen at the edge of the crowd, standing very straight in their uniforms and absolutely not tapping their feet.

The dance itself was very old and very simple. Clap. Step forward. Turn your hands in front of you, slowly, as if you were scooping up water from a stream. Step back. Clap again. It went round and round the tower all evening without stopping, because it is a dance for the dead, and the dead, people say, like things that go round and round and come back to where they started.

"Come on," said Itsuki, grabbing his wrist.

"I can't dance."

"Nobody can dance. That's not the point. The point is the grandmothers think it's sweet when young men try and fail." He was already pulling Rei toward the circle. "Look sad. Look like you lost someone. They'll adopt you."

They joined the circle between a grandmother with a fan and a little boy of about six in a kimono printed with blue goldfish, and for the next twenty minutes they were very bad at it. Itsuki was worse than Rei, but loudly and on purpose, turning his hands the wrong way and clapping a beat too early, until the grandmother with the fan lost patience completely and took him by both elbows and taught him properly, step by step, scolding him the whole time in a soft rough voice, and he let her, and bowed to her afterward so deeply and so gravely that she slapped his arm and laughed until she had to fan herself. The little boy in the goldfish kimono watched Rei's hands very carefully and copied them, every movement, and when Rei got it wrong the boy got it wrong too, solemnly, in exactly the same way, so that after a while Rei had to concentrate very hard on getting it right just so that the child would not learn the dance badly for the rest of his life.

It was hot, and loud, and the drum went on and on, and Rei found that he was smiling, and that he had been smiling for a while without noticing.

[break Stories]

Afterward they bought shaved ice, melon for Itsuki, bright green and so sweet it made his teeth ache, and plain strawberry for Rei, and sat on the stone steps of the shrine to eat it, with their knees up and their backs against the warm stone, and watched the dance go round below them.

"Did I ever tell you," said Itsuki, digging in his ice with the little plastic spoon, "about the summer I tried to run away to Kyoto?"

"You've never told me that."

"Good. Because it's humiliating, and I've been saving it." He pointed the spoon at the lanterns. "I was nine. It was Obon. My whole family was at the house in Nezu for the ceremonies, all the aunts and uncles and the cousins and my grandmother's friends, and everyone was dressed in black and talking in that voice. You know the voice. The one where everything's a funeral, even the tea." He put on the voice, low and slow and sorrowful. "*Itsuki, sit up straight. Itsuki, the ancestors are watching.* So I packed a bag. Very carefully. I packed four rice balls, my sketchbook, a torch, a map of Japan I'd torn out of a school atlas, and one sock."

"One sock."

"I was nine. I had priorities." He ate a spoonful of green ice and winced at the cold. "And I walked out the back gate while they were all chanting, and I got as far as the konbini at the bottom of the street, and I bought a bottle of melon soda with my own money, and I sat on the kerb outside to drink it and plan my route to Kyoto. And then I got a bit sleepy, because it was very late and I was nine. And I woke up at six in the morning, still on the kerb, with my head on my bag, and the old man who ran the konbini had put a blanket over me and was standing there looking down at me with a second melon soda."

Rei laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, a real laugh, and Itsuki looked pleased with himself.

"Nobody at the house had even noticed I was gone," said Itsuki. "That's the best part. I walked back up the street with my bag and my one sock and went in through the back gate and sat down at breakfast, and my father said, *You have rice on your face,* and that was the whole conversation." He shrugged, and something went across his face for a second, very fast, and was gone. "Anyway. That's why I hate melon soda."

"You're eating melon ice."

"That's different. That's revenge." He scraped the bottom of the cup. "Your turn. Tell me one. A Gin one. I like the Gin ones."

Rei thought about it. There were so many. Gin teaching him to ride a bicycle in the car park at the bottom of the hill and running behind him holding the seat for so long that Rei had been riding on his own for two whole circuits before he looked back and saw his brother standing at the far end of the car park with his hands in his pockets, smiling. Gin coming to his school sports day straight from a night shift, still in his work coat, and falling asleep in the stands with his head on a stranger's shoulder, and the stranger letting him. Gin burning a pan of curry so badly that the smoke alarm went off and the neighbours came out on the walkway, and Gin opening the door with the burnt pan in one hand and bowing to all of them and saying, perfectly seriously, *Please forgive us, my brother is learning to cook.*

"He blamed me for the curry," said Rei. "I was eleven. I wasn't even in the kitchen. I was in the bath."

Itsuki laughed so hard he nearly dropped his ice down the steps, and a grandmother below them turned round and looked up and smiled at the two of them, the way old women smile at young men laughing on temple steps in summer, as if they were part of the festival too.

[break Her]

Itsuki opened his sketchbook again while Rei was finishing his ice. He did it the way some people take out their phones, without thinking, the way you scratch an itch. The pencil came out of his hair, and his hand began to move, and his face went quiet and far away, the way it always did when he was drawing, as if part of him had stepped out of the room.

He was drawing the dance. The tower, the lanterns, the turning hands. The grandmother with the fan. The little boy in the goldfish kimono. Quick, loose, beautiful lines that caught the way people moved better than a photograph could.

Rei watched the circle go round below them, and that was when he saw her.

She was dancing between a man in a dark blue kimono and a girl with a paper fan. A small old woman with white hair pinned up in a neat bun, wearing a faded summer kimono with a pattern of morning glories on it, pale blue and violet. She danced beautifully. Better than anyone in the circle. Every movement of her hands was slow and exact and full of a kind of quiet pleasure, as if she had danced this dance every summer for seventy years and had never once grown tired of it.

But she was dancing to a different drum.

When the drum on the tower beat, everyone in the circle stepped. She did not. She stepped half a beat later, always, in time with something else. Something nobody else could hear. A drum that was very far away, or a very long time ago. And when she turned under the lanterns, the red light went through her a little, the way light goes through a paper screen when someone stands behind it.

Nobody looked at her. The man in the dark blue kimono danced right beside her, so close their sleeves should have touched, and he never once turned his head.

Rei felt the familiar pressure start behind his eyes. It always began there, a soft weight, like the feeling just before a headache, and it always grew. If he let it, the colours of the festival would go thin and pale, and the music would sound as if it were coming from the far end of a long corridor, and the old woman would turn her head, slowly, in the middle of the dance, and see that he could see her.

So he did what his brother had taught him when he was small.

He breathed in for four counts. He held it for four. He breathed out for six, slowly, through his nose. And on the out-breath he imagined a door inside his chest, a heavy old wooden door with an iron latch, swinging shut, and he heard the latch drop.

[img scene_kitchen_lesson.png | Gin teaching Rei at the kitchen table]

Gin had taught him that at the kitchen table when Rei was eight years old and could not sleep because of the things he saw at night. *It's a trick for bad nights,* Gin had said, sitting across from him with a cup of tea going cold between his hands, very seriously, as if he were teaching Rei something as ordinary and important as tying his shoes. *Close the door. Nothing comes in that you don't invite.* For years and years Rei had believed it was only a trick to help him sleep. He knew better now, at nineteen. He did not know exactly what it was that he knew. He only knew that the door worked, and that he did not want to find out what happened if one day it did not.

The pressure behind his eyes eased, and stopped. The festival came back, loud and bright and close. When he looked down at the circle again there was a gap between the man in the dark blue kimono and the girl with the fan, a gap about the width of one small old woman. The dance went round. The gap moved with it, round the tower, under the lanterns, and then the circle closed up, and it was gone.

She only came home to dance, Rei thought. It's Obon. She's allowed.

Beside him, Itsuki's pencil stopped.

Rei looked at the sketchbook. Itsuki was frowning down at his own drawing with an odd, puzzled expression, the pencil hanging forgotten in his fingers, like a man who has walked into a room and cannot remember why he came in.

The drawing was of the dance. The tower. The lanterns. The grandmother with the fan, the boy in the goldfish kimono, the man in the dark blue kimono. And between the man in the dark blue kimono and the girl with the fan, in the middle of the circle, there was a small old woman with her hair in a bun, turning her hands in the air, drawn in lines so light and pale that you could almost see the paper through her.

"Huh," said Itsuki quietly.

"Who's that?" said Rei, very carefully.

"No idea." Itsuki tilted the sketchbook toward the light from the lanterns, then away again, squinting at it. "I didn't see her. I just drew. My hand does that sometimes, puts people in who aren't there." He laughed, but it was a short, uneasy laugh, and he did not look at Rei when he did it. "Weird, right? My teachers think it's a style. They call it *haunted.* They give me good marks for it." He closed the sketchbook with a snap and slid the pencil back into his hair. "Don't look at me like that. It's nothing. I'm just tired. I've been up since yesterday."

Rei did not say anything. He looked at the circle going round below them, at the place where the old woman had been, and he thought that one day he was going to have to tell Itsuki about the door in his chest, and about the things that came to the kitchen window at night when he was small. Not tonight. But one day.

[break Home early]

He left at half past eight.

"It's *Obon*," said Itsuki, scandalised, standing on the shrine steps with his empty ice cup. "Nobody goes home at half past eight at Obon. The grandmothers haven't even started on the beer. There's going to be karaoke. There's a man over there with a guitar and a very sad face and I *need* to know what he sings."

"I've got work tomorrow."

"You're an *intern*. Interns don't have work. Interns have photocopying and crying in the toilets."

"Then I've got photocopying tomorrow." Rei hesitated, and then said the real reason, because it was Itsuki, and there was never any point lying to Itsuki. "Gin might come home early."

Itsuki looked at him with the plastic spoon still in his mouth.

"In Obon week," he said, round the spoon.

"He might."

"He never does. You told me. Last year you didn't see him for four days."

"He might," said Rei.

Itsuki looked at him for a moment longer, and his face softened, the way it almost never did when other people were around. He took the spoon out of his mouth.

"Okay," he said. "Go on, then. Go home and wait for your big brother like a good little housewife." He grinned to take the sting out of it. "I'm going back to the studio to paint. I can't sleep anyway. Text me when he gets in, so I know you're not sitting there all night staring at the door like a dog."

"I don't do that."

"You absolutely do that." He punched Rei's shoulder, lightly. "Go. And take the long way, by the lanterns. It's pretty. You never look at anything pretty."

[img place_oji_station_dusk.png | Ōji, below the hill]

Rei took the long way. He walked up the hill under the lanterns with the drum of the Bon dance following him, getting fainter and fainter behind, and the cicadas still screaming in the trees of the park even though it was dark. The flat was on the third floor of an old concrete building near the top of the hill above Ōji station. Like most small apartment buildings in Tokyo it had no inside hallway. Every front door opened onto a long concrete walkway that ran along the outside of the building, with a metal railing on one side and the street three floors below. Rei climbed the outside stairs and let himself in and took off his shoes in the genkan. In a Japanese home the genkan is the little entrance just inside the front door, one step lower than the rest of the floor, where everyone leaves their shoes before stepping up into the house, so that the dirt of the street stays outside.

On the wall of the genkan there was a brass hook. It was empty. Gin hung his keys on that hook every single time he came in, before he took off his coat, before he even said hello. The brass was worn bright and smooth in one small place, where his thumb always went.

[img res_apartmentlivingroom.png | The living room of the Uedera flat]

The flat was two small rooms and a kitchen. Gin had the room with the desk and the window. Rei had the other room, but most nights he slept on the sofa in the living room with his feet hanging off the end, because he liked to hear the trains going past at the bottom of the hill, and because from the sofa he could see the front door. The kitchen was the heart of the place. A small table with two chairs, one for each of them. A gas stove with two rings. A rice cooker with a crack in its lid that Gin had been saying he would replace for three years.

Rei did the things he always did on the nights Gin worked late.

He made ginger pork, because it was Gin's favourite. He sliced the pork very thin and fried it in the pan with soy sauce and mirin and a spoonful of sugar and a great deal of fresh ginger, much more than any recipe said, because that was how Gin liked it, until the whole kitchen smelled sweet and sharp and the window steamed up. He ate his own half standing at the counter. He put Gin's half on a plate with a mound of rice, turned a second plate upside down over the top of it to keep it warm, and left it on the counter by the stove, exactly the way Gin always left dinner for him on the nights Rei came home late.

He took Gin's work shirts off the drying rack on the balcony and ironed two of them, not very well, and hung them on the back of Gin's bedroom door so that they would be ready. He filled the kettle so that it would only need switching on. He put Gin's slippers in the genkan with the toes pointing inward, toward the house, so that Gin could step straight out of his shoes and into them without having to turn round.

At nine o'clock he sent a message. *Dinner's on the counter. Ginger pork. Don't eat it cold, use the microwave, I mean it.*

No answer. That was normal. In Obon week Gin often worked until two or three in the morning and came home smelling of incense and salt and something else, something sharp and burnt, that Rei had never asked about. He did not always answer messages. But he always came home.

That was the thing about Gin. He was late, sometimes. Sometimes he was very late. Sometimes Rei woke up on the sofa at four in the morning and found that the television had been switched off and a blanket had been put over him, and the keys were on the hook, and Gin's door was closed, and the only sign that his brother had come home at all was the blanket, and the empty plate rinsed and left upside down in the sink. But in nineteen years there had never, not once, been a night when Gin did not come home in the end.

[img scene_three_am_exams.png | A night Gin came home late]

Rei sent Itsuki a message. *Home. He's not back yet.*

The answer came at once. *shocking. go to sleep. he'll wake u up stomping around and eating ur pork at 3am like a raccoon.*

Rei smiled at the screen. He lay down on the sofa with the television on and the sound turned off. A cooking programme. An old man making soba noodles by hand, folding the dough over and over on a wooden board and cutting it with a great square knife into threads as thin as string. Rei watched it without really seeing it. Down at the bottom of the hill, very faint now, he could still hear the drum of the Bon dance, going round and round.

At some point, without meaning to, he closed his eyes.

[break After three]

He woke up because the drum had stopped.

The room was dark except for the cold blue light of the television, which had given up on the cooking programme and gone to a screen of coloured bars. His neck ached from lying on the arm of the sofa. He was hot and sticky and his mouth tasted of strawberry ice.

There was no blanket over him.

He lay still for a moment and listened. No drum. No trains. No cicadas, even. It was that hour at the very bottom of the night when even Tokyo goes quiet, when the last trains have stopped and the first ones have not started, and the whole enormous city seems to be holding its breath. He turned his head on the sofa arm and looked at the green numbers on the clock on the stove, because it was the only clock he could see from where he lay.

3:08.

He got up, stiffly, and switched the television off. The room went fully dark. He went to the genkan and switched on the small light there.

The hook was empty. Gin's slippers were exactly where he had left them, side by side, toes pointing in.

He went into the kitchen and switched on the light over the stove. The plate was still upside down over the other plate on the counter. He lifted it. The ginger pork had gone cold and grey, the sauce set into a thin brown jelly, and the rice had dried hard and yellowish at the edges. Nobody had touched it.

He took his phone out of his pocket. No messages. His own message, *Don't eat it cold, use the microwave, I mean it,* still said *Delivered* underneath it in small grey letters. It did not say *Read*.

And that, more than the empty hook, more than the cold plate, was what made his stomach turn over. Because Gin read everything. Even when he was too busy to answer, even in the middle of the worst nights of Obon, he read. Rei could watch the little word appear under his messages, *Read*, at midnight, at one, at half past two, and know that somewhere out there in the dark city his brother had pulled his phone out of his coat pocket for one second, and looked, and put it away again. It was such a small thing. It was the kind of small thing you never notice until the day it is not there.

He called. It rang four times, and then Gin's recorded voice came on, calm and slightly bored, the voice of a man who did not like talking to machines. *This is Uedera. Leave a message.*

Rei did not leave a message. He put the phone face down on the kitchen table and sat down in his own chair and looked across at Gin's empty one.

He's working, he told himself. It's Obon. Something happened somewhere in the city, something bad, and he's right in the middle of it, and his phone is in his coat and his coat is in a car. He'll come in at five o'clock with salt on his shoes and dark rings under his eyes and say sorry, sorry, I know, and eat the pork cold out of the pan with his fingers even though I told him not to.

He waited.

3:15. A taxi went past on the main road at the bottom of the hill, and the sound of its engine climbed up through the open window and faded away. 3:22. Somewhere in the building a toilet flushed, and the old pipes knocked inside the wall like someone tapping to be let out. 3:30. He got up and filled a glass with water at the sink and stood holding it and did not drink it. 3:36. He called again. Four rings. *This is Uedera. Leave a message.*

He sat in the dark kitchen with the cold plate in front of him and the phone face down on the table, and he was nineteen years old, and he felt about eight.

[break 3:47]

It was a little after twenty to four when he noticed the light.

He had been staring at nothing, at the dark little hallway between the kitchen and the two bedrooms, the way you stare at nothing when you are waiting for a sound. And at the bottom of Gin's door, along the floor, there was a thin bright line of yellow light.

Rei sat very still in his chair.

There had been no light under that door when he came home. He was completely sure of it. He had hung the two ironed shirts on the back of it at nine o'clock, and he had opened it a hand's width to reach the hook on the inside, and the room behind it had been dark. The curtains shut. The desk lamp off. He was completely, completely sure.

He got up. He walked down the little hallway. The floorboards were warm under his bare feet. He stopped outside his brother's door, with the two ironed shirts hanging on the other side of it, and the thin line of light lying across his toes.

"Gin?" he said. His voice came out small and strange in the quiet flat.

Nothing.

He looked back over his shoulder at the green numbers on the clock on the stove.

3:47.

He opened the door.

[img res_archive.png | Gin's desk]

The room was empty.

The bed was made, the way Gin always made it, with square corners and the pillow straight. The curtains were drawn. The two shirts swung gently on the back of the door as it opened, and settled. And the desk lamp was on. The small one with the green glass shade, bent low over the desk, making a circle of warm yellow light on the bare dark wood. The desk chair was pulled out and turned a little to one side, the way a chair is left when someone has just that moment stood up from it to go and answer the door.

In the circle of lamplight, lying open, was Gin's archive.

That was what Gin called it. *The archive.* A thick field notebook with a soft black cover gone pale and furry at the corners from years of being carried. Gin took it everywhere. Into places Rei was not allowed to know about, and out again, year after year. He wrote in it at the kitchen table very late at night, in his small exact handwriting, and he always closed it, gently but quickly, whenever Rei came near. He did not leave it on tables. He did not leave it anywhere. And here it was, lying open under the lamp, squared neatly to the edge of the desk, as if someone had arranged it there for the next person who came in and sat down.

Rei did not sit down. He stood in the doorway and looked at it, and he felt the pressure begin behind his eyes, and he did not close the door in his chest. He did not even think of it. He was too frightened to think of anything.

The top drawer of the desk was open a few centimetres.

He knew that drawer. Every morning for as long as he could remember, before he left for work, Gin had pulled that drawer open, looked inside it for one second, and slid it shut again. Every single morning, the way other people check that they have their wallet. Rei had seen him do it a thousand times. He had never once asked what was in it. It was just one of the things Gin did, like hanging his keys on the brass hook, like smoking exactly half a cigarette on the walkway with his morning coffee and putting the other half out against the railing.

He crossed the room and pulled the drawer open.

There was nothing in it but a key.

A single brass door key on a plain steel ring. The spare key to this flat. Rei had not even known that they had a spare. He reached in and picked it up.

It was cold.

Not cool, the way metal is cool in the shade. Cold. Properly, painfully cold, like a can from a vending machine on a summer day, so cold that his fingers ached around it, in a hot closed room in the middle of August. And the moment it was in his hand, the strangest thing happened. The floor felt steadier under his feet. The pressure behind his eyes stopped growing. It did not go away, but it stopped where it was, the way a wave stops at the top of a beach and hangs there for a moment before it slides back. As if the key were a nail, and Rei were a picture hanging on a wall, and as long as he held on to it he would not fall.

He did not understand it. He would not understand it for months.

He looked down at the archive lying open in the circle of lamplight. Both pages were covered in Gin's handwriting, small and neat and exact, the handwriting of a man who trusted ink more than he trusted his memory. He could not make himself read it. Not yet. If he read it, it would be real, all of it, the empty hook and the cold plate and the word that would not change from *Delivered* to *Read*.

He switched off the lamp. He left the book exactly where it lay. He went back to the kitchen with the cold key in his fist and sat down in his own chair, across from his brother's empty one.

Then he picked up his phone and wrote to Gin.

*Where are you?*

He watched the screen for a long time. The message said *Delivered*.

It never said *Read*.
