#title Haruki
#part Book One. The Archive

The note in the archive said only *Jazz bar, Thursday to Saturday evenings.* There was no address. Under Tsukishiro Haruki's name Gin had drawn a tiny coffee cup, and next to it a musical note, and next to that an arrow pointing to a station name. Nezu.

Nezu is an old neighbourhood in Bunkyō, the ward next to Ueno. It is one of the few parts of Tokyo that did not burn in the war, so it still has narrow lanes of wooden houses with potted plants crowding their doorsteps, old shops that sell rice crackers and paper, and a big shrine with a tunnel of red gates. On Friday evening Rei came up out of the subway into warm air that smelled of grilled fish and mosquito coil. The lanes were full of families walking slowly in the dusk, and old men in undershirts sitting outside their doors on folding chairs, and a boy practising the trumpet very badly behind an open window.

He walked the lanes for forty minutes before he heard the piano.

It came from a basement under a shop that sold wooden combs. A narrow staircase went down from the street. At the bottom there was a heavy door with a small brass plate on it. No name, just a drawing of a crescent moon. Rei pushed the door open.

The bar was tiny. Eight stools at a wooden counter, three small tables, a piano in the corner with a man in his seventies playing it with his eyes closed. The walls were covered with old record sleeves. The air was cool and dark and smelled of whisky, old wood and coffee. Very good coffee. Rei had never smelled coffee like it.

Behind the counter, a young man in a dark jacket was making it.

[img char_tsukishiro-haruki.png | Tsukishiro Haruki]

He was about thirty. He had black hair that fell untidily over his forehead and a narrow, handsome, sleepy face, the face of someone who has just woken up from a nap and is pleased about it. He wore a dark suit jacket over a white shirt with the collar open. Pinned to the lapel of the jacket was a small dried flower, pale yellow, pressed flat. He was pouring hot water from a kettle with a long thin spout over a paper filter, in slow circles, with complete attention, as if nothing in the world mattered except the circle.

He did not look up. "Sit anywhere," he said. "I'll be one minute. You can't hurry this."

Rei sat at the counter. The man finished pouring. He waited, watching the coffee drip through, for what felt like a very long time. Then he poured it into a white cup and put it in front of an old woman at the end of the counter, who smiled at him like a grandmother at a favourite grandson. Then he turned to Rei.

And stopped.

The sleepy face went very still. The man looked at Rei for a long moment. At his face. At his eyes. And then, exactly as Okada had, down at the cord round his neck where it disappeared under his shirt.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, no."

"You're Tsukishiro Haruki," said Rei.

"I am." The man put the kettle down. "And you're Rei. You have his jaw. He always said you didn't, and he was wrong." He took a breath. "He didn't come last night. He comes every Thursday. Every Thursday for three years. He sits on that stool, he drinks two cups, he complains about the Division, he goes home. Last night he didn't come. I called him nine times." He looked at the cord again. "That's his key. Isn't it."

Rei did not trust his voice. He nodded.

Haruki stood very still behind his counter for a moment. Then he turned away, took a clean white cup down from a shelf, and began to make another coffee. He did it exactly as slowly as before. The kettle. The circles. The waiting. His hands were steady. His face was not.

"I'm going to make you a coffee," he said, with his back to Rei. "And while I make it, I'm going to be very upset in private. And then I'll turn round and we'll talk properly. Is that alright?"

"Yes," said Rei.

The old man at the piano played something slow. The old woman at the end of the counter sipped her coffee. Haruki made the coffee. When he turned round and put the cup in front of Rei, his eyes were red at the edges, but he was smiling again. A small, crooked smile.

"Drink it while it's hot," he said. "It's the only thing I'm really good at."

Rei drank. It was the best coffee he had ever tasted. It was strong and clean and tasted faintly of dark chocolate and of something like cherries. For a few seconds he forgot to be afraid.

[break Best friend]

"How did you know him?" Rei asked.

Haruki leaned on the counter on his elbows. "2012. A case in Kita. A house on the river where a family kept getting sick and nobody could find out why. I was there for my reasons, he was there for the Division's. We spent four days in that house together. On the third night we were sitting in the kitchen at three in the morning, both terrified, both pretending not to be, and he said, *Do you have any food,* and I had a box of chocolate biscuits in my coat, and we ate the whole box." He smiled at the memory. "After that we were friends. Best friends, I think. He'd never have said it. He'd say *colleague* or *contact*. But he came here every Thursday for three years. You don't do that for a contact."

He touched the dried flower on his lapel without seeming to notice he was doing it.

"He talked about you," Haruki said. "All the time. Rei's exam. Rei's terrible cooking. Rei fell asleep on the sofa again. Rei notices everything. He was so proud of you it was embarrassing."

"He never told me about you."

"No." Haruki's smile went thin. "He never told anyone about anyone. He kept every part of his life in a separate box with a separate lock. I think he thought it kept us safe. All of us. You most of all."

"Did it?"

Haruki did not answer. He looked at the piano player instead.

"My family is the Tsukishiro," he said after a while. "Have you heard of us?"

"One of the Tower Families."

"The oldest. We've been doing this since before Kyoto was a capital. We have a big old house in Bunkyō with a lot of rooms and a lot of rules and a lot of things in the basement nobody talks about. I have a wing of it to myself, which sounds grand and mostly means I have my own bad heating." He shrugged. "I don't work for the family. I don't work for the Division. I don't really work for the Grid either. I work between them. I find things out. People pay me, sometimes. Mostly I make coffee." He tapped the counter. "This bar belongs to an old friend. He lets me stand behind it three nights a week. It's the only place in Tokyo where nobody asks me which side I'm on."

"What's the Grid?"

Haruki raised his eyebrows. "He really didn't tell you anything." He thought about it. "The Grid is everyone who can do what you can do and is not on the Division's list. People who never registered. People who registered and then left. People who don't trust the government to write down what they are." He looked at Rei. "People like you, right now."

[break What Haruki offered]

Rei told him everything. The knock at 3:47. The key. The archive. The woman on the phone. The man on Ōji platform with his face being worn from the inside. Okada and her card. Tanizaki and his two cups of tea. The old woman in the arcade in Jūjō. He had not said any of it out loud to anyone except Itsuki. Once he started, he could not stop. Haruki listened without interrupting. He made two more coffees while Rei talked, one for Rei, one for himself, and he did not drink his.

When Rei got to Tanizaki, Haruki laughed out loud for the first time.

"Two cups," he said. "He gave you two cups and sent you home."

"He said come back when I'd finished being in a hurry."

"He said that to Gin when Gin was nine." Haruki was still smiling. "And to me when I was twenty-five. It took me three visits to get the third cup. It took Gin, I think, about two years."

"I don't have two years."

"No." The smile faded. "No, you don't." He picked up his own cold coffee, looked at it, put it down again. "Okay. Here's what I can do, and here's what I can't. I can't find Gin tonight. I've been trying since last night. Whatever has him is quiet, and it's careful, and it's not leaving tracks I can read. I can't make the Division tell you the truth. And I can't teach you what Tanizaki teaches. Nobody can. That's his."

"Then what can you do?"

"I can walk with you." Haruki said it simply. "Wherever you go next, I can come too. I know the Grid. I know the families. I know which doors in this city are safe to knock on and which ones you only knock on once. I know how to keep you alive while you learn." He looked at the key on its cord. "And I knew your brother. I knew him better than almost anyone. That has to be worth something, when you're looking for him."

Rei looked at him. At the dried flower on his jacket. At the red edges of his eyes, and the small crooked smile that was trying very hard to hold.

"Why?" he said. "You don't know me."

"I know you a bit," said Haruki. "He talked about you every Thursday for three years." He shrugged again. "And I owe him a box of chocolate biscuits."

[break The konbini]
[need ch7_konbini_soup | 1am konbini in Nezu, Rei and Haruki standing at the window counter with hot cans of corn soup and fried chicken, bright flat lights, a construction worker choosing rice balls behind them]

It was after one in the morning when they came up the stairs into the street. Nezu was asleep. The lanes were dark except for a vending machine humming red and white at the corner, and the lights of a konbini at the end of the street, white and flat and bright.

"Come on," said Haruki. "Lesson one."

He walked Rei into the konbini. The door chimed. A sleepy clerk said *Irasshaimase* without looking up from his phone. The light inside was so bright after the bar that Rei squinted. The air conditioning was freezing. A radio played a pop song very quietly from somewhere in the ceiling.

Haruki bought two hot cans of corn soup from the warming cabinet and a bag of fried chicken from the glass case by the till. He handed Rei a can and a piece of chicken and made him stand by the window counter and eat it.

"Here?" said Rei.

"Here. Exactly here." Haruki pulled the ring on his own can. "Gin wrote about this in the archive. Did you get to it yet? The Kombini Protocol. He was very proud of the name."

Rei shook his head.

"The veil is thin where big things happen to people," said Haruki. "Deaths. Fights. Love. Grief. Old houses, hospitals, stations, shrines. Places full of feeling. And what does a konbini have?" He waved his chicken at the shelves of instant noodles and toothpaste and magazines. "Nothing. Nothing ever happens in a konbini. That's the whole point of it. Bright lights. Cold air. A clerk who doesn't care about you. Someone behind you trying to choose between two kinds of rice ball. It's the most ordinary place in the world. And ordinary is the strongest medicine there is for people like us."

Rei drank his soup. It was too sweet and too hot and it burned his tongue. Behind him a man in a construction worker's uniform was standing in front of the rice balls, reading every label, very slowly, as if it were the most important choice of his life.

And Rei felt it. Slowly, the way warmth comes back into cold hands. The scraped-out feeling he had carried since the arcade in Jūjō started to fill. The pressure behind his eyes, which had been there faintly for a week without his noticing, went quiet. The floor felt like a floor. The world felt like a place where nothing would knock on the door at 3:47.

"Oh," he said.

"Yeah." Haruki smiled at him, the crooked smile. "When you help a spirit, you spend yourself. That's the tiredness you felt in Jūjō. Your legs, your hands, the tunnel in your eyes. That comes back with sleep and food. But there's another thing that goes down, slower, deeper, every time you open that door in your chest. The thing that keeps you, you. Gin called it stability. When it gets low, things can get in." He tapped his can against Rei's. "This fills it back up. A hot can of soup at one in the morning under bad lights. Your brother came in here every night of his life after a bad case. I used to find him in the one by Ōji station, eating oden at the counter, staring at the magazines like they were a fire."

They stood at the window and ate fried chicken and watched the empty street. The man behind them finally chose a salmon rice ball, then put it back, and chose a tuna one.

"Same time next Friday?" said Haruki.

"Yes," said Rei. And then, because it was true, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the corn soup." Haruki crushed his can. "And go back to Tanizaki. Keep going back. Sit there and drink his tea and don't ask him for anything. It'll make you crazy. Do it anyway."

The last train had gone long ago. Rei walked to Ueno and took a taxi home, and watched the meter climb past three thousand yen with a sick feeling in his stomach, because he did not know any more how long his money had to last. He slept for nine hours without dreaming. When he woke up it was raining for the first time in two weeks, and his phone had one message on it, from a number he did not know.

*Tuesday. Café Asahi. Ni-chōme. After seven. Ask for the usual. H.*
