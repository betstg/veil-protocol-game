#title Hands That Make Tea
#part Book One. The Archive

*Gin.*

The first thing Gin knew was the smell of tea.

Not just any tea. Hōjicha, the roasted kind, brown and smoky, that he drank on bad nights because it had almost no caffeine in it. He knew the smell the way he knew his own name. It was the smell of the kitchen table at three in the morning, with the archive open and Rei asleep on the sofa in the next room.

He thought, for one warm second, that he was home.

Then he saw his hands.

They were pouring water from a kettle into a small clay teapot. They were doing it well. Slowly, in a thin steady stream, the way he always did, the way Tanizaki had taught him when he was a boy. Not too fast, or the leaves burn. Not too slow, or the water cools. His hands knew. They had done this ten thousand times.

He had not told them to.

He tried to stop. He thought, *Stop,* very clearly, the way you think *stand up* or *turn left*. Nothing happened. The water went on pouring. His right hand put the kettle down on a small gas ring. His left hand put the lid on the teapot. Neither of them asked him.

It was like sitting in the back seat of a car with the doors locked, watching someone else drive.

[break The room]

He did not know the room. It was a small kitchen with a sink, a gas ring and a window with a frosted glass pane. The walls were old yellow paper. A calendar hung on a nail. It was from a dry-cleaning shop, and it still showed July. Through the frosted glass he could see that it was night, and he could hear rain.

His body lifted the teapot and poured tea into one cup. One. Not two.

That was the thing that frightened him most, in the end. Not the locked doors. Not the hands. It was that the hands only poured one cup. Every night for nineteen years he had put out two cups, even when Rei was asleep, even when Rei was away, even when there was no one at all to drink the second one. His hands had always done it without asking him.

Now they did not. Something else was in charge of his habits now, and it had decided which ones to keep.

His body lifted the cup and drank. He tasted the tea. It was good tea, made properly. He could feel the heat of it go down his throat, all the way to his stomach. He could feel everything. He just could not do anything.

*You're awake,* said a voice.

It did not come from the room. It came from inside him, from somewhere behind his eyes, close and quiet. It was a man's voice, low and calm and very courteous, with an old-fashioned way of speaking, like a very old doctor at a very old hospital. It was the most reasonable voice Gin had ever heard. It sounded like someone who had never shouted in its life and never needed to.

*You should not be awake,* it said. *It is not good for you. Rest.*

*Who are you,* Gin thought. His thought came out ragged and small, like a shout under water. *What are you. Get out of me.*

*You know what I am,* the voice said kindly. *You have been studying things like me for six years. You wrote about me in your book. You simply did not know you were writing about me.*

*Where's Rei.*

The voice paused. It was a small pause. Gin felt it like a hand that tightens for a moment on your shoulder.

*Your brother is well,* it said. *He is at home. He is eating. He is sleeping. I have made sure of it.*

*Liar.*

*I do not lie,* said the voice, and it sounded almost hurt. *Lying is not in my nature. I keep my word. I discharge my obligations. That is all I am.*

On the table, beside the teapot, Gin's phone buzzed.

His eyes went to it. He could not stop them, and the voice did not stop them either. On the screen there was a message. He read it before anything could take it away.

*I helped someone today. In Jūjō. With salt and anpan. I think I did it right.*

Something happened inside Gin then. He did not have a word for it. It went through him like an electric shock, from his chest out to his fingers. It was pride and terror and grief all at once. *Jūjō. The arcade. The old woman by the watch shop. Oh, Rei. Oh, you idiot. You brilliant idiot.*

And for one second, one single second, the little finger of his left hand moved.

He felt it move. He moved it. It twitched against the wood of the table, one centimetre, toward the phone.

The voice went very quiet.

Then, calmly, without any hurry at all, his right hand reached out and turned the phone face down on the table.

*You should not be awake,* said the voice again. It was still kind. It was still courteous. But there was something under it now, something heavy and patient and cold, like the bottom of a very deep lake. *You have done enough. You have carried it for so long. Let me carry it now. That is what I am for.*

*Carry what,* Gin thought. *Carry what. What do you want.*

*What is owed,* said the voice.

The dark came up over him slowly, like warm water over a sleeper in a bath. He fought it. He fought it with everything he had. He held on to the smell of the tea, and the sound of the rain, and the words on the phone. *I think I did it right.* He held on to them the way a drowning man holds on to a rope.

The last thing he saw was his own face, in the dark glass of the window over the sink. Long black hair loose past the jaw. Tired eyes. And a small, gentle, satisfied smile that was not his, that he had never in his life made, that turned up only at the corners and did not touch the eyes at all.

Then nothing.

His hands washed the cup, and dried it, and put it away, one cup, on the shelf.
