#title The Table of Thirteen
#part Book Three. The Thirteen

The Grid Archive was a library that did not exist.

It was in Ochanomizu, a hilly part of central Tokyo full of universities and music shops and second-hand bookshops, on the fourth floor of an old office building above a shop that sold guitars. The sign on the door said *Kanda Translation Services*. Behind the door, there was a small dusty office with a desk and a dead plant. Behind the office, through a second door, there were books.

Rei had never seen so many books. Shelves from floor to ceiling, in rooms that went on and on, one after another, much further than the building could possibly reach. Old books in leather. New books in plastic covers. Notebooks, scrolls, boxes of letters, stacks of photographs tied with string. The air smelled of paper and dust and green tea. Somewhere, very faintly, someone was playing a cello.

"Everything the Division wants to forget," said the young woman who let him in, "we keep." She was small and quick, about thirty, with round glasses and her black hair in a long plait over one shoulder, and she was looking at Rei with an expression of total, delighted curiosity, as if he were a new book she could not wait to open. "Chen Lian. I hold Wonder. Mori said you'd come. Mori said you didn't hit him." She grinned. "Most people hit him. I hit him."

[break The table]

The meeting was in the last room. A round room, with a round table of very old dark wood, and thirteen chairs.

Twelve people were sitting at it. Or standing near it, or leaning on the shelves, or pacing. They did not look like anything. That was what struck Rei first. They did not look powerful or strange or old. They looked like the people in the empty swimming pool. Like people on a train.

Mori was there, sitting very straight, his arms folded. Hoshino was there, still in his apron, eating a rice ball and waving at Rei with it. A tall thin woman in her forties in a running jacket, with short grey hair and very bright nervous eyes that went to the door every few seconds. *Nakashima. Fear,* Chen Lian whispered. A soft, handsome man of about thirty in a cashmere sweater, smiling at everyone. *Sakuma. Love.* A woman in a severe black suit who would not look at anyone directly. *Kobayashi. Shame.* A broad cheerful woman in her forties in a construction company jacket. *Okabe. Courage.* A thin young Korean man with tired eyes who kept checking his phone. *Park. Longing.* And a man of about forty sitting cross-legged on top of a bookshelf, very high up, near the ceiling, with no ladder anywhere near him, looking down at them all with an expression of peaceful astonishment. *Kasumi. Awe. Don't ask how he got up there. Nobody knows. Not even him.*

One chair was empty because its holder was in Kyoto. *Asakura. Grief. She's on the phone.* There was a mobile phone lying face up on the table in front of that chair, with a call open and the timer counting.

One chair was empty because nobody had come yet.

And one chair was simply empty. It was set a little back from the table, as if pushed away. Its seat was dusty. Nobody looked at it.

Rei knew what it was before anyone told him.

[break Late]

The door opened, and a man came in who was not walking.

He was simply there. One moment the doorway was empty. The next, a tall, lean, long-limbed man in a jacket with many pockets was standing in it, with a bag over his shoulder, and a gust of cold air came in round him smelling of rain and petrol and somewhere else entirely. His hair was dark and unkempt. His face would have been handsome if it had ever stopped moving.

"Sorry," he said. "Osaka. Bad night. Something in the canal." He dropped into a chair. "What did I miss?"

"Inoue," said Chen Lian to Rei. "Hunger. He teleports. All the time. He never takes trains. He says trains make him itchy."

"Trains do make me itchy," said Inoue, without looking round. Then he did look round. At Rei. And stopped.

All of them stopped.

Rei felt it. Twelve people at a round table turning, one after another, to look at him, standing at the back of the room by Chen Lian. Not with their eyes. With something else. With whatever it was that let Hoshino feel half of Kita laughing at once. He felt them reach for him, twelve hands, all different, curious and warm and frightened and wary. He felt the latch on his wrist go so cold it burned, and he did not let the door in his chest move one millimetre.

The man on top of the bookshelf, Kasumi, said softly, "*Oh.*" Just that. With an expression of total wonder, like a man seeing the sea for the first time.

Nakashima, the woman with the nervous eyes, had gone white. "That's," she said. "Mori. That's..."

"I know what it is," said Mori.

"You brought it here? To the table?"

"*Him*," said Mori. "I brought *him*. Sit down, Yuna."

[break The last chair]

The last empty chair stayed empty for another twenty minutes, while they talked.

They talked about the city. About things Rei only half understood. About pressures, and currents, and thin places. About the Arakawa and the rats. About something in a canal in Osaka. About an increase, forty percent, in sightings across Tokyo, which the Division called *public anxiety* and they called something else. About Kita. Always, again and again, about Kita.

"It's colder every month," said Okabe, the woman in the construction jacket. "The Font's carrying the dark seat on its own. It can't do it forever. Something's going to break."

"Something already broke," said Park quietly, looking at his phone. "In August."

And then the door opened again, slowly, and the last holder came in.

He was in his mid thirties. Thin. Too thin. His clothes were good once, and old now, washed so many times they had gone soft and grey. His hair was dark and going grey unevenly, in streaks. His face was quiet and plain, the face of someone completely comfortable with silence. He sat down in the last chair without a word, and folded his hands on the table, and looked at nothing.

And Rei, standing at the back of the room with the door in his chest open one finger's width, felt the cold.

It was coming from him. From the thin man. Not from inside him. Through him. As if he were a window in a warm house, and someone had opened it, and the night was coming in. And with the cold came a feeling. Rei had felt it before. In the white room through Gorō's eyes. In the teacher's smile.

Despair.

Not sadness. Not grief. Something worse. A grey, flat, endless feeling. That nothing matters. That nothing will change. That trying is pointless and hope is a joke and everything you love will end and there is no reason, no reason at all, to get up in the morning.

It was pouring through the thin man like water through a broken dam. And it was not staying in him. It was going somewhere. Out of him, out of the room, west, across the city. A long thin grey thread, pulling and pulling, like a mouth at a straw.

"*Watanabe,*" whispered Chen Lian. "*Despair.*"

Rei did not mean to speak. Mori had told him. *You don't speak unless you're spoken to.* But he could see it so clearly. The grey thread going west, pulling. The thin man going paler, very slowly, as he sat there. Being drunk.

"He's failing," he said.

Every head turned.

[break What Rei saw]

"Who said that?" said Kobayashi, the woman in the black suit. She still did not look at him. She looked at the table. "Who said that? Who brought a child to this table to say things like that?"

"Emi," said Mori.

"No. No, Mori. I want to know. I want to know who this is, that you've brought here, who looks at one of us and says *failing*." Her voice was sharp and thin and shaking. "Do you know what we are? Do you know what it costs to sit here? Do you know what Jun gives up, every day, holding that? Holding *despair*, for the whole city, so that the rest of them can get up in the morning?" She turned, finally, and looked at Rei. Her eyes were wet. "And you come in here, with that thing in you, that empty thing, and you say..."

"Something's drinking from him," said Rei.

The room went silent.

"There's a thread," said Rei. He could hear his voice shaking. He did not stop. "A grey thread. Going out of him. West. Across the city. Something at the other end is pulling it. Like a straw. It's not his fault. He's not weak. Something is eating him from a long way away, and he's letting it, because if he fights it, the current will spill." He looked at the thin man. "Isn't it?"

Watanabe Jun lifted his head and looked at Rei for the first time.

His eyes were very tired. Very kind. Completely calm. The calm of a man who has looked at the very bottom of everything and come back up and sat down at a table to talk about it.

"Yes," he said. His voice was quiet and soft and slow. "Since last spring. A little more every month." He looked down at his folded hands. "I didn't tell anyone. There was nothing anyone could do."

"Jun," said Mori. Very low.

"I'm sorry, Takao." Watanabe did not look up. "I thought I could hold it until someone came for the dark seat. I thought if the seat was filled, the Font would have more to give, and I'd be all right." He smiled, very faintly. "I'm not sure I can hold it that long."

"What's at the other end?" said Okabe. "Of the thread. Jun. What's drinking you?"

Watanabe shook his head. "I don't know. Something old. Something that knows despair the way I do. From the inside." He looked at the table. "It feels like it's sitting in a white room, very quietly, pouring tea."

Rei's hands went cold.

He looked at Mori. Mori was looking at him.

[break The chair]

At the end, at three in the morning, when the others had gone, Mori stood up and walked round the table to the empty chair. The one pushed back. The one with dust on its seat. He put one big hand on the back of it.

"This is the Duty seat," he said. "Empty since 2009. The current under it is being held by the Font alone. That's why Kita is cold. That's why things are getting through. That's why your brother went looking." He looked at Rei. "The seat has been calling for someone with the right shape for six years. Your brother had the shape. A little. Enough that a thing of Duty found him and climbed into him in August. Because he was close to the shape, and the shape was empty, and things of Duty go where the shape is."

Rei could not breathe.

"You have it more," said Mori. "Much more. Hoshino felt it. Kasumi felt it from the top of a bookshelf. The fire felt it." He took his hand off the chair. "I am inviting you to the Thirteen, Uedera Rei. For the Duty seat."

The room was very quiet. Far away, the cello had stopped.

"You can't sit in it," said Mori. "Not yet. Not for a long time. If you sat in it now, the current would go through you like a train and leave nothing standing. You'd be ash." He said it flatly. "To hold a seat you have to be Guardian rank. All the way up. And you have to have every path open. All six. Every hand. Because a holder has to be able to use everything, to hold anything. That's the rule. It's always been the rule." He looked at Rei. "You've got two paths half open. You've got a long way to go."

"How long?"

"Years, usually." Mori picked up his coat. "Watanabe doesn't have years. The city doesn't have years." He put the coat on. "So you'd better be quicker than usual."

He walked to the door. Chen Lian was waiting there, turning off the lamps one by one.

"Mori-san," said Rei. "Duty. That's what I am. Isn't it."

Mori stopped at the door.

"Hoshino wouldn't tell you," he said. "He thinks you should find it yourself."

"I'm asking you."

Mori looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked at the empty chair.

"People don't usually know," he said. "What moves them. Most people go their whole lives and never find out. They think they're one thing and they're another." He turned back to the door. "You walked into a bath house and a temple and a cat-filled flat and a folded building and a white light. Nobody asked you to. Nobody paid you enough. You went because somebody had to, and you were there." He went out. His voice came back from the dark corridor. "You tell me what that is."

Rei stood alone in the round room with the twelve chairs and the one empty one, and the dust on its seat, and the dark books going away on every side.

He did not sit in it. He did not touch it.

But he looked at it for a long time.
