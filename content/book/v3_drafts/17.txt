#title Two Sides
#part Book Two. The Families

Without Itsuki the days had a hole in them. Rei filled it with work.

He went to the police station three days a week and sorted files. He went to Tanizaki on Saturdays and sat for an hour, and then to Tsuru-no-yu and sat in the hot water and then in Hara Setsu's kitchen and told her how he was. Properly. *Not fine.* On Fridays he went to Nezu and drank Haruki's coffee. On Tuesdays he went to Café Asahi, and Matsuda Keiko put toast and coffee in front of him and, some weeks, a folded square of paper.

The squares of paper were work. Grid work. A family in Ōji whose new baby screamed every night at three o'clock, in the room where the grandmother had died. A taxi driver whose car kept smelling of a perfume his dead wife used to wear, and who wanted it to stop, and then cried when it did. A small temple in Takinogawa where the bell rang by itself at dusk. They paid ten thousand yen, twenty, once forty. Sometimes they paid in food. The family with the baby gave him a whole box of mandarin oranges and a hand-knitted hat for Gin.

He was getting better. He did not fall over afterwards any more. He opened the door in his chest slowly, a finger's width, the way Sōun had taught him, and closed it slowly, and only shook a little. He went to the konbini after every job and ate something hot under the bright lights and felt himself fill up again.

It was still not enough money.

[break The watch]

[need ch17_pawn_shop | A small pawn shop in Ueno at evening, glass counter full of old watches and cameras, an old man with a jeweller's loupe examining a plain silver wristwatch, Rei on the other side of the counter not looking at it]

The rent for November was due on the twenty-seventh of October. On the twentieth Rei counted everything he had and was thirty-one thousand yen short.

He sat for a long time at the kitchen table that night. Then he went into Gin's room and opened the top drawer of the desk.

Gin's watch was in there. A plain silver Seiko with a black leather strap, the kind every salaryman in Japan owns. Gin had bought it on his first day at the Division, six years ago, with his first salary, and worn it every day since. He had taken it off on the twelfth of August, the morning before he disappeared. Rei did not know why. He had found it on the desk next to the lamp, face down.

He held it in his hand. It was not like the key. It was not cold. It did not steady him. It was only a watch.

That was what he kept telling himself, all the way to Ueno.

The pawn shop was in a narrow street behind the station, between a shop that sold fishing rods and a shop that sold wigs. An old man with a jeweller's glass screwed into his eye looked at the watch for a long time, and turned it over, and opened the back with a tiny knife, and closed it again.

"Thirty-five thousand," he said. "It's a good one. Somebody looked after it."

"Yes," said Rei. "Somebody did."

He signed the paper. He took the money. He walked out into the street and stood by the fishing rod shop with the notes in his pocket and felt sick. He told himself he could buy it back. He told himself Gin would understand. He told himself Gin would have done the same.

All of that was true. It did not help at all.

[break The cat]

That night he sent Kato a cat.

They met at the family restaurant in Itabashi at eleven. Kato was already in the corner booth with his back to the wall and two melon sodas. He looked up as Rei sat down and put his king piece on the table.

"You sold something," he said.

"Don't."

"Sorry. You're thinking about it really loudly." Kato pushed a melon soda across. "It was his, wasn't it."

"I said don't."

"Okay." Kato held up both hands. "Okay. Not doing it. Thinking about the weather. It's very nice weather. October. Lovely." He picked up a menu and held it in front of his face. From behind it he said, "I'm sorry about the watch."

Rei laughed, despite himself. It came out shaky. "You're an idiot."

"I'm a very useful idiot." Kato put the menu down. His face was serious now. "I found out what *upstairs* means."

[break Upstairs]

"Our building in Akabane has nine floors," said Kato. He was speaking very quietly, though the nearest table was empty. "If you look at the directory in the lobby, it lists eight. One to eight. Personnel, Investigation, Field, Assessment, Records, all that. There's no nine." He turned the king between his fingers. "The lifts only go to eight. The stairs stop at eight. There's a door on the eighth floor at the end of the corridor with no handle on the outside and a card reader that's never been used by anyone I've ever seen."

"So what's on nine?"

"Nobody says. Nobody even thinks about it, most of the time. Which is weird, right? A whole floor nobody thinks about. I listened for two weeks. In the lifts. In the canteen. In the copy room." He put the king down. "And every so often, somebody would think one word, very fast, and then think about something else. Like stepping off a hot stone."

"What word?"

"*Protocol.*"

Rei frowned. "Veil Protocol? Like the name of the... like everything?"

"The Division calls itself the Civil Veil Division on the posters. But in the old paperwork, the stuff from the seventies, it's the Civil Veil Division, *Veil Protocol section*. Like a department. I thought it was just the old name." Kato looked at the table. "It's not. It's still there. It's a whole department inside the Division that doesn't appear on any directory. And they don't police the veil. Not really. They study it." He swallowed. "They study us. People who can see. They keep a list of everyone. Registered and unregistered. What they can do. Where they live. Who their family is."

Rei thought of the man in the expensive suit, standing up in the empty pool. *They have a list.*

"The Grid says that," he said slowly. "Somebody at the Gathering said exactly that."

"Yeah, well." Kato did not smile. "The Grid's right more often than the Division likes to admit." He hesitated. "Your brother's file went upstairs. That's what they meant. Not that he's dead. That he's become something Protocol is interested in."

"Interested how?"

"I don't know yet." Kato met his eyes. "But I know nobody comes down."

[break The trade]

Before they left, Kato took a folded paper from his jacket.

"This is the Division's list of every thin spot reported in Kita and Itabashi in the last month," he said. "Thirty-one of them. Fourteen are marked for response. Seventeen are marked *monitor only*." He pushed it across. "Seventeen places where something's coming through and nobody official is going to do anything about it."

Rei looked at the paper. Then at Kato.

"You want me to give this to the Grid."

"I didn't say that." Kato stood up and put on his jacket. "I said I was giving it to you. What you do with it is your business. I'm just a trainee who makes copies." He put a thousand-yen note on the table. "But if somebody in an empty swimming pool happened to know where seventeen thin spots were, I bet a lot of grandmothers would sleep better."

"And what does the Grid give you back?"

Kato stopped. He thought about it.

"There's a woman in Shin-Ōkubo who reads situations faster than anyone the Division has ever trained," he said. "She worked with Gin once. Choi something. If she ever wanted to tell somebody what she knows about the Vessel Program, I'd want to listen." He shrugged. "I'm not asking for anything. I'm just saying what I'd want. If I were asking."

Rei watched him go out under the green sign.

He understood, sitting there with the paper in his hand, what Switch had meant. *You can't sit on the edge of the pool forever.* He was not sitting on the edge. He was standing in the middle, with one foot on each side, carrying things across. From the Division to the Grid. From the Grid to the Division. Like a boy carrying water in his hands from one bucket to another, and losing a little every time.

He thought of Itsuki. *They're going to use you.*

He thought, maybe I'm using them too.

He did not know if that made it better or worse.

[break Kabukichō]

He gave the list to Switch on the Thursday, by hand, in the doorway of a closed launderette in Higashi-Jūjō that was that week's message point. Switch read it standing up, all of it, without any expression. Then they folded it into their coat.

"Seventeen," they said. "That'll keep us busy."

"I want something back," said Rei.

Switch looked at him with mild interest, as if a cat had asked for the newspaper. "Go on."

"Kanna-sensei. Where she is. How she finds people."

"I don't know where she is. She moves." Switch was quiet for a moment. "But I know how she finds people. She sends someone to Kabukichō. To the kids."

[need ch17_toyoko | Kabukichō at night, the plaza by the cinema tower crowded with Toyoko kids, teenagers in dark hoodies and pale make-up sitting on the pavement with phones, neon reflected on wet ground, a stocky sixteen-year-old girl with buzzed sides and a hoodie looking up warily]

Kabukichō is the entertainment district on the other side of Shinjuku station, the opposite of Ni-chōme. It is enormous and loud and bright all night, full of bars and host clubs and karaoke and love hotels and huge glowing signs. In the middle of it stands a tall cinema tower with a giant model of Godzilla's head looking down over the roofs. And on the pavement at the foot of that tower, every night, sit the Toyoko kids.

That is what the city calls them. Teenagers who have run away from home, or been thrown out, or have a home they do not want to go back to. They come from all over Japan. They sit on the ground by the cinema in dark clothes and heavy make-up, in groups, with their phones, and they sleep in net cafés or on friends' floors or in the rooms of strangers who are not their friends. Some of them are fourteen. The police move them on. They come back.

Switch had given him a name. Rei found her on the third night, sitting on the kerb with her back against a lamp post, eating crisps out of a bag.

She was sixteen. Stocky and solid, in a huge black hoodie, with her hair shaved short at the sides and longer on top. She had three phones lined up on her knee. She did not look up when his shadow fell over her.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked anything."

"You're going to. Everyone's going to. Answer's no." She ate a crisp. "Unless you're paying. Then the answer's maybe."

"Switch sent me."

That made her look up. Her eyes were small and sharp and very tired, and they went over him fast, the way Choi Hana's had. Not the same sight. Just the plain hard look of someone who has had to read strangers to stay alive.

"Yua," she said, after a moment. "Five thousand for the first answer. Cash. And you buy me a hamburger."

"What if the answer's no good?"

"Then you've bought a nice girl a hamburger." She stood up and put the phones in three different pockets. "Come on. I'm starving. And you look like you are too."

They sat in the window of a hamburger place and she ate two burgers and a large fries and he paid five thousand yen of Gin's watch and she told him about the woman.

"She comes on Sundays," said Yua. "Nice coat. Nice voice. Thirties. She doesn't go for the loud ones. She goes for the quiet ones. The ones who sit on their own. The ones who say they hear things, or see things, or feel things nobody else does." She ate a fry. "She gives them a card with a little white flower on it and says, there's a place where people like you can rest. Where nobody will laugh at you. A tea house. A teacher who understands."

"Do they go?"

"Some of them." Yua looked out of the window at the crowd going past. Her face did not change, but her hand stopped halfway to the fries. "My friend Mina went. In June. She hears voices. Real ones, I mean. Not crazy. She came back in August." She ate the fry. "She doesn't hear them any more. She doesn't hear anything any more. She sits in the corner of the net café and smiles at the wall."

Rei felt the cold come up his back.

"Do you have the card?"

Yua looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached into her hoodie and took out a small square of thick white card and put it on the table. On it was printed a single small white flower. A chrysanthemum. Under it, in tiny grey letters, three words.

*The Far Shore.*

"That one's free," said Yua. "For Mina." She picked up her drink. "Don't go there. People who go there come back empty." She sucked on her straw and looked at him over the top of it, and for one second she looked her age. Sixteen. "You look like you'd go anyway."

"I might."

"Then buy me another hamburger first," said Yua. "In case you don't come back."
