#title Gorō
#part Book Two. The Families

After that, every time Rei was in the Akabane building, he went up to the roof.

He did not go every day at first. There was too much else. A trainee at the Division had to be everywhere at once. He sat in Records with Okada and learned the boring half of three hundred files. He sat in meetings with Field Operations and Reintegration and Assessment and Public Confidence, one after another, at the bottom of long tables, and was introduced, and bowed, and forgot everyone's name, and had to be introduced again. On Thursdays he went to Nezu, to the Kuroda house, and sat in the cold library with Grandmother Mitsu's tea and read the books Sōun put in front of him until his eyes hurt. On Saturdays he went to Tanizaki. In between, Haruki took him round the Grid like a cousin being shown off at a wedding, a shop in Ueno, a bar in Golden Gai, a bathhouse, a temple, a back room behind a pharmacy, and everywhere there was someone older to bow to and someone who decided things and someone who wanted to look at Gin's brother. Every group had its ladder. The Division. The Grid. The families. Even the street kids had theirs. He was at the bottom of every one, and he had to learn all of them at once.

But whenever he had half an hour in the Akabane building, he climbed the last flight of stairs past the sign that said SERIOUSLY, and pushed open the steel door, and stood at the low wall and looked at the city.

And every time, within a few minutes, the black and white crow came.

It never came straight to him. It landed on the water tank first, or on top of the humming air conditioning boxes, and sat there pretending to look at something else. Then it walked along the wall, closer and closer, very casually, as if it just happened to be going that way. Then it stopped a metre from him and looked at his pocket.

Rei always brought two rice balls now. One for himself and one for the crow. The crow always took his.

After the first week it started bringing him things. A bottle cap, green, from a melon soda. A screw. A bit of red ribbon. A child's plastic hair clip shaped like a strawberry. It dropped them on the wall beside his hand and stepped back and waited, very stiffly, to see what he would do. Rei put them in his pocket, and said thank you, and the crow made a small satisfied noise in its throat, like a crow who has been proved right about something.

He talked to it. He did not mean to, at first. But it was easier than talking to people. He told it about the files in Records, and Okada's finger tapping the page. He told it about Kato listening to everyone in the lift and pretending not to. He told it about the cactus on the clean desk by the window. The crow listened with its head on one side, the blue eye on top, and sometimes, when he stopped, it made the creaky-door noise, as if to tell him to go on.

[break The head of the aviary]

In the second week of January he found out where it came from.

He was on the roof at lunchtime with the crow beside him on the wall, eating its rice ball with its back turned, when the steel door crashed open behind them. An old woman came out onto the roof, breathing hard from the stairs. She was small and sturdy, in a padded green jacket and rubber boots, with a feed bucket in one hand and a long-handled net in the other.

She saw the crow. She saw Rei. She stopped.

"*There* you are," she said to the crow, in the voice of a mother who has found her child on the roof of a shopping centre. "Four flights. Four flights of stairs, at my age, because the girl in Dispatch saw you out of the window." She pointed the net at Rei. "And who are you? Are you feeding him?"

"Tuna mayonnaise," said Rei. "Sorry. Is he yours?"

"He's nobody's," said the old woman. "That's the whole problem." She sat down heavily on an upturned crate by the water tank and put her bucket between her boots. "I run the aviary. Downstairs, behind the training camp. That's Gorō. He's one of mine. He lives in the aviary with sixty other crows and twelve owls, and he's the only one who keeps getting out, and he's the only one I can never catch."

The crow, Gorō, turned round on the wall and looked at her with the black eye. Then he went back to his rice ball.

"We train them here," said the old woman. "In the building. Every crow in that aviary was hatched in this Division or brought in young. We teach them to sit on a mirror, to go where they're sent, to come back. To find the cold places. He was trained like all the rest." She looked at Gorō with deep dislike. "Badly. He learned everything we taught him and then decided he didn't want to do any of it. He bites the handlers. He steals. He undoes my knots. He went into the Director's office in October through an open window and took a pen off his desk and dropped it in the pond in the camp. Nobody will work with him. I've stopped asking them to."

"He's never bitten me," said Rei.

The old woman looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Gorō, who had finished the rice ball and was now standing very close to Rei's elbow on the wall, so close that his feathers touched Rei's sleeve.

"No," she said slowly. "I can see that."

She got up, and picked up her bucket, and did not even try with the net.

"Bring him down when you go," she said. "If he'll come. If he won't, leave the steel door open a crack. He knows the way home. He just likes to make me climb the stairs."

[break Forms]

[img cvd_camp.png | The training camp]

After that, Rei went down to the aviary as well as up to the roof.

The aviary was in the training camp, the strange walled green that you reached through a fire door on the third floor, a door with a running man on it that should have opened onto a stairwell and opened onto grass instead. Trainees did forms there on Tuesday and Friday mornings. Forms were the slow, careful movements every practitioner learned first, how to stand, how to breathe, how to open and close, so that your body would know what to do when something frightened you, before you had time to think. Rei did them badly, in a tracksuit, in the cold, in a row of trainees all doing them better than him. Afterward, with his arms aching and grass on his knees, he went and stood at the aviary wire.

The aviary ran the whole length of one wall of the camp, three metres high, full of noise. Crows hopping on the perches and squabbling over food and shouting at each other in the cold. At the far end, in the shadows under a wooden roof, the owls sat in a row with their eyes shut, round and grey and silent as stones.

And every time, from the top corner where he sat alone, Gorō came down to the wire.

To everyone else he was the same as ever. He bit a trainee from Assessment who tried to stroke him through the wire. He stole the head of the aviary's glasses off her nose and dropped them in the water trough. He opened a feed bin and threw the contents out, beakful by beakful, onto the heads of the sleeping owls. But to Rei he brought bottle caps.

Once, while Rei was standing there, a handler came out of the side door of the aviary with a crow on her gloved wrist. A senior officer from Field Operations, a tall grey-haired woman Rei had never spoken to. She walked to the middle of the green, lifted her arm, and said something quiet. The crow went up off her wrist in three strong beats and away over the wall, straight as a thrown stone, toward the east. She watched it go. Then she went back inside without looking at anyone.

"That's hers," said Aoi, who was doing forms beside him. "Her own crow. Twenty years together. There are maybe four people in the building who have that." She turned her bead bracelet. "The rest of us borrow."

[break The mirror room]

[img cvdroom_mirror_room.png | The mirror room]

Shimizu showed him what *borrow* meant, on a Friday, after forms.

There was a door beside the camp door that Rei had never noticed. Behind it was a small dim room, and every wall of it, from the floor to the ceiling, was covered with mirrors.

Mirrors of every shape and size and age. Round ones and square ones and long narrow ones. Mirrors in heavy carved frames and mirrors with no frames at all. A cracked bathroom mirror. A little round one from a woman's handbag. Hundreds of them, fitted together edge to edge like the scales of a fish, so close that there was no wall left showing between them. A single wooden chair stood in the middle of the floor, facing them.

And not one of the mirrors showed the room.

Each one showed somewhere else. A different piece of the city, seen from above, moving. A rooftop in Itabashi with laundry flapping on a line. A street corner in Ikebukuro with people crossing at the lights. The surface of a river, very close, with the light shaking on it. A temple roof. A car park. A schoolyard with children running in it. A train going over a bridge. A branch of a tree, swaying, and through the leaves a window, and through the window a woman sitting at a table with her head in her hands. Hundreds of small moving pictures of Tokyo, all at once, all silent, glowing softly in the dark room like windows in a building at night.

Rei stood in the doorway and could not speak.

"Every mirror in this room is bound to a crow," said Shimizu, behind him. "When the binding is done, the mirror shows what that crow sees. Always. Day and night. Wherever it is." She came in past him and stood with her hands in her pockets, looking at the wall. "Most of these crows are out on a job right now. Watching a house. Following a car. Sitting on a roof opposite somebody's window. If you need to see what one of them sees, you sign the mirror out, and you sit in that chair, and you look."

"They're all from the aviary?"

"All of them. Raised here, trained here, bound here, by the seal-makers upstairs." She nodded at a few of the mirrors near the ceiling, older and darker than the rest, in heavy frames. "Those ones were made specially, by a medium, years ago, because the Division asked. They see further. Nobody touches those without a form from the eighth floor." She was quiet for a moment. "And a handful of the mirrors aren't here. They're in somebody's desk drawer, or somebody's coat pocket. Those are the people who have their own crow. Who don't have to borrow."

Rei looked at the hundreds of small bright pictures of the city, moving silently on the walls. Somewhere in among them, he thought, there might be a mirror that showed a branch outside a white house on a quiet street. Or a dark river under a bridge. Or a third-floor walkway on a hill above Ōji.

"Does Gorō have one?" he said.

Shimizu looked at him sideways.

"Gorō has never let anyone finish binding him," she said. "Eight people have tried. He's bitten all eight." She turned back to the wall. "There's no mirror in this room for Gorō."

[break Asking]

It took him another week to ask.

He had been thinking about it every day. He had not said it to anyone. It seemed much too big a thing to ask, for a trainee at the bottom of every ladder, when there were maybe four people in the whole building who had a crow of their own.

But on a Friday in the middle of January, standing at the aviary wire after forms, with Gorō on the other side of it pushing a melon soda cap through the mesh toward his hand, he turned to the head of the aviary, who was filling the water trough with a hose, and he said it.

"Could I have him?" he said.

The head of the aviary turned off the hose and looked at him for a long time.

"Him," she said. "That one."

"Yes."

"You know what he is. He's a menace. He's a thief and a vandal and he bites."

"He doesn't bite me."

The old woman looked at Gorō on his perch, with a stolen hair clip in his beak, looking back at her with the black eye. Then she looked at Rei again, at his new suit and his tired face and the bottle caps in his pocket.

"It's not mine to give, a crow," she said at last. "It's his. But the aviary's mine." She picked up her bucket. "If you can train him, he's yours. Three days of food from your hand. Then the mirror, at dusk, with a seal-maker who knows what he's doing." She started to walk away, and then stopped. "And if he bites you halfway through the rite, don't come crying to me. I'll have told you."

[break Three days]

He did not bite.

Rei came every day for three days, at lunchtime, with a boiled egg from the canteen in his pocket. The old woman said Gorō liked eggs best. Rei stood in the aviary and held out his hand with the egg on it, and on the first day Gorō sat on the top perch and looked at the egg for ten minutes, very rudely, as if it had insulted his family. Then he came down and took it, and flew back up, and ate it with his back turned.

On the second day he came down in two minutes and ate the egg standing on Rei's wrist, his claws cold and tight through the shirt sleeve, his heavy beak knocking against Rei's palm.

On the third day he was waiting at the wire when Rei came in. He took the egg. Then he did not fly away. He sat on Rei's wrist after the egg was gone, and looked at the latch tattoo that showed at the edge of Rei's cuff. Looked at it with the blue eye for a long time. Then he pressed the side of his head against it, once, briefly, and made a small low sound in his throat that was not a *kaa* at all.

"He's never made that noise," said the head of the aviary, through the wire.

"What does it mean?"

"How should I know? He's never made it." She went back to her bucket. "Tomorrow at dusk. Bring your seal-maker."

[break The seal]

The seal-maker was Uruma.

Rei found him in the Sigil Room on the fifth floor, at the far end, where the sheets of drying seal paper hung thickest. Uruma was sitting cross-legged on top of a table in a long dark apron, grinding ink on a stone, and humming the song about the boat.

"Gin-kun's little brother," he said, without looking up. "With a crow. *That* crow. The one who stole my best brush in the spring and never gave it back." He giggled his small high giggle. "The whole building is talking about it. Somebody in Accounts is taking bets on how long you keep your ear. Kato has put a thousand yen on *forever*."

"Can you make the seal?"

"Of course I can make the seal. I make all the seals. I'm the only one in this building who can write a straight line." Uruma stopped grinding and held up the inkstone so Rei could see. The ink in it was not black. It was a dark, dull, earthy grey-brown. "Charcoal," said Uruma, "and earth from a graveyard, and water. I went to Yanaka this morning and dug it up myself with a spoon. A policeman asked me what I was doing. I told him." He giggled again. "He went away very quickly."

He put the stone down. He took a strip of pale seal paper from a drawer, about the length of a hand. He laid a fine brush beside it. And a small, clean, very sharp knife.

"The character is *sight*," he said. "視. It has to be written in your blood. Not mine. Yours. The crow is giving you something, so you have to give something back, or the bargain doesn't hold." He held out his long black-nailed hand. "Give me your finger. Left hand. Not the thumb, you need the thumb."

Rei gave him his finger.

Uruma pricked it so quickly and so neatly that Rei did not feel it until the blood came. A bright round red bead on the tip of his finger. Uruma dipped the brush into it. Then he did a strange thing. He put the brush into Rei's right hand, and closed his own long fingers round Rei's, from behind, so that they held the brush together.

"The Symbol Work is mine," he said quietly, close to Rei's ear. For once he did not giggle. "You haven't got enough of it yet. The wanting is yours. I can't want him for you." His hand was cool and dry and perfectly steady. "Think about the crow. Not about the character. Let me worry about the character. You think about him sitting on your wrist with his head against your arm."

Rei thought about it.

The brush moved. Uruma moved it, and Rei moved with it, and the character came out on the pale paper in Rei's blood, dark red, seven clean strokes, and Rei felt each stroke go into the paper like a small key going into a small lock.

When it was done Uruma let go and sat back and looked at it.

"Oh, that's good," he said softly. "That's very good. That's better than your brother's." He giggled. "Don't tell him I said that. He'll sulk."

He gave Rei the seal, wrapped in a clean sheet of paper. Then three lengths of black cord, each as long as Rei's forearm.

The mirror the head of the aviary had given him that morning, from a cupboard at the back of the mirror room, where they kept the ones nobody had bound yet. An empty mirror. It showed nothing but the room in front of it, like any other. A square of plain glass about thirty centimetres across in a cheap pine frame, with one corner chipped, that looked as if it had come off the door of a wardrobe in somebody's grandmother's house. Uruma turned it over, and painted the back of it black with a wide brush in four quick strokes, and blew on it.

"Dark-backed," he said. "Works better. Nobody knows why. Gin-kun thought it was because the other side likes it darker." He shrugged his long shoulders. "Dusk. Not a minute before. Not a minute after. And Rei-kun."

"Yes."

"If it goes wrong, you don't get another try with him. Not ever. You'd need a different crow." He looked at Rei with his dark still amused eyes. "So don't let it go wrong. I've grown fond of your ear."

[break Dusk]

At half past four the next afternoon, the winter light began to go.

Rei knelt in the cold grass of the training camp in front of the aviary, with his coat on over his suit. The head of the aviary had let Gorō out into the green, which she said she would regret. He was sitting on the wire roof of the aviary, hunched against the cold, watching. Shimizu stood a little way off with her hands in her pockets. Uruma had come too, in an enormous black coat, and was sitting on an upturned bucket eating rice crackers, which the head of the aviary disapproved of, loudly.

Rei laid the mirror face-up on the grass. The sky went into it, pale and darkening, with one thin cloud.

He unwrapped the seal. He laid it on the glass face down, so the blood character pressed against the mirror. Then he took a boiled egg out of his pocket, peeled it, and set it on the edge of the frame.

The sky in the mirror turned from pale to grey to violet. The lights came on in the windows of the building that went round the camp, which should not have been there, and was. Somewhere a train went by on the real railway lines outside. And the light reached that strange in-between point it reached every evening, the moment the old people called *ōma-ga-toki*, when it is not day and not night and you cannot quite see the face of the person walking toward you.

"Now," said Uruma quietly, round a rice cracker.

Rei looked up at the roof of the aviary.

Gorō looked down at him.

For a long moment the crow did not move. Then he dropped off the roof, opened his patched black-and-white wings, and came down in one low, slow swoop across the darkening grass and landed, with a small hard click of claws on glass, in the very middle of the mirror.

He stood on his own reflection. He looked at the egg. He did not eat it. He looked at Rei.

Rei picked up the first black cord. He laid it across the mirror from edge to edge, very slowly, past Gorō's feet, not touching him. Then the second. Then the third. Three black lines across the glass, and the crow standing among them, perfectly still.

And he spoke the rite. Quietly. The old words from Gin's archive, that he had learned by heart in the flat on the hill over three nights. He did not understand all of them. He did not need to. He said them the way you say a name you have been practising. He opened the door in his chest, carefully, a finger's width, and then a little more, until he felt the latch on his wrist go cold and hold.

Gorō bent his head and looked down into the mirror, at himself.

And the mirror changed.

[img animal_crow.png | The crow and the mirror]

Then the dark glass stopped showing the violet sky. It showed grass. Very close. And Rei's own face, from above and to the side, huge and pale and serious, with his mouth moving. A strange view. Too sharp at the edges. Too bright. Full of small details no person would notice, the shine of a button, a loose thread on his coat, the pulse moving in his neck. It was how a crow saw him.

And it stayed. The violet sky did not come back into the glass. The mirror went on showing the grass and Rei's own face from above, and then, as Gorō turned his head, the aviary wire, and the wall, and the lit windows of the building. Whatever the crow looked at, the glass showed.

Uruma stopped eating his rice cracker.

"There," he said softly.

Gorō hopped off the mirror onto the grass. He ate the egg, in three furious gulps, as if he had been made to wait for it far too long. Then he flew up onto Rei's shoulder and took his ear in his beak again, quite hard this time, and held it.

"Ow," said Rei.

"That's the bargain," said Uruma, standing up off his bucket, enormously tall against the last of the light. "You've got him. He's got you. Nobody said he was going to be nice about it." He giggled, and put the rice crackers in his coat pocket, and looked at the crow on Rei's shoulder with something that was almost tenderness. "Look after him, Gorō. His brother would never forgive either of us."

The head of the aviary picked up her bucket and walked back toward the aviary, shaking her head.

"Eight people," she said, to nobody. "Four years. I'll be damned." At the wire she stopped. "Uedera. The mirror's supposed to live in the mirror room with the others."

"Yes."

"Nobody else is ever going to sign that one out." She sniffed. "Take it home. Don't tell anyone I said so."

[break Open]

Rei looked at the crow on his shoulder. He looked at the aviary, at the dozens of crows behind the wire, shouting and squabbling, and at the empty top corner where Gorō had sat, alone, for four years.

"Can he stay out?" he said. "Not in there. Out."

The head of the aviary turned round.

"If he flies off," she said, "he's gone. That's all. He's a wild bird with a bit of training on him. There's nothing in a mirror that makes him come back."

"I know."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she shrugged.

"He's yours," she said. "I told you."

Rei lifted his arm. Gorō walked down it to his wrist, and sat there, heavy and cold-footed, and looked at him.

"Go on," said Rei.

Gorō went.

He went straight up, in four huge beats of his patched wings, up past the bare trees and over the wall of the green that should not have been there, and up again, into the violet sky over Akabane, and away east, getting smaller, a black and white speck over the railway lines, and then nothing.

Rei stood in the cold grass and watched the place where he had been.

"Well," said Uruma, after a while, gently. "That's the ear safe, anyway."


[break First flight]

He was on the balcony when Rei got home.

Sitting on the railing outside the door of the flat on the hill, in the dark, under the walkway light, with his feathers fluffed up against the cold and something in his beak. When Rei came up the stairs he dropped it at Rei's feet. It was a bottle cap.

Rei stood on the walkway and laughed until the woman in 204 opened her door to see what was wrong.

He tried the mirror that night, in the flat on the hill, alone, because Shimizu said you should always try the first one somewhere you can lie down afterward.

He sat on the floor with the mirror on his knees and the window open, and the cold coming in. Gorō sat on the railing of the balcony outside, in the dark, his white patches very pale under the walkway light. Rei looked into the black glass and opened the door in his chest and thought, very clearly, *show me.*

And he was flying.

It was so sudden and so huge that he cried out. The flat fell away beneath him. The hill. The station. The railway lines, a bright tangle of silver going away north and south. The whole of Kita spread out under him in the dark like a cloth sewn with lights. Cold air under the wings. The tiny hard strength of a body that weighed nothing. And the sight. Gorō's sight. The sharp black eye that saw every lit window and every moving thing, and the pale blue eye that saw something else. Something under the lights. A faint shimmer over the river. A cold place by the old water tower, where something unquiet was sitting. A thread of grey going west.

The blue eye saw the dead.

Then the blood came out of his nose and onto the mirror, and the flat came back, and he was on his side on the floor with his cheek on the cold tatami, retching, with the window banging and his whole head ringing like a struck bell.

When he could sit up, Gorō was sitting on the windowsill, inside the room, which he was not supposed to be, looking at him with the blue eye.

"*Kaa,*" he said. It sounded like a criticism.

Rei laughed, and it hurt, and he wiped his nose with the back of his hand and laughed again.

"Yeah," he said. "I know. I'll get better."

Gorō hopped down off the windowsill onto the floor, walked across the tatami with his rolling crow's walk, and settled down beside Rei's hip, against his leg, with his feathers fluffed out, as if he had always slept there.

He was still there in the morning.

[break Bored]

It took Rei about a week to understand what Gorō was.

He was not wicked. He was not cursed. He was not a menace. He was bored. He was a very clever bird who had been trained once, by someone who knew what she was doing, and then put behind wire for four years with nothing to do but sit in a corner and watch other crows fly out on errands that were never his. So he had stolen glasses, and bitten trainees, and thrown feed at owls, because it was something to do.

Out of the wire, he was a different bird.

He was everywhere. Rei came out of Akabane station in the morning and there was a black and white crow on the station clock. He walked to the Division and there was a black and white crow on the bicycle rack outside. He went to Tanizaki's on Saturday and Gorō was already sitting on the stone lantern in the wild garden, as if he had been invited, and Tanizaki looked at him over the newspaper and said, "Your friend is early," and poured him a saucer of water. He went to the Grid with Haruki and caught, out of the corner of his eye, a flash of white on a telephone wire above the street, gone when he turned his head. He was never close. He was never far. He followed Rei across the city the way a thought follows you round on a bad day, always just behind your shoulder.

And when Rei looked into the black-backed mirror, at the right time, in the in-between light, with the door in his chest open a finger's width, he could go further than looking. The glass showed where Gorō was at every hour of the day, the way all the mirrors in the mirror room did. But if he opened the door in his chest, it was more than a picture. He could feel the wind under the wings. A roof. A river. The back of a temple. A cemetery in Yanaka full of other crows. And the pale blue eye always found the cold places. The places where something unquiet sat.

Once, on a grey Sunday, he tried to tell Gorō where to go.

He stood on the balcony and said it out loud, feeling stupid. "The river. The Arakawa. By the floodgate. Go and look."

Gorō looked at him with the black eye. Then he flew off, very fast, in completely the wrong direction.

When Rei looked in the mirror at dusk, Gorō was sitting on the awning of the Kondō bakery on Jūjō Ginza, watching a girl eat a melon bun.

"*Kaa,*" he said, the next morning, on the railing, with crumbs on his beak.

"That wasn't the river," said Rei.

Gorō looked at him with the blue eye, quite calmly, as if to say that he had heard, and he would think about it, and in his own time, and not before.
