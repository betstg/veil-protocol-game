#title The Aviary
#part Book Two. The Families

On his second morning at the Division, Rei got lost.

The Akabane building was eight floors of grey concrete beside the railway lines, and from the outside it looked like a tax office. Inside it looked like a tax office too, mostly. Long corridors with grey carpet tiles. Fluorescent lights that hummed. Doors with little brass plates. Photocopiers that jammed. Men in tired suits carrying files up the stairs because the lift was always full.

But it was not a tax office, and once you had worked there for a day you began to notice.

[need ch26b_corridor | A plain grey Japanese government office corridor with grey carpet tiles and humming lights, but small white paper seals tucked into the corners of every door frame, a line of salt along the skirting board, and a young man in a cheap new suit with a lanyard looking around, lost]

On the second floor, where the trainees sat, there was a small white paper seal tucked into the top corner of every door frame. On the third floor, outside Assessment, the skirting boards had a thin line of salt along them that the cleaners swept round, very carefully, and never into. In the lift there was a mirror, as in every lift, but this one was covered with a cloth, and someone had written on a sticky note on the cloth, *DO NOT UNCOVER. YES, YOU, TAKAHASHI.* On the fourth floor a man walked past Rei carrying a cardboard box with holes in it, and the box said something in a small cross voice, and the man said, "I know. I know. Nearly there."

Rei had been sent to fetch printer paper. He had found the stationery cupboard on the first floor, and then he had taken the wrong stairs back up, and now he was in a corridor he did not recognise, with a box of paper in his arms.

At the end of the corridor a woman was walking toward him with a mug in both hands.

She was perhaps forty. Tall, with brown hair going grey at the temples, pulled back. A soft grey cardigan over her work clothes. She walked the way some people walk through their own house at night, without needing to look. She was going to pass him. Then she looked at his face, and slowed down.

"You're lost," she said. It was not a question.

"I'm looking for the second floor."

"You're on the fourth." She looked at him a moment longer. Her eyes were warm and brown and tired around the edges, as if she had been tired a long time ago and had kept the lines as a souvenir. "You're the new trainee. Shimizu's."

"Uedera," said Rei, and bowed as well as he could with a box of paper. Then, because he had been brought up by someone who would have said it, he said, "Sorry to stop you. Are you alright? You look like you were going somewhere."

The woman stopped walking altogether.

For a second something went across her face that Rei could not read. Then she smiled. It was a small smile, but it went all the way up.

"Nobody asks that in this building," she said. "Katagiri. Field Operations." She lifted the mug a little. "And yes. I was going somewhere. I'm going to sit with a cat."

"A cat?"

"Come on. You can carry your paper. It's on the way."

[break The Cattery]

The Cattery was at the bottom of a back staircase that Rei would never have found on his own, behind a door with no plate on it at all.

It was warm, and dim, and smelled of paper and cat and dried bonito. Low shelves round the walls. Old filing cabinets with the drawers taken out and cushions put in. And on five of the cushions, five cats. A tortoiseshell. A grey. A white one with odd ears. A fat orange one asleep upside down. And on the highest shelf, by itself, a large dark cat with a heavy head and very still yellow eyes, which looked at Rei the way a judge looks at a man in the dock.

[need ch26b_cattery | A warm dim basement room with low shelves and old filing cabinets turned into beds with cushions, five cats each on its own cushion with a small brass name tag, the largest dark cat on the highest shelf looking down, a woman in a grey cardigan sitting on a low stool holding a mug, a young man in a suit standing at the door holding a box of paper]

Each cushion had a small brass tag on it with a name. Sera. Itou. Mochida. Hara. Kuga.

"They're named after people," said Katagiri, sitting down on a low stool under the high shelf with her mug. "Five people from this building who went out and didn't come back." She said it simply. "The cats are doors. For the handlers. When something has to get somewhere fast, and there's no time for trains." She looked up at the big dark cat on the high shelf. "That's Kuga. He's the strongest. When it really matters, the handlers go through Kuga and nobody else." She paused. "If he agrees. It's always his choice. He outranks all of us."

Kuga looked at Rei. Rei looked at Kuga. Then, very slowly, because it seemed the polite thing, Rei bowed to the cat.

Kuga closed his eyes.

"Oh," said Katagiri softly. "He likes you." She drank her tea. "He doesn't like anyone. He barely likes me, and I come down here every afternoon." She did not say why she came down here every afternoon. She did not need to. She sat under the shelf with the mug in both hands and her shoulder almost touching the edge of Kuga's cushion, and the big dark cat let her.

Rei understood, then, that the Cattery was not only for crossing.

"I knew your brother," said Katagiri, after a while, not looking at him. "He used to talk about you. *The Kita one,* he called you. For years. I kept saying, bring him in, I want to meet him, and he kept saying, *not yet.*" She turned the mug round in her hands. "I'm glad you came anyway."

Rei did not know what to say to that. So he said thank you, and picked up his printer paper, and went back up the stairs. He got lost twice more on the way. He did not mind.

[break The door that should be a stairwell]

On the third day Shimizu took him to the training camp.

"Forms," she said, in the corridor, walking fast with her coffee. "Tuesday and Friday mornings. Every trainee. You learn what your hands do when you're frightened, before you're frightened." She stopped at a door at the end of the third-floor corridor. It was an ordinary fire door, with a push bar and a little green sign of a running man. Behind it, by every rule of buildings, there should have been a stairwell. "Don't stare when we go through. Everyone stares the first time. It's embarrassing."

She pushed the bar.

There was no stairwell.

There was grass.

[need ch26b_camp | Through an ordinary office fire door with a green exit sign, a walled green field under a winter sky, worn grass, a sparring circle worn down to bare dirt, and along one wall a long tall wire aviary full of black crows and, in the shadows at one end, sleeping owls]

A wide walled green, the size of a school playground, under a cold pale winter sky. Bare trees along the far wall. A circle in the middle worn down to brown dirt, where two people in tracksuits were moving slowly round each other, very carefully, with their hands open. The air smelled of cold earth and something sharp, like after lightning. And along the whole length of the left-hand wall, three metres high, a long wire aviary, full of noise.

Crows. Dozens of them. Black and shining and loud, hopping along the perches, squabbling over bits of food, shouting at each other in the cold. At the far end, in the shadows under a wooden roof, a row of owls sat with their eyes shut, round and grey and silent as stones.

Rei stared.

"I said don't stare," said Shimizu.

"Where are we?"

"The third floor." She drank her coffee. "Don't ask. Nobody knows. It's been here longer than the building. They built the building round the door." She walked toward the sparring circle. "Come on. You've got forms. And afterward you can go and look at the birds, because everyone does, and get it out of your system."

[break The mirror room]

After forms, with his arms aching and grass on his knees, Shimizu showed him the room beside the camp door.

It was a long narrow room with no window, like a cloakroom. Along both walls there were shelves, and on the shelves, standing up in rows, there were mirrors. Dozens of them. Ordinary mirrors. Bathroom mirrors with chipped corners, cheap round shaving mirrors, a hand mirror with a pink plastic handle, a square one in a wooden frame that looked as if it had come off a wardrobe. Most of them were covered with a cloth. Each one had a paper tag tied to it with string, and on the tag there was a name in neat brush writing. Not a person's name. A crow's.

[need ch26b_mirror_room | A long narrow windowless room like a cloakroom, shelves along both walls holding rows of ordinary mirrors of every kind, bathroom mirrors, round shaving mirrors, a pink plastic hand mirror, most covered with cloths, each with a paper name tag tied on with string, one or two older mirrors in carved dark frames on a high shelf apart]

"When a crow's bound to a mirror, the mirror lives here," said Shimizu. "When you need to see something, you sign the mirror out, and you go and sit somewhere quiet, and you look." She touched the tag of a cracked round one. *Hayate.* "Most of them aren't special. A mirror's a mirror. It's the binding that matters, not the glass." She nodded at the high shelf at the end. Two or three older mirrors stood there by themselves, in dark carved frames, uncovered. "Those ones were made. A medium made them for the Division, years ago, because we asked. They work further. Nobody's allowed to touch them without a form signed on the eighth floor."

"Do you have one?"

"A crow?" Shimizu looked at the shelves. "I sign one out when I need it. Like everyone." She was quiet for a second. "A few people in this building have a crow that's really theirs. That comes when they call it and goes where they tell it. Senior people. People who've spent years with one bird." She turned off the light. "It's rare. Crows don't like many of us. They don't have to."

[break Gorō]



The head of the aviary was an old woman from Field Operations in a padded green jacket and rubber boots, with a bucket. She did not say her name and Rei did not ask. She looked at him the way farmers look at weather.

"Trainee?" she said to Shimizu.

"Trainee. First week."

"Right." She unlocked the aviary door and stood back. "You can go in and look, since you've come. Everybody does once. Stand still. Don't feed anybody. Don't look anybody in the eye." She sniffed. "They'll look at you. That's all. Don't take it personally when they lose interest."

Rei went in. The door clanged shut behind him. The noise was enormous. He stood in the middle of the gravel floor with his hands at his sides and looked at nothing in particular, the way you look at nothing on a train.

The crows looked at him.

He felt them looking. Dozens of small bright black eyes, turning, measuring. A few came lower, to the perches near his head. One landed on the gravel near his feet, looked at his shoes, and hopped away. Another came close, very close, and pecked his trouser leg, and flew off shouting. None of them stayed.

Then, from the very top of the aviary, from the corner where the wire met the wall, something came down like a dropped stone.

It landed on his shoulder.

It was a big crow. Bigger than the others. And it was the wrong colour. Black mostly, but splashed with white, across the back and one wing and down the chest, in uneven patches, like a black cat that has walked through spilled paint. And its eyes did not match. The left eye was black, like any crow's. The right eye was a pale, pale blue, the colour of a winter sky, and it looked at Rei from a distance of about four centimetres with an expression of the deepest suspicion.

[need ch26b_goro | Close up of a large piebald crow, black splashed with uneven white patches on the back and one wing and chest, one black eye and one pale winter-blue eye, perched on the shoulder of a young man in a suit inside a wire aviary, staring sideways into his face from very close]

Outside the wire, the old woman put her bucket down.

"Oh, *no*," she said.

"Is that bad?" said Rei, not moving his head.

"That's Gorō." She came to the wire and looked at the crow on Rei's shoulder as if it had been sick on her boots. "Nobody picks Gorō. Nobody's *allowed* to pick Gorō. He bites. He steals. He opens the feed bins. He took a pen from the Deputy Director in October and dropped it in the pond. He undid three of my knots last week and let half the aviary out and sat on the roof laughing at me while I caught them." She glared at the crow. "He came to us trained. Proper training, beside a mudang, a Korean shaman, before he came here. He reads the dead. He could be the best dead-reader we've got. And he won't do a thing for anybody. Eight people have tried to work with him in four years. He's bitten all eight. Nobody even puts his name down any more."

Gorō turned his head and looked at her with the black eye. Then he turned back and looked at Rei with the blue one.

Then, very slowly, very deliberately, he leaned forward and took Rei's ear in his beak. Not hard. Just enough. The way you hold something you have decided is yours.

"*Kaa,*" he said, quietly, round the ear.

The old woman stared. Shimizu, behind her, lowered her coffee.

"Well," said the old woman at last. "That's new." Then she banged on the wire with her palm, hard, and Gorō let go of the ear and went back up to his corner, shouting about it all the way. "Out you come. Before he changes his mind and takes the ear with him."

Rei went back to the second floor with his arms aching and grass on his knees and a red mark on his ear. Kato looked up from behind his tower of photocopies.

"Somebody in Accounts," he said, "has already started a bet on how long you keep that ear." He went back to his photocopies. "I've put a thousand yen on *forever*. Don't let me down."
