#title The Gathering

"You can't find the Gathering," said Hara Setsu. "The Gathering finds you."

"That sounds like something on a poster."

"It is something on a poster. Switch had it printed as a joke. Nobody laughed." She was knitting in her kitchen behind the bath, on the second Saturday in October, with the red scarf for Gin growing slowly over her knees. Rei sat across the low table from her with his hair still wet and his skin still pink from the water. Through the wall, in the men's bath, Mr. Kishi's corner was warm. "The Grid meets once a month. Never in the same place twice. You only hear where it is the day before, and only if somebody who's already inside says you can come."

"Are you inside?"

"I'm seventy-one and I run a bath house in Kita," said Hara Setsu. "Everybody who sees anything in this ward has sat in my water. Yes, I'm inside." Click, click went the needles. "I've told Switch you can come. That means if you do something stupid, it's my name they'll look at. So please don't do something stupid."

"What counts as stupid?"

She looked at him over the needles.

"You have a card in your wallet with a white owl on it," she said. "Don't take it out."

[break The pool]

The message came on Friday afternoon. An address in Kita, near the river, and a time. Ten at night. Under it, one line. *Bring nothing that beeps.*

The address was an old public swimming pool. Rei had swum there as a boy in summer, with Gin standing at the side in his work clothes, not swimming, watching. It had closed three years ago when the city built a new sports centre by the station. Now it was a long low concrete building with its windows boarded up and a chain on the front doors. The chain was not locked. Somebody had wrapped it round the handles to look locked.

Rei pushed the door open. Inside it was dark and smelled of old chlorine, a sharp clean smell that had soaked into the concrete and would never come out.

[need ch18_empty_pool | A closed old public swimming pool at night, the pool drained and dry, forty people standing and sitting along the tiled edge and down in the empty basin, lit by camping lanterns and phone torches, a stocky person with hair tied back severely standing on the diving board]

The pool had been drained. It was a long empty rectangle of pale blue tiles with black lines painted down the bottom, and the deep end sloped down into darkness. There were people everywhere. Sitting on the edge with their legs hanging over. Standing in the shallow end. Sitting down in the deep end on folding chairs and on cushions and on the bare tiles. Forty of them, maybe fifty. Camping lanterns stood in a row along the side and threw long shadows up the walls. Somebody had brought a big flask of tea and paper cups.

They were not what Rei had expected. He had expected something secret and dangerous. Men in black. Instead there was an old woman with a shopping trolley. A young father with a sleeping baby in a sling. Two schoolgirls in uniform doing homework by torchlight. A man in a chef's whites who had clearly come straight from a kitchen. A tall woman in a nurse's uniform with her hair coming out of its clip. A boy of about twenty with dyed blond hair and a guitar. A businessman in an expensive suit sitting very straight on a folding chair, looking at his phone. All sorts. All ages. All of them, Rei understood slowly, like him.

Every person in this room could see what he could see. Not one of them was on the list.

A small old man by the door, sitting on an upturned crate with a thermos, looked at Rei and nodded. "First time," he said. It was not a question. "Setsu-san's boy. Sit anywhere. It's less dramatic than people think."

[break Switch]

At ten exactly, someone climbed up onto the old diving board at the deep end and stood there with their hands in their pockets.

They were about thirty-five. Stocky and solid, with black hair pulled back so tight into a short tail that it looked painful. They wore a plain grey coat over plain grey clothes. There was a heavy old signet ring on one finger. Their face was square and calm and gave away nothing at all. Rei looked at them for a long time and could not have said if they were a man or a woman, and after a moment he understood that this was on purpose, and that it did not matter, and stopped trying.

"That's Switch," said the old man with the thermos, who had followed Rei in. "Don't call them him. Don't call them her. Don't call them anything except Switch."

"Evening," said Switch from the diving board. Their voice was flat and carried easily over the empty pool. "Same rules. Nobody records. Nobody photographs. Nobody uses real names unless they want to. Nobody talks about who they saw here when they leave." A pause. "If you brought anything that beeps, you can leave now, and I'll know if you don't."

Nobody moved.

"Good," said Switch. "Business."

It was not like any meeting Rei had ever seen. People stood up one at a time from wherever they were sitting and talked. Nobody had a microphone. Nobody had a list. A woman in the shallow end said there was a thin spot opening by a primary school in Itabashi and she needed two people who could do wards, and two hands went up, and that was that. The chef asked if anyone could read a knife. It had belonged to his old teacher and it was cutting him every time he used it, and he did not want to throw it away. Three people offered. A grandmother said her husband had died in March and was still in the house, and he was happy, and she was happy, and she wanted to know if that was alright. There was a long, gentle discussion. Everyone agreed it was alright, for now. Someone gave her a phone number for when it stopped being alright.

Money changed hands. Favours. Phone numbers on scraps of paper. It was like watching a village.

Then the man in the expensive suit stood up.

[break What they said]

"I want to talk about the Division," he said.

The pool went quiet.

"My sister registered in 2011," he said. "After the earthquake. She thought she should. She thought it was the right thing. She could help. They put her on a list and they tested her and they gave her a number." He was holding his phone in both hands, very tight. "In 2013 they asked her to join something called the Vessel Program. They said it was an honour. They said she would be helping keep the city safe. They said it was cohabitation. A partnership." His voice went flat. "I visited her last month. She was sitting in a clean little room with soft toys on the bed and she called me by the wrong name. Twice. And the thing behind her eyes looked at me like I was a meal it had already eaten."

Someone in the deep end said, very quietly, "Bastards."

"They have a list," the man went on. "Every person who registers, they catalogue. What you can do. How strong. Where you live. Who your family is. And now they're cataloguing us too. People who never registered. They have people out on the street, watching for us. Measuring us from across the road." He looked round the pool. "They say it's to protect us. They took my sister's mind and gave her a teddy bear."

Other people stood up. A woman said the Division had told her family her father died of a stroke, and she had seen the thing that killed him, and she had been made to forget it for a year, until it came back to her one morning in the bath. A young man said a friend of his had been taken in for *assessment* six months ago and had not come out. A schoolgirl said, in a small fierce voice, that her teacher told the class there was nothing to be afraid of on the trains, and that there was, and that the posters with the smiling owl were lies.

Rei sat on the edge of the empty pool with his legs hanging over and listened to all of it. He thought of the blue suits on Ōji platform. The mother's face changing slowly. *The heat. Yes.* He thought of a cactus on a windowsill on the third floor in Marunouchi that only one person in the whole building bothered to water.

He thought of Okada, laying her card on the table between them. *The front is my desk. The back is me.*

He did not know what he thought.

[break Choi Hana]

"You."

He looked up. A girl was standing over him on the pool edge, looking down.

She was about his age, maybe a little younger. Small and thin, with black hair cut short and blunt. She wore a black hoodie with the sleeves pulled down over her hands, all the way to her fingers, even though the night was warm. Her face was sharp and pale and her eyes were very dark and very wary, the eyes of someone who has been hit before and is waiting to see where the next one is coming from.

"You smell like the Division," she said.

Several heads turned.

"I'm not Division," said Rei. He kept his voice level, as he used to at the station when a drunk at the front desk wanted a fight. It came out more like Kumagai than he liked.

"I didn't say you were. I said you smell like it." She crouched down in front of him, close, too close, and held out her right hand, palm up.

His wallet was in it.

He had not felt it go. It had been in the back pocket of his jeans, and he was sitting on that pocket, and now it was in her hand, and there was a faint cold feeling on his hip where it had been, like a draught. She had not touched him. She had not moved.

She opened the wallet with one thumb, without looking down, and pulled out a white card with a small owl printed on it.

"Someone gave you this," she said, "and told you to call the back of it."

"Hana," said the old man with the thermos. "He's Setsu-san's."

"I know whose he is. That's why I'm asking him and not throwing him in the deep end." She did not look away from Rei. "Who's on the card?"

Rei could have lied. He did not think it would work. He did not think anything would work on this girl.

"A woman called Okada," he said. "Investigation. Kita district. My brother is missing. He was Division. She was his friend. She's trying to help."

The girl's face changed. Not much. Something moved behind the hard eyes.

"Your brother," she said. "Uedera Gin."

"Yes."

"I worked with him once. Two years ago. A school in Shin-Ōkubo." She was still crouched in front of him. Her sleeves were still pulled down over her hands. "He was decent. For one of them. He didn't try to put my name on anything. He didn't ask where I lived." She stood up. "That doesn't make you decent. That makes your brother decent."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked down at him. "Your friend in Investigation is very nice, I'm sure. She tells you to call the back of the card. One day she's going to tell you to come in for an assessment, just half a day, just to be safe. And you'll go, because you trust her." Her voice was very quiet now. "And then they'll have your number. And then one day there'll be a little clean room with soft toys on the bed."

"Hana," said Switch, from the diving board. Not loudly. Everyone heard it.

The girl looked up at the diving board. Then back at Rei.

"Choi Hana," she said. "In case you want to put it on a list." She put the card back in the wallet and dropped the wallet in his lap.

She walked away down the length of the pool and sat down by herself at the deep end, on the bare tiles, with her back against the wall and her knees pulled up. Rei watched her go. As she sat down, her left sleeve slipped up her arm a little, and he saw, for one second, in the lantern light, the edge of a scar. Pale and thick and ridged, running up the inside of her forearm toward the elbow. She pulled the sleeve down again at once, without looking at it, the way you pull a curtain closed.

[break After]

Switch found him at the end, by the door, while people were folding up chairs and taking the lanterns away.

"Setsu vouches for you," they said. "So you can come back. Once a month. If you want." Their square face was unreadable. "You heard what people said tonight."

"Yes."

"You'll hear worse, if you keep coming." Switch looked at him. "You'll have to decide, sooner or later, which side of the room you're sitting on. You can't sit on the edge of the pool forever."

"I'm looking for my brother," said Rei. "That's my side."

Switch was quiet for a moment. Then they almost smiled. It changed their whole face, and then it was gone.

"That's what Gin said," they said. "Word for word. In 2012. In a car park in Kawaguchi." They turned away. "He kept coming back for three years. He never picked a side. I never asked him to." They stopped at the door. "There's a woman on a quiet street on the west side of the city running a tea house where lonely sensitives go and come out emptier. People call her Kanna-sensei. Three of ours went there this year. Two came back quieter than they went. One didn't come back." They looked at him over their shoulder. "Your brother was asking about her in July. I thought you'd want to know."

Then they were gone into the dark, and Rei was standing alone in the doorway of an empty swimming pool, with the smell of old chlorine in his nose and a name in his head he had never heard before.

*Kanna-sensei.*

He thought of a drawing from the fat folder in Itsuki's studio. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table, and nothing in it at all. The last file Gin had opened on his laptop, the night before he went.

He went home and did not sleep. At four in the morning he gave up and sat at the kitchen table with the book open at the white room and smoked three cigarettes one after another out of the open window, and a crow on the telephone wire opposite watched him do it with an expression of deep disapproval, as if it had been told to keep an eye on him and was writing it all down.
