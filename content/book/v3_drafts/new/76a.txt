#title The Listener

Kato Ryusei had been able to hear other people think since he was six years old, and in all the years since he had never once wanted to hear more of it.

He had told Rei that, once, in the Arsenal, across the counter, in the first week they knew each other. Rei had asked what it was like. Nobody ever asked what it was like. They asked whether he could hear them right now, and then they thought about something embarrassing on purpose to test him, and then they stopped standing near him. Rei had asked what it was like, and Kato had told him the truth, which was that it was like living in a station. All day. Every day. Ten thousand announcements at once, none of them for you.

So he built rows. That was how he managed. He had worked it out when he was nine, in a classroom in Saitama, with forty children thinking at him at once. You put each voice on a shelf. You give it a place. You keep it there, small and quiet and in order, like books in a library, and you only take one down when you need it. Rei had said once that the inside of his head sounded like a library. It was the nicest thing anybody had ever said about it.

He ran up the road from the freight yard toward Ōji with Usami beside him, and the shelves began to fill.

[break Ōji]

Ōji is the oldest part of Kita. It sits on a hill above the river, round a station where three lines cross, and it has been a place people come to for a very long time. There is a shrine to the fox god, Inari, up a flight of stone steps behind the station, where on the last night of every year people in fox masks walk through the streets with paper lanterns. There is a park on the hill, Asukayama, which a shōgun planted with cherry trees three hundred years ago so that ordinary people would have somewhere to go in spring, and there is a tiny free monorail that runs up the side of the hill to it, for old people and pushchairs, that takes two minutes and that every child in Kita has ridden at least once for no reason at all. There is a tram, the last one left in Tokyo, that rattles past the foot of the park with one carriage and a bell. There is a river in a stone channel at the bottom of the hill, very small, very fast, with a walk along it under the trees.

At twenty to one on the last night of Obon, it was full of people walking.

Kato heard them before he saw them. He came round the corner by the post office at the foot of the hill and they were there, coming up the road from the river, in ones and twos and little groups, on the pavements and down the middle of the road. People in work clothes and pyjamas and school uniforms. People holding white flowers. Not hurrying. Not talking. Turning off the main road into the side streets, one at a time, as if each of them had an address written on the inside of their eyes.

And they were quiet. That was the first thing. In his head, they were quiet. Every one of them a shelf with nothing on it. A low grey hum, like the one Tendo had made in the spring. And on top of the hum, in every one of them at once, the same small soft voice, very far away, saying the same thing.

*Knock. Knock gently. When they open, say come and rest.*

"She's in all of them," said Usami, beside him. He had his hand pressed flat against the wall of the post office, and his eyes were shut, and Ren was very close behind his face. "Ren can feel it. Like a thread through beads. One voice. All the way back to the river."

"What happens when they knock?"

Usami opened his eyes.

Halfway up the side street opposite, a woman in a cardigan had stopped in front of a narrow wooden house with a potted plum tree by the door. On the step there was a little clay dish of grey ash. The woman bent down and blew on it, very softly, as if blowing out a candle. Then she straightened up and knocked. Three times. Gently.

A light came on upstairs.

Kato heard the man inside wake up. Heard him wonder who was knocking at one in the morning. Heard him put on his slippers and come down the stairs and think *drunk, probably, it's the end of Bon*, and reach for the door.

He heard the man open it.

He heard the woman on the step say *come and rest* in a voice that was not hers.

And he heard the man's mind go quiet.

Not empty. Not hollow, not yet. Just quiet. As if somebody had laid a hand on it. The man stood in his own doorway in his slippers and looked at the woman in the cardigan, and then he stepped out onto the step, and pulled the door shut behind him, and the two of them turned together and walked down the street toward the river.

"She's taking them," said Kato. His voice did not sound like his voice. "She can't have the dead. So she's taking the living. One door at a time. All night. Every house in Kita."

*I hear you,* said Haruki, in his head, very calm. *Shimizu says stop the knocking. Okada's sending two officers to you from Tabata. Itō and Hirata. Five minutes. Usami, Ren, can you tell Kato where they're going before they get there?*

Usami put both his hands flat on the wall of the post office.

"Yes," he said. "Ren can. He says the walls know. He says every house that's going to be knocked on is already listening for it."

[break The walls]

It worked like this, for the first hour.

Usami stood with his back against a wall and his palms flat on it, and Ren read the street. Not the people. The houses. Which ones were warm, with somebody asleep upstairs. Which ones had a dish of ash on the step. Which ones had a white flower coming toward them. Ren felt the whole street through the stone, like somebody feeling a carpet in the dark for a dropped earring, and he told Usami, and Usami said it out loud, very fast, in his soft apologetic voice, a little breathless.

"Third house on the left, the one with the bicycle. Somebody's coming up from the river. A man. Big. Now. Sorry. Now."

And Kato went.

He ran to the house with the bicycle and got there before the big man did. He stood on the step between the big man and the door. He did not touch him. He did not need to. He reached into the big man's head, past the hum, past the soft far voice, down to where the man was, small and asleep at the bottom like a child in a cot, and he said *wake up*.

Not loudly. He did not know how to do it loudly. He had spent his whole life learning to do everything as quietly as possible. He said it as you say it to somebody on a train who has gone past their stop. *Excuse me. Wake up. This isn't your house.*

The big man stopped on the bottom step.

He looked at the door. He looked at the white flower in his hand. He looked at Kato. His eyes went from black to brown, slowly, like a puddle clearing when the mud settles. He said, "Where am I?" in a frightened voice, and sat down on the step of a stranger's house, and started to shake.

"Ōji," said Kato. "You're all right. Stay here. Somebody's coming."

Then Usami said, from the wall, "The one with the noren. Two of them. Sorry. Sorry, now," and Kato ran.

He woke eleven of them in the first hour. One at a time. Each one took him longer than the last, because the soft far voice was getting louder, and the hum was getting thicker, and there were more of them coming up the hill from the river every minute than there had been the minute before. He woke them on steps and in gateways and once in the middle of the road in front of the tram stop, a girl of about fifteen in a school blazer who had been walking straight toward the doors of the tram depot. When she woke up she screamed at him and hit him in the face, and he let her, because it was a reasonable thing to do.

The two officers from Tabata came at a run at five to one. Itō and Hirata. Hirata was forty and quiet and had been in Field for fifteen years. Itō was twenty-four. He had joined the Division the October before, three months ahead of Rei, and Kato had sold him a seal kit in the Arsenal and heard him think the whole time about whether his girlfriend would say yes if he asked her to move in, and about ramen, and about whether the kit was too expensive. He had a round friendly face and a crooked front tooth and his lanyard was on the wrong way round.

"Kato-san," he said, out of breath. "What do we do?"

"Salt on the steps," said Kato. "Every house Usami says. A line across the door. It won't stop them knocking. It'll stop the thing in them coming in if somebody opens."

"How do we know which houses?"

"Usami tells you."

Itō looked at Usami against the wall, with his eyes shut and his hands flat and his cardigan buttoned wrong, and something went through Itō's head that Kato heard and did not like. *The Vessel. The one with the thing in him.* It went through and was gone, and Itō did not say it, and was a little ashamed of it, Kato could hear that too.

"Okay," said Itō. "Okay. Tell me which houses."

Usami told him. He went.

[break The hill]

At a quarter past one, the street went wrong all at once.

Kato was on the step of a little sweet shop by the Inari stairs, waking a man in a delivery uniform, when he heard it. Not one voice. All of them. Every hollow one in Ōji, at once, on every street on the hill, stopping where they were and turning their heads up toward the park. As if somebody had called their names.

*Faster,* said the soft far voice. *Faster now. All the doors. All at once.*

And they began to knock.

Not one at a time. Every one of them. On every street. Kato heard it like rain starting on a roof, a few drops and then all of it. Knocking. Up the hill, down the hill, along the river, on the steps of the shrine, in the lanes behind the station. Fifty doors. Eighty. A hundred. And behind every door, somebody waking up. Somebody putting on slippers. Somebody thinking *who is it* and *it's the end of Bon* and *I'll just see*.

The shelves in his head broke.

That was what it felt like. All of them at once. Twenty years of rows and order and small quiet voices, kept in their places, and all of it came down at once like a bookcase falling over in an earthquake. A hundred people waking up. A hundred hollow ones with the soft far voice in them. Usami and Ren behind him, frightened. Itō on a step two streets away with a bag of salt, frightened. Hirata. Haruki on the roof of a car a kilometre away. Shimizu in the yard. Rei, somewhere, very far off, in capitals. All of them at once, all of them loud, in no order at all, pouring in through every gap.

He sat down on the step of the sweet shop because his legs had gone.

He put his hands over his ears. It made no difference. It never had.

"Kato," said Usami. "Kato. Kato-san."

Usami was crouching in front of him. Kato could see his mouth moving. He could not hear him, not with his ears. He could hear him thinking, a small frightened voice in the middle of the roar, and behind it, wound round it like a coat, a second voice, quieter and stranger and very calm.

*You're holding the walls up,* said Ren.

Kato looked at him.

*All of them,* said Ren. *Every voice. You're trying to put them back on shelves. There are too many. You can't. You'll break.* A pause. *Stop holding the walls up. Let them fall. Rei took his door off. You can take your shelves down.*

"I can't," said Kato. Out loud. It came out cracked. "I can't. If I let it all in, I won't know which one is me."

Usami took his hand.

He did not ask. He just took it, in both of his, warm and slightly sticky from the icing on the rabbit cakes, and held on.

"You'll know," said Usami, in his own voice, out loud. "I'm holding this one. This one's you."

[break Stop]

So Kato stopped holding the walls up.

He let them fall. All of it. The hundred doors and the hundred people and the hollow ones and the soft far voice and Itō and Hirata and Haruki and Shimizu and Rei, and the man in the sweet shop behind him who had woken up and was standing in his doorway in his vest staring at two young men sitting on his step holding hands. He let all of it come in, and he did not put it anywhere, and he did not try to keep it in order. He let it be a station. Ten thousand announcements at once.

And in the middle of it, there was a hand holding his, and it was his hand.

He found that he could hear all of them at once. Not one after another. At once. The way you can hear a whole orchestra and not one violin. A hundred hollow ones on a hundred steps with their hands raised to knock. And under every one of them, small and asleep at the bottom, a person.

He said *stop*.

Not loudly. He did not know how to do it loudly. He said it the way he had said *wake up* on the first step of the night. Quietly. Plainly. Like a station announcement, the kind that comes over the speakers in a calm recorded voice, that everybody on the platform hears at the same moment and nobody argues with.

*Please stop. Please sit down where you are. Please wait.*

All over Ōji, a hundred hands stopped in the air in front of a hundred doors.

Kato felt them stop. Every one. He felt their surprise. He felt the soft far voice in them, at the river, pause, as if somebody had interrupted it in the middle of a sentence.

Then, one after another, on every step and in every gateway and on the stone stairs up to the shrine, they sat down.

Slowly. Like tired people at the end of a long day. On the steps of houses that were not theirs, with their white flowers in their laps, under the paper lanterns and the dark windows. They sat down and they waited, as the voice in their heads had asked them, very politely, to do.

Behind a hundred doors, a hundred people with their hands on the latch heard nothing at all, and stood there for a moment in their slippers, and thought *must have been the wind*, and went back up to bed.

[break Holding]

He held them for twenty-six minutes.

He did not know it was twenty-six minutes. Usami told him afterwards. Usami had looked at his phone at the start and at the end because he did not know what else to do, and because he thought somebody ought to know.

It was the hardest thing Kato had ever done. A hundred people, and under each of them a hundred more things, the soft far voice and the hum and whatever small grey thing was riding each of them, all leaning, all the time, like a hundred people leaning on a door. He held them all still with one word, over and over. *Wait. Wait. Please wait.* His nose bled. Then his ears. His hands began to shake and would not stop, and then his whole arms, and Usami held on to the one hand he could reach and did not let go.

*Kato,* said Haruki, from very far away. *Kato. Hold on. They're coming. Okada's on the hill with six cars. Moriya's coming from Tabata. Mori's coming from the canal. Hold on. Five minutes.*

*I know,* thought Kato. *I can hear them.*

He could. He could hear the field teams coming up the hill in their cars. He could hear Okada in the first car with her notebooks on her knee, thinking very fast and very clearly in lists. He could hear Moriya swearing at his crutch. He could hear the old man in the sweet shop behind him, still in his doorway, who had worked out that something was happening that he did not understand and had decided to make tea about it.

He could hear Itō.

Two streets away. On the step of a ground-floor flat in a narrow lane behind the shrine, with a bag of salt in his hand.

He could hear Itō, because Itō was frightened, and frightened people are loud. And he could hear why.

There was a man in the lane who had not sat down.

[break Itō]

He was one of the hollow ones. Kato had him. He had all of them. But this one was not only hollow. There was something in him, under the hum, that was not small and grey and patient like the others. It was angry. It was very old and very angry and it had no interest at all in waiting, and it had been pushing against Kato's *wait* since the first second, the way a dog pushes against a lead.

It was in a man of about fifty in a security guard's uniform, and it had a kitchen knife from somebody's draining board in its hand.

And there was a family in the flat behind Itō's back. A mother and two small children. The mother had heard the knocking and had not opened the door, and was standing on the other side of it now with the children behind her and her phone in her hand, and Itō had heard her and had stepped onto her step with his bag of salt between her door and the lane.

*Itō,* thought Kato. *Get off the step. Get off the step, he's not stopping, I can't hold this one, get off the step.*

Itō did not have Kato in his head. Nobody had told him to listen. He could not hear.

*Haruki,* thought Kato. *Itō. The lane behind the shrine. Tell him to move.*

*Telling him,* said Haruki, and Kato felt him reach, quick and light, across the hill. *Itō. Move. Now.*

And Kato heard Itō hear it. Heard him understand. Heard him look over his shoulder at the door behind him, where a woman and two small children were standing in the dark of their own hallway, and heard him think, very clearly, in the plain ordinary voice of a young man who sold nothing and was not special and had joined the Division ten months ago because it was a job with a pension.

*No. Not this one.*

He stayed on the step.

The man in the security guard's uniform came down the lane.

Kato held a hundred people still on a hundred steps, and could not let go of any of them to help, and heard all of it.

He heard the salt go over the step in a white line, too late and too thin. He heard Itō put his arm up. He heard the knife. He heard Itō think *oh*, surprised, the way you think *oh* when you miss a step on a staircase in the dark. He heard him think of a girl called Nao, and a flat in Akabane with a balcony they had looked at the week before, and whether she would have said yes. He heard him think of his mother. He heard him think *the door's still shut. Good. Good.*

And then he did not hear him any more.

Hirata got there forty seconds later. Kato heard him put the man in the guard's uniform face down on the road with one knee in his back and press iron into the back of his neck until the angry thing came out of him screaming and went up into the dark over the shrine. Heard him say Itō's name. Twice. Heard him take off his own jacket.

Behind the door of the ground-floor flat, the woman with the two small children did not open it until the ambulance came.

[break The boy]

Okada's cars came up the hill at twenty to two, and the field teams began to take the hollow ones off the steps.

They did it gently, as Shimizu had told them to. One officer to each. A hand on the shoulder. A pinch of iron in a palm. A word. And as each one was taken, Kato let go of them, one at a time, a hundred small weights coming off him, until he was holding twenty, and then ten, and then three, and then none.

He did not stand up. He could not. He sat on the step of the sweet shop with his back against the wall and his face wet with blood and something else, and Usami sat beside him still holding his hand, and the old man from the shop came out in his vest with two cups of barley tea on a tray and put the tray down between them on the step and went back inside without asking anything.

On the bottom step of the Inari stairs, across the road, one of the hollow ones had not got up when the officer touched his shoulder.

Usami saw him first. He let go of Kato's hand and went across the road.

He was very young. Seventeen, perhaps. Perhaps not even that. Very thin, in a grey hoodie that had been washed so many times it had gone soft, and trainers held together with tape, and no socks. He had no bag. He had no phone. He had nothing in his pockets at all, the officer said, not a train card, not a coin. He was sitting on the bottom step of the shrine stairs with a white chrysanthemum in his lap and his head against the stone banister, and his eyes were open, and they were brown, and he was not breathing.

Usami sat down on the step beside him.

He put his hand flat on the stone, beside the boy. Kato, across the road, felt Ren go into it. Into the stone of the step. Into the banister. Into the shrine stairs going up into the dark between the stone foxes. Looking. Feeling for anything the boy had left in the place where he sat.

There was almost nothing. Kato felt Ren find almost nothing. A boy who had been hollow for so long that there had been very little left in him for the thing to hold on to, and when Kato said *wait*, and the thing let go, there had been nothing left at all to keep him there.

Ren found one thing. Kato heard Usami say it, out loud, very softly, to nobody.

"He liked the steps," said Usami. "These ones. He used to sleep here, sometimes. In the winter. Under the roof of the shrine, where it's dry. The foxes kept him company." Usami's voice cracked. "That's all. That's all Ren can find. Nobody knew his name. He didn't think anybody would ever want it."

He took off his cardigan, the one with the buttons done up wrong, and put it over the boy, carefully, up to the chin. Then he sat on the step beside him with his knees drawn up and did not move, and nobody asked him to.

[break Vanguard]

*Kato,* said Haruki.

He did not say it the way he had said everything else all night, quickly, in passing. He said it slowly, as you say someone's name across a room when you want them to look up.

*Kato. Do you know what you just did?*

*I held them.*

*You held a hundred and four people on a hundred and four steps for twenty-six minutes with one word,* said Haruki. *Without touching any of them. While you could hear every single one of them and everything in them and me and all of us.* A pause. *Gin could do eleven. On a good day. With his eyes shut. I can do about thirty. There are four people in this country registered at what you just did, and two of them are dead.*

Kato did not answer.

*That's Vanguard,* said Haruki. *That's what that's called.* A pause. *You were always this. Gin knew. When you first came into the building they read you, and he went into the room after the reading and did something to the paper, and to you, so the number came out small. He told me. He said if they knew what you were they'd use you until there was nothing left, and you'd let them, because you wanted so much for somebody to need you.* Another pause. *It wore off. Tonight. You took it off yourself.*

Kato could not say anything.

*So,* said Haruki. *That's what that's called. I'm going to tell Adachi. I'm going to tell him tonight, and I'm going to make him write it down, and nobody in that building is ever going to look at you across a room again and wonder what you're hearing. They're going to know.*

Kato sat on the step of the sweet shop with his hands shaking in his lap and two cups of barley tea going cold beside him.

Across the road, Hirata was kneeling in the lane behind the shrine beside a shape under a jacket. Usami was sitting on the bottom step of the stairs beside a shape under a cardigan. Up and down the hill the field teams were walking the woken people home, one at a time, with their hands on their elbows.

He could hear all of them. He did not try to put them anywhere.

"I sold him a seal kit," he said, out loud, to Haruki, to nobody. "In October. He thought it was too expensive. He was right. I gave him the discount anyway. He didn't know I'd heard him." He looked at his hands. "He had his lanyard on the wrong way round. All night. I didn't tell him."

*I know,* said Haruki.

The old man from the sweet shop came out again, with a third cup, and looked at Kato, and at the cups he had not touched, and sat down on the step on his other side with a small grunt, and drank the third cup himself, slowly, looking out at the road. He did not say anything. After a while Kato picked up one of the cold cups and drank it too.
