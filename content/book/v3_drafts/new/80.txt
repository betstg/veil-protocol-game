#title Who Comes

The grey sea moved.

Rei felt it before he saw it. Everything was coming in through the empty frame in his chest, and so he felt the far shore change the way you feel the floor of a train change when it goes over points. A shudder. A shift of weight. Something very large turning over, a long way out, in the fog.

Kanna-sensei had not moved from beside her low white table. She stood on the grey beach with her hands at her sides now, open, and her face lifted, and her eyes on the fog. And she was speaking. Not to them. Out. Into the grey. In a voice that was not a voice, that was the sound a lake makes at night when the ice is forming.

Calling.

Rei could not hear the words. He could feel them. Each one went past him like a cold draught under a door. Not names. Promises. *There is room. There are bodies. There is a city full of warm people with nobody holding the door. Come. Come now, while it's still open.*

*She's calling across,* said Kato, inside his head. Kato's voice was so thin it was almost gone. *Rei. Everything over there that wants to come. Anything. She's telling them there's room.*

Out in the fog, the shapes came.

[break The fog]

Rei never could describe them properly afterwards. He tried, once, to Chen Lian, and gave up halfway through.

They were not bodies. They were shadows on the fog. Hundreds of them. Small ones and large ones, coming up out of the grey sea toward the beach like storm clouds coming up over the edge of the sea on a summer afternoon. Some of them had been people, once, a long time ago, and had gone bad on the far side, and were coming back for what they thought was theirs. Most had never been people at all. Hungry things. Angry things. Things like the grey one that had sat on Tendo's river, and the small sour thing under the stair at Ōji, and the black hair round the waiting woman in the Hotel Rosa. Small, mostly. But so many. And here and there among them, much larger, slower, older, shapes that made the river under them go still.

None of them was like the thing in Gin. None of them was like the woman on the beach. They were what came when somebody old and strong held a door open and said *there's room*.

*Come in,* she was saying. *Come in. There are so many warm people. Nobody is holding the door any more.*

Rei felt the round room in Ochanomizu, four kilometres away, shudder. Felt eleven holders in their chairs gripping the arms. Felt Chen Lian's light flicker. Felt the warmth in Watanabe's empty chair waver, like a candle when a door opens.

Beside him, inside Gin's body, he felt the voice go very, very still.

[break The first]

Mori walked toward the river.

He did not run. He walked as he had walked into the round room the night Rei first saw it. Straight. Heavy. With his arms at his sides and his big hands open. The air round him did not shimmer. It burned. Properly. Orange. Like the door of a boiler. The weeds he walked through went up in small bright flames behind him, one by one, and went out.

He stopped at the edge of the grey beach.

The nearest of the big shapes on the sea saw him. It turned toward him. It was red at the edges, like something that had been burning for a long time, and very big. It roared without sound, and the river under it boiled.

Mori lifted his chin. "No."

He said it as he had said it at the round table, and in the boiler room, and in the round room with his hand on Rei's shoulder. Not loud. Absolutely certain.

His open hands closed. "*That is wrong.*"

The red thing stopped.

It hung over the grey sea, very close, and Mori stood on the edge of the beach in front of it, one man in a black jacket that did not fit him across the shoulders, burning. And for a moment, just a moment, the two of them were the same size.

But only for a moment.

The red thing leaned. Down. Toward him. And Mori's knees bent, slowly, like a man's under a weight he did not expect, and his face went grey with strain, and the fire round him went from orange to red to a deep dull angry crimson, like a coal at the very bottom of a fire that has burned all night.

He did not step back.

[break The first who came]

Something stepped out of nowhere beside him.

Rei saw it happen. One moment there was nobody on the riverbank but Mori. The next there was a woman. Small. Very small, barely up to Mori's shoulder. In dark practical clothes with cat hair on the knees, and short black hair, and a thin ginger cat draped round her neck like a scarf, and an expression of mild interest, as if she had been on her way to the shops and had stopped to look at something odd in a garden.

She had not walked there. Rei, with everything coming in, could feel it. She had come from the other side. Across. Through the fog, with the cat, the way other people come up out of a subway station, and stepped out onto this side of the beach as if it were a pavement.

"Oh," she said. She looked up at the red thing. "So that's what they look like from this side."

She lifted one hand. Palm out. Toward it. The ginger cat on her shoulders opened its eyes.

And the space between her hand and the red thing went *empty*.

Not dark. Not white. Not grey. Empty. A place where nothing was. Not air. Not light. Not the far side. Not this side. Just nothing, a round clear hole of nothing the size of a house, sitting in the air in front of her palm like a soap bubble that had forgotten to have a surface.

The red thing leaned into it.

And stopped. As if it had walked into a wall of glass. As if, inside that bubble of nothing, there was nothing to be angry at, because there was nothing at all.

The woman looked at it with mild interest.

"Inoue," she said, without turning her head, to Mori. "Misaki. Not Satoru. We're not related. Everybody asks." She held her palm very steady. "And this is Renado. He doesn't like you. He doesn't like anybody." A pause. "You're very hot. Take a breath. I've got this one for a bit."

Mori stared at her.

Then he took a breath. One long one. And the deep dull crimson round him went back to orange.

[break Who else]

They came after that. All of them. One after another. Like people coming to a fire in the night, from every street, in whatever they were wearing.

A gate opened in the middle of the yard.

Not painted. Built. A black lacquered doorframe, standing on its own in the weeds where there had been nothing a second before, with a white paper rope across its top. And through it, out of a raked gravel garden and a smell of four hundred years of incense, came Kuroda Sōun. In his dark haori with the white crest. Upright. Silver-haired. With a face like a closed house.

Behind him came Takuya, with his car keys still in his hand. Two uncles Rei had seen once across the training hall. A girl of about sixteen with a long ponytail and very straight shoulders. And Grandmother Mitsu, very small, very old, leaning on nobody, looking round the yard with an expression of deep disapproval, as if it had been badly kept.

Sōun did not look at the river. He did not look at the shapes on the grey sea. He looked across the yard at the freight car with the white door painted on its side, and the street inside it, and the boy on the crate with his white wet hands on the frame, and the other boy holding him up.

He walked to them.

He did not hurry. He walked as he walked across his training hall. When he reached them he stood for a long moment and looked at the painting. At the lanterns and the bath house and the dog asleep in the road.

"Your mother would like to see this," he said.

Itsuki turned his head and looked at his father. His face was white and wet and he was shaking from head to foot.

His voice cracked on it. "You came."

"You asked." Sōun turned to the yard. To the living people still standing among the rails, waking up, frightened, a long way past their stop. To the ones still walking toward the beach with black eyes. Hundreds. "Takuya. Mitsu-san. Everybody who can open a gate. One each. To the hospital in Ōji. To the shrine at Tabata. To our garden. Anywhere that is not this river." He lifted his own hand. "Take them out. Carefully. They have been through enough."

Black lacquered doorframes stood up in the weeds, one after another, all over the yard, out of nothing. And through them, one at a time, gently, the Kuroda began to walk the living out.

[break And more]

A man came over the fence. Not a big man. Dense. Dark. Calm in the way very deep water is calm. He landed in the weeds and stood up and looked at the yard, all of it, at once, with dark eyes that did not stop. Behind him, heavier, came another man, older, in a dark jacket, with a canvas medical bag over his shoulder and the posture of somebody who has spent twenty years being required to take up space correctly.

The first man looked across the yard at Gin's body. At Gin's shadow. Which fell, Rei saw, for the first time, half a second ahead of Gin, against the white light from the river, the wrong way.

"Kurosawa," said the man, to nobody in particular. "Shou." He did not take his eyes off Gin. "Adachi asked." A pause. "I've been wanting to look at that shadow properly for six months."

Gin's body inclined its head to him. Very slightly. Rei felt both of them do it.

Kurosawa looked at it for a long moment. Then he nodded, once.

"Two," he said. "Thought so." He turned to the river. "Tanaka. Stay with the kids. I'm going to the water."

He walked down to the grey beach and stood beside Inoue Misaki, with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the shapes coming out of the fog like a man looking at weather. Where he stood, the shapes nearest the shore began, very slowly, to slow down, like a heart slowing.

[break The dog]

The Director came on foot.

He came down the road from the cordon in his grey suit, at a walk, with Riki limping beside him on a lead, and Ono a few steps behind him with Yua, who had refused to stay at the cordon and was wearing a police jacket three sizes too big. Adachi did not look at the river. He stopped at the gate beside Okada, who was standing with her three notebooks under her arm, and looked at the yard, and at all the people in it, and at the doorframes standing in the weeds, and at the painted door on the freight car.

"Director," said Okada.

"Okada-san." He looked at the river at last. At the shapes. At the small woman by the white table. "I'm at a budget hearing," he said. "You'll remember that, if anybody asks."

"I'll write it down," said Okada.

Riki sat down at Adachi's feet in the gateway, with his bad leg out, and looked at the grey beach, and showed his teeth, and did not make a sound.

[break The cats]

The last to come came quietly.

A taxi stopped at the gate. A small round man in a grey cardigan that was too warm for August got out of the back with great difficulty, with a pencil behind his ear, and lifted out five cat carriers, one after another, and set them in a row in the weeds by Riki.

Mr. Tsuji knelt down and opened the carriers one at a time.

The tortoiseshell. The grey. The white one with the odd ears. The fat orange one, who did not want to get out and had to be tipped. And the last. The biggest. Black. Heavy-headed. With still yellow eyes.

Kuga walked out of his carrier and sat down in the weeds and looked across the yard at the grey beach and the fog and the shapes and the hollow ones still walking toward the water.

Then he looked at Rei.

Rei was forty metres away. Kuga looked at him anyway, and Rei felt it through the empty frame in his chest, like a hand laid flat on his back. *Tuesday,* said the look. *It's only a Tuesday on the other side. Busy. That's all.*

"Go on," said Mr. Tsuji to the cats. His voice was not quite steady. "All of you. Go and fetch them back."

The five cats went.

They went across the yard through the weeds and the people, low and fast and silent, like cats going through long grass at night. Down to the grey beach. Onto the grey sand. And then, one after another, without stopping, without looking back, into the grey.

They crossed.

Rei felt them go. Five small warm lights, going out across the far shore into the fog among the shapes coming in, like five small boats going out into a storm. And on the other side, where the hollow ones had left so much of themselves for months and years, walking toward a teapot and a kind face, the five small lights began to move.

Finding the pieces. One at a time. Nudging them. As a cat nudges your hand when it wants you to get up. *This way. Not that way. Back. Back. Somebody's waiting.*

In the yard, the hollow ones still walking toward the beach stopped.

The woman with the handkerchief. A university student. A man in a crumpled work shirt. They stopped, one after another, all over the yard, with their flowers in their hands. And the black went out of their eyes, slowly, as it had gone out of Mika's, and Kaneda's, and the woman on the walkway. And they looked round at the yard and the lights and the river and each other, with the faces of people who have woken up on a train a long way past their stop, and the Kuroda took them gently by the elbow and walked them through the doorframes, out.

On the grey beach, the small woman in the grey cardigan turned round.

She looked at the cats in the fog. At Inoue Misaki's bubble of nothing. At Kurosawa beside it with his hands in his pockets. At the doorframes in the weeds. At the old woman with the white bun, who had found Majima and was standing over him with her hand on his chest and her mouth pursed, saying something that made him laugh out loud. At an old man in a grey suit in the gateway, with a dog.

At Rei, with his door off.

At Gin, with two in him, standing beside his brother with one hand on the boy's shoulder.

Her face did not change. But her hands closed, slowly, into fists. And out on the grey sea, the shapes stopped coming in.

They waited.

*Very well,* she said. In the air. In the weeds. In every ear in the yard. *Very well. You've all come. How kind.* She looked at Gin. *Then you can all watch.*

She smiled at him. A smile that said, as clearly as words, *you last.*

Then she lifted one hand, very gently, like a conductor before the music, and out on the grey sea, all the shapes began to come in again at once.
