#title Who Comes

The grey sea moved.

Rei felt it before he saw it. Everything was coming in through the empty frame in his chest, and so he felt the far shore change the way you feel the floor of a train change when it goes over points. A shudder. A shift of weight. Something very large turning over, a long way out, in the fog.

Kanna-sensei had not moved from beside her low white table. She stood on the grey beach with her hands at her sides now, open, and her face lifted, and her eyes on the fog. And she was speaking. Not to them. Out. Into the grey. In a voice that was not a voice, that was the sound a lake makes at night when the ice is forming.

Names.

Rei could not hear them. He could feel them. Each one went past him like a cold draught under a door. Eleven of them. Old. Older than the city. Older than the river. Names nobody living had spoken in a very long time.

*She's calling them,* said Kato, inside his head. Kato's voice was so thin it was almost gone. *Rei. She's calling the others. All of them. The Shells. Every seat.*

Out in the fog, the shapes came.

[break The Shells]

Rei never could describe them properly afterwards. He tried, once, to Chen Lian, and gave up halfway through.

They were not bodies. They were shadows on the fog. Eleven of them. Coming up out of the grey sea toward the beach the way storm clouds come up over the edge of the sea on a summer afternoon. Each one a different shape. Each one the worst of something.

One was red, and roared without sound, and the weeds along the riverbank went black where its shadow fell. *Rage.* Rei felt Mori, behind him, go rigid.

One was white and thin and endless, and everyone it looked at took one step back without knowing why. *Fear.*

One was small and bright and hungry, and had a thousand mouths, and was laughing. *Hunger.*

One was very beautiful, and had no face at all. *Love,* in its worst form. The kind that will not let go.

One was a great slow grey shape like a hill of wet ash, and it was looking for something it had lost, and it would never find it. *Grief.*

And more. Eleven. Coming up out of the fog across the grey sea toward the beach, toward the woman in the soft white dress who stood with her arms held out to them like a mother calling children in from the garden.

*Come and sit,* she was saying. *Come and sit. There are chairs for all of us. Thirteen chairs. Tonight we sit, and the current runs our way, and it never runs back.*

And behind her, on the grey beach, at the low white table, cushions were appearing. One. Then another. Then another. Thirteen. In a ring. With a gap in it.

Rei felt the round room in Ochanomizu, four kilometres away, shudder. Felt eleven holders in their chairs gripping the arms. Felt Chen Lian's light flicker. Felt the warmth in Watanabe's empty chair waver, like a candle when a door opens.

Beside him, inside Gin's body, he felt Yushiro go very, very still.

[break The first]

Mori walked toward the river.

He did not run. He walked the way he had walked into the round room the night Rei first saw it. Straight. Heavy. With his arms at his sides and his big hands open. The air round him did not shimmer. It burned. Properly. Orange. Like the door of a boiler. The weeds he walked through went up in small bright flames behind him, one by one, and went out.

He stopped at the edge of the grey beach.

The red shape on the sea saw him. It turned toward him. It was very big. It roared without sound, and the river under it boiled.

Mori lifted his chin. "No."

He said it the way he had said it at the round table, and in the boiler room, and in the round room with his hand on Rei's shoulder. Not loud. Absolutely certain.

His open hands closed. "*That is wrong.*"

The red shape stopped.

It hung over the grey sea, very close, and Mori stood on the edge of the beach in front of it, one man in a black jacket that did not fit him across the shoulders, burning. And for a moment, just a moment, the two of them were the same size.

But only for a moment.

The red shape leaned. Down. Toward him. And Mori's knees bent, slowly, the way a man's knees bend under a weight he did not expect, and his face went grey with strain, and the fire round him went from orange to red to a deep dull angry crimson, like a coal at the very bottom of a fire that has burned all night.

He did not step back.

[break The first who came]

Something stepped out of nowhere beside him.

Rei saw it happen. One moment there was nobody on the riverbank but Mori. The next there was a woman. Small. Very small, barely up to Mori's shoulder. In dark practical clothes with cat hair on the knees, and short black hair, and an expression of mild interest, as if she had been on her way to the shops and had stopped to look at something odd in a garden.

She had not walked there. She had not teleported there. Rei, with everything coming in, could feel the difference. She had simply been somewhere else, and now she was here, the way your keys are on the kitchen table when you have just looked on the kitchen table twice and they were not.

"Oh," she said. She looked up at the red shape. "So that's what they look like close up."

She lifted one hand. Palm out. Toward the shape.

And the space between her hand and the red shape went *empty*.

Not dark. Not white. Not grey. Empty. A place where nothing was. Not air. Not light. Not the far side. Not this side. Just nothing, a round clear hole of nothing the size of a house, sitting in the air in front of her palm like a soap bubble that had forgotten to have a surface.

The red shape leaned into it.

And stopped. As if it had walked into a wall of glass. As if, inside that bubble of nothing, there was no Rage to be, because there was nothing to be angry at, because there was nothing at all.

The woman looked at it with mild interest.

"Inoue," she said, without turning her head, to Mori. "Misaki. Not the Hunger one. We're not related. Everybody asks." She held her palm very steady. "You're Rage. I can tell. You're very hot." A pause. "Take a breath. I've got this one for a bit."

Mori stared at her.

Then he took a breath. One long one. And the deep dull crimson round him went back to orange.

[break Who else]

They came after that. All of them. One after another. The way people come to a fire in the night, from every street, in whatever they were wearing.

A gate opened in the middle of the yard.

Not painted. Built. A black lacquered doorframe, standing on its own in the weeds where there had been nothing a second before, with a white paper rope across its top. And through it, out of a raked gravel garden and a smell of four hundred years of incense, came Kuroda Sōun. In his dark haori with the white crest. Upright. Silver-haired. With a face like a closed house.

Behind him came a young man with a shaved head and a jaw like a brick. A girl of about sixteen with a long ponytail and very straight shoulders. And an old woman with a white bun, very small, very old, leaning on nobody, looking round the yard with an expression of deep disapproval, as if it had been badly kept.

Sōun did not look at the river. He did not look at the shapes on the grey sea. He looked across the yard at the freight car with the white door painted on its side, and the boy holding it open with his white wet hands, and the other boy holding him up.

He walked to them.

He did not hurry. He walked the way he walked across his training hall. And when he reached them, he did not say anything. He put one hand flat on the painted door, beside Itsuki's, and the other flat on Sei's back, beside Sei's own.

The door brightened. All at once. Like a lamp when someone turns the wick up.

Sōun did not look at either of them. "Hold. Both of you. I've got the frame."

Itsuki turned his head and looked at his father. His face was white and wet and he was shaking from head to foot.

His voice cracked on it. "You came."

Sōun pressed his palm flatter against the wet paint. "You asked."

[break And more]

A man came over the fence. Not a big man. Dense. Dark. Calm in the way very deep water is calm. He landed in the weeds and stood up and looked at the yard, all of it, at once, with dark eyes that did not stop. Behind him, heavier, came another man, older, in a dark jacket, with a canvas medical bag over his shoulder and the posture of somebody who has spent twenty years being required to take up space correctly.

The first man looked across the yard at Gin's body. At Gin's shadow. Which fell, Rei saw, for the first time, half a second ahead of Gin, against the white light from the river, the wrong way.

"Kurosawa," said the man, to nobody in particular. "Shou." He did not take his eyes off Gin. "Adachi asked." A pause. "I've been wanting to look at that shadow properly for six months."

Gin's body inclined its head to him. Very slightly. Both of them. Rei felt both of them do it.

Kurosawa looked at it for a long moment. Then he nodded, once.

"Two," he said. "Thought so." He turned to the river. "Tanaka. Stay with the kids. I'm going to the water."

And he was gone. Not stepping. Gone. And then he was standing on the grey beach beside Inoue Misaki, with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the eleven shapes coming out of the fog the way a man looks at weather.

[break The cats]

The last to come came quietly.

A car stopped at the gate. A small grey car with a dented door. A woman got out. Grey cardigan. Brown hair going grey at the temples. A face that had cried a great deal in the last three weeks and had stopped, on purpose, before she got out of the car.

She opened the back door. She lifted out five carriers, one after another, and set them in a row on the ground by the old dog lying in the gateway. The dog lifted her head and looked at them, and put it down again.

Katagiri Noa knelt in the weeds and opened the carriers one at a time.

The tortoiseshell. The grey. The white one with the odd ears. The fat orange one, who did not want to get out and had to be tipped. And the last. The biggest. Black. Heavy-headed. With still yellow eyes.

Kuga walked out of his carrier and sat down in the weeds and looked across the yard at the grey beach and the fog and the eleven shapes and the thousands of hollow ones still walking toward the water.

Then he looked at Katagiri.

She was kneeling with her hands on her knees, not touching him. Not asking. She did not know how to ask any more.

Kuga got up. He walked across the weeds to her. And he put his heavy black head against her knee, once, hard, the way he had put it against Rei's knee on the first day, and held it there.

Katagiri made a sound. She put her hand on his back.

"Go on," she said. Her voice broke. "All of you. Go and get them. Go and bring them back."

The five cats went.

They went across the yard through the weeds and the walking crowd, low and fast and silent, the way cats go through long grass at night. Through the hollow ones' legs. Down to the grey beach. Onto the grey sand. And then, one after another, without stopping, without looking back, into the grey.

They crossed.

Rei felt them go. Five small warm lights, going out across the far shore into the fog among the vast cold shadows of the Shells, the way five small boats go out into a storm. And on the other side, on the far side of the far side, where the souls of the hollow ones had been walking for months and years toward a teapot and a kind face and the end of everything, the five small lights began to move among them.

Turning them. One at a time. Nudging. The way a cat nudges your hand when it wants you to get up. *This way. Not that way. Back. Back. Somebody's waiting.*

In the yard, the woman with the handkerchief stopped walking.

Then the university student.

Then the man in the crumpled work shirt.

They stopped, one after another, all over the yard, in the weeds and on the rails, with their flowers in their hands. And the black went out of their eyes, slowly, the way it had gone out of Mika's, and Kaneda's, and the woman on the walkway. And they looked round at the yard and the lights and the river and each other, with the faces of people who have woken up on a train a long way past their stop.

On the grey beach, Kanna-sensei turned round.

She looked at the cats in the fog. At Inoue Misaki's bubble of nothing, holding the red shape still. At Kurosawa beside it with his hands in his pockets. At Sōun with his hand on a painted door. At the old woman with the white bun, who had found Majima in the weeds and was standing over him with her hand on his chest and her mouth pursed, saying something to him that made him laugh out loud.

At Rei, with his door off.

At Gin, with two people in him, standing beside his brother with one hand on the boy's shoulder.

Her face did not change. But her hands closed, slowly, into fists again. And behind her, out on the grey sea, the eleven shapes stopped coming in.

They waited.

*Very well,* she said. In the air. In the weeds. In every ear in the yard. *Very well. You've all come. How kind.* She turned back to the white table. To the ring of thirteen cushions with the gap in it. *Then you can all watch.*

She looked at Gin's body for a long moment. She smiled. A smile that said, as clearly as words, *not yet. You last.*

Then she lifted one hand, very gently, the way a conductor lifts a hand before the music, and out on the grey sea, all eleven shapes began to come in again at once.
