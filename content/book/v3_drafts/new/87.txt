#title Lanterns

A year later, on the last afternoon of Obon, Gin stood in front of the mirror in the hall with a tie in each hand.

One was navy. One was grey. He held them up against his shirt one after the other, frowning, with the expression he used for bad reports.

"No," said Itsuki, from the kitchen.

Gin did not turn round. "I haven't asked you anything."

"You don't need to. You've been standing there for nine minutes." Itsuki came and leaned in the kitchen doorway with a glass of barley tea. He had cut his hair. He had a ring on his left hand now, a heavy old silver one with the Kuroda crest, which he turned round and round when he was nervous and took off when he was painting, which was almost never. "No tie."

"It's a restaurant."

"It's a restaurant with a garden in Yanaka where they bring you the food on leaves. She chose it. She's going to turn up in a dress with paint on the hem and three brushes in her hair. If you turn up in a tie she'll think she's been called in to a meeting." Itsuki took the ties out of his hands, both of them, and hung them on the hook by the door, beside the key. "Undo the top button. Roll up your sleeves. Not too far. Like you've just finished something and you're pleased about it."

Gin looked at himself in the mirror. He undid the top button.

"Better," said Itsuki.

On the sofa, with his feet on the arm and inside the actual end of it, Rei looked up from a case file.

"Are you going to the Thirteen meeting next week?" he said. "With her?"

Gin went still in the mirror.

"Because I'm in that meeting," said Rei. "Thirteenth chair. Next to Mori. Opposite her." He turned a page. "So if you're coming as her date, I just want to know whether it's personal or whether you're trying to take my seat. Because if you're trying to take my seat, I think that's a bit much. I only got it last year."

Itsuki made a noise into his barley tea.

Gin turned round.

"I'm not going to the meeting," he said, with great dignity. "I am going to dinner. In Yanaka. And it is going to take three hours to walk two hundred metres from the station, because she stops at everything. Everything. A cat on a wall. A drain cover. A shop that sells only brooms. Last week she stood in front of a vending machine for four minutes because the light was hitting the cans in a way she wanted to remember." He rolled his sleeves up, not too far. "She's got paint on her fingers all the time. She keeps a brush behind her ear like a pencil. She said a pigeon was *architecturally confident*." He stopped. Something happened to his face that he clearly had not authorised. "She has a very sweet artist's air about her. Is the point."

Rei put the file down on his chest.

"Huh," he said. "A painter."

"Yes."

"Paint on her fingers."

"Yes."

"Stops at everything. Looks at things for ages. Says weird things about pigeons."

Gin looked at him. Then at Itsuki, in the kitchen doorway, with paint dried permanently into the creases of his knuckles and a brush, Rei noticed at that moment, stuck behind his ear.

"Well," said Gin, picking up his keys. "Clearly the Uedera brothers have similar taste." He put his shoes on. "You two have a lovely evening. Stay in. Look at each other. Have a nice time with your painter boyfriend, Rei."

"He's not my..." said Rei, sitting up.

"I'm not his..." said Itsuki, at the same moment.

Gin shut the door on both of them.

They heard him laughing all the way down the stairs.

[break The river]

They went to the river at dusk.

Not the yard. Further down, the Sumida, at the place below the bridge where the bank was concrete and there were steps, and on the last night of Obon people came with paper lanterns and candles and set them on the water to float away.

There were a great many people that year. More than anyone could remember. The neighbourhood association had run out of lanterns by six and somebody's grandmother had started folding new ones out of shopping bags on a card table.

Everybody came.

Adachi came in a cardigan, which nobody had ever seen, with Riki limping beside him on a lead. He had retired in March. He had handed the Division over to a twenty-five-year-old telepath with a chess piece in his pocket, in front of the entire Operations floor, and said only *he will be very good at this, because he will hear you not saying things, so you may as well say them*, and walked out to the lift with his dog. He spent his mornings now in the park by the river in Ōji, brushing Riki on a bench, and his afternoons at a tofu shop in Akabane doing the accounts, for no money, because the old man who ran it could not add.

Kato came with him. Director Kato. In a dark suit with his hair combed flat and one strand already falling, with his hand in his pocket on the knight. He had a lanyard on that he kept forgetting to take off. He stood on the steps among the crowd and Rei could see him hearing all of them, all the thoughts at once, the whole river of the city, and not flinching from any of it.

*You're thinking very loudly,* he thought, at Rei, across the steps.

*You always say that.*

*It's always true.* Kato's mouth went sideways. *It's a nice noise.*

Hana was there, on the top step, with her hood down for once. Moriya, without his crutches, with his two children asleep against him. Okada, with her notebooks. Uruma, very tall, with a paper lantern he had painted himself, covered in tiny seals, which he would not let anybody else touch. Mio had come from Kyoto. Mori was there, enormous and hot and silent, with Hoshino beside him handing out rice balls from a cool box. Yua, in a clean T-shirt, eating a hamburger out of a paper bag with great seriousness. She held up a finger at Rei as he went past.

"Thirty-one," she said. "He's paid nine. Tell him I'm counting."

[break Usami]

Usami found them on the steps, with a bag of cakes.

Rabbits, this time. Iced white, with pink ears, and every one slightly different, and one of them winking. He gave the first one to Kato.

"Thank you," he said. "Thank you for the form."

Kato looked at the cake. "I didn't do a form. I said no forms. I said if you wanted to go, you could go."

"That's what I mean," said Usami. "Thank you for the form you didn't do."

He had a small rucksack on his back. That was all. Two years in the Vessel floor and a year on Shimizu's team, which was Kato's team now, and everything he was taking with him fitted in a small rucksack with a cartoon hedgehog on the pocket.

"Are you frightened?" said Rei.

"Yes," said Usami. His eyes came round first, and his head a half second after. "Very." He smiled. "Ren isn't. Ren says it's only going home. Ren says it's going to be his job, over there, to sit in the chair at the head of the table that nobody's sat in since before the river. He says it's been empty so long that everything over there has forgotten what it feels like, and somebody has to remind them." He touched his own chest, lightly. "He says he can't do it without me. He says that's the whole point of the chair."

Beside him, where most people could not see, a thin shy young man with his hands folded nodded, very seriously.

Inoue Misaki was waiting at the bottom of the steps by the water, with the ginger cat round her neck, looking at her watch with mild impatience.

"I'll live over there," said Usami. "As a person. Among them. Misaki says there's a street. With a bath house. Itsuki painted it." He looked at Itsuki. "I'd like to see if it's true."

"It's true," said Itsuki. "I painted it from the outside. I'd like to know if the bath house is any good."

"I'll write," said Usami. Then, uncertainly, "Can you write? From there?"

"On Tuesdays," said Rei. "Sometimes. Somebody I know sends a cup back empty. That's a sort of letter."

Usami hugged him. Then Kato, who did not know where to put his arms, and then did. Then Itsuki. Then he went down the steps to the water with his rucksack and his bag of cakes, and Misaki held out her hand, and he took it, and the ginger cat opened its eyes.

And then they were not on the steps any more.

Rei felt them go. Through the empty frame where his door had been, which he had never put back. A small warm light, and another, wound round it like a coat, going across, out over the water and through the veil and on, to a street with lanterns and a bath house and a dog asleep in the road.

Far away, under everything, at a long table in the fog, somebody sat down in a chair that had been empty for a very long time. And everything on the far side felt it, all at once, like the first warm day of spring.

[break The names]

Rei wrote the names.

He sat on the concrete steps with a felt pen and a stack of white paper lanterns and wrote them one by one in his best handwriting, which was not very good.

*Tanizaki Seiichi.*

*Watanabe Jun.*

*Shimizu Ren.* Kato sat beside him while he wrote that one, and took the lantern off him afterwards, and carried it down to the water himself, and stood there with his hand in his pocket until it had gone out of sight.

*Majima Kenji.* Mika put that one on the water. She was nineteen now and worked at a vet's in Ueno. The old dog sat on the step above her watching it go, very grey round the muzzle now, with a stick of chicken skin in her mouth, the skin already pulled off.

Then Itsuki took a lantern and wrote on it himself. *Kuroda Shige.* A great-great-aunt nobody had believed. He took his cigarettes out of his pocket, the last packet, which he had been carrying for eleven months without opening, and put the whole packet in the lantern beside the candle, and pushed it out onto the water.

Three steps up, Grandmother Mitsu, who had insisted on coming and had been carried down the steps by Takuya, said *finally* in a voice that carried right across the river.

Then Rei wrote one more, and sat looking at it.

There was no name on it. Just a little drawing, in felt pen, not very good. A cup, with steam coming off it.

Itsuki leaned over his shoulder. "Who's that one for?"

"A young man on a step," said Rei. "With a tray. Who waited a very long time at a gate." He looked at it. "And somebody who decided to stay under the light instead of going home, because he liked the work. I go and see him on Tuesdays. He's learning about trains."

Itsuki took the pen out of his hand, and under the cup, in his small careful painter's hand, he drew a bath house. Very small. With steam coming out of it.

They put it on the water together.

It floated out into the current and turned once, slowly, and went away downriver among all the others, a little square of orange light in a long line of orange lights going away into the dark toward the sea.

On the step behind them, Hana sat down next to Rei with her sleeves pulled down over her hands. She watched the lanterns for a long time. Then she pushed her left sleeve up, and looked at the scar on her forearm, and took a breath.

"I've been meaning to tell you," she said. "For two years. About this." She turned her arm over. "It was the summer I was..."

On the far bank, somebody let off the first firework of the night.

It went up white and burst gold right over the river, enormous, with a bang that bounced off every building on both banks, and everybody on the steps went *oh*, all at once, as people do. And by the time the sparks had come down and gone out in the water, Hana had pulled her sleeve down again and was looking at the sky with her mouth a little open, like everybody else.

"Another time," she said, and smiled, very slightly. "It's a long story."

[break The far bank]

On the far bank, under the fireworks, Rei saw them.

He did not look for them. He would have liked it on the record that he did not look. But everything came in through the empty frame, and so he saw.

A woman in a summer dress with paint on the hem and two brushes stuck through her hair, stopped dead in the middle of the crowd on the far bank with her head tipped back, looking up at the gold coming down, with her mouth open and her hands pressed together under her chin. Completely still. As if she wanted to remember exactly how it fell.

And beside her, waiting, with his sleeves rolled up not too far and the top button of his shirt undone, a tall thin man with his hair cut short at last, not looking at the fireworks at all. Looking at her.

He was smiling.

Not the polite smile. Not the careful one. Gin's own. The crooked one that went up on the left first, that Rei had seen perhaps once a year growing up, if he was lucky, if the rent was paid and the fridge was full and nothing had stood at the kitchen window for a week.

Chen Lian said something, pointing at the sky. Gin laughed. Out loud. Rei could not hear it from this side of the river. He did not need to.

"Stop staring at your brother," said Itsuki, beside him.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring. You've been staring for two whole fireworks." Itsuki bumped his shoulder against Rei's. "Leave him alone. He's having a nice time. It's disgusting."

[break Work]

Rei's phone buzzed at half past nine.

A photograph of a cat on a bin. No words. From the Director of the Civil Veil Division. Which meant *are you busy*.

Rei looked at it. Then he stood up off the concrete steps and brushed down his trousers and looked at Itsuki.

"Right," he said. "Work. Kato's got something. Kita. A hunt."

Itsuki did not move from the step.

"I'm not Division," he said. "I've never been Division. I'm not on any list." He leaned back on his elbows on the warm concrete and turned the heavy silver ring round on his finger. "I'm the head of a Tower family. I'm extremely rich. I don't have to work. I have people for that."

"You have three people for that," said Rei. "And one of them's Takuya."

"Takuya is very loyal."

"Takuya drove into a bollard last week."

"With great loyalty." Itsuki looked up at him. "Is it a bad one?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then you don't need me. I only come for the bad ones. I've told you. I don't paint the ordinary ones any more. I want to find out about Wednesday on Wednesday." He held out his hand, and Rei pulled him up off the step anyway, and he came anyway, as he always did, grumbling, with his hands in his pockets.

They walked up the steps away from the river, through the crowd, into the streets.

And Rei saw them.

Not the hollow ones. Not the things in the fog. Those were gone, or held, or sitting quietly at a long table on the other side with a mender among them and somebody young at the head. These were others. Ordinary ones. An old man in a grey summer kimono sitting on a bench outside a closed tobacconist's, watching the crowd go home, nodding at people who did not see him. A woman in an old-fashioned school uniform leaning on the railing of the bridge, looking at the lanterns on the water with her chin on her hands. A child running along the edge of the pavement, among the living children, in a striped T-shirt, dripping wet, with a small brown dog at his heels, both of them laughing without any sound, and the living children ran with them a little way without knowing why, and laughed too.

Nobody was frightened. Nobody was hungry. Nobody was leaning on anybody. They were only there. Like the living. Out late on a warm night at the end of the summer, because there was nowhere they had to be.

Rei walked through the middle of it with his door off and his friend beside him, and felt all of it, and held none of it, and let it go through.

*The middle,* he thought. *Not holding the line. Walking down it.*

"You're smiling," said Itsuki. "It's disgusting. You're as bad as your brother."

"Shut up."

"You shut up."

They went to work.

[break The last page]

Gin got home at two in the morning with paint on his sleeve.

The flat was dark. Rei's door was shut. Itsuki's door was shut, with a line of light under it, and the smell of turpentine, which meant somebody had come to the Kuroda house with something bad, and an answer was being painted. Gin did not knock.

He made tea, too strong, in the brown pot with the chipped lid. He took down three cups out of habit. He poured all three, and put the third at the end of the table, by the empty chair.

Then he sat down at the kitchen table, under the yellow light, and opened his black field book at the back, at a page with a chapter heading on it in his own handwriting. *Fourteen.* He had written the heading two years ago, on the twelfth of August, at this table, before he went out. He had meant to finish it when he got home.

He had been writing it for a year now. In the evenings. When everybody was asleep. Not as a report. It did not fit in a report. It had a beach in it, and a table, and a man in an indigo coat, and a mender from the other side who had learned to say thank you for the tea. It had his brother in it, taking his door off with his own hands on a black beach, because nobody had ever taught him anything else to do with a door except open it.

So he had written it as you write about somebody you love when you are trying very hard to be fair to them. From the outside. As if it had happened to somebody else. A little brother called Rei, who lost his big brother on the eve of Obon and went looking for him all year through a city full of the dead.

He wrote the last pages that night.

At about four in the morning he got up and went to the end of the table and picked up the third cup, which had sat there since two and gone stone cold.

It was empty.

He looked at it for a long time. It had been full when he poured it. He had watched himself pour it.

Then he smiled, and put it in the sink, and sat back down. It was a Tuesday. He had forgotten.

He looked at the last page, and at the window going grey with the morning, and at the key on its hook by the door, and at the painting of the walkway hung beside it, and at the door of the little room down the hall where his brother was asleep with his feet inside the end of the bed.

And he wrote the last line.

And that is how I got back to my brother.
