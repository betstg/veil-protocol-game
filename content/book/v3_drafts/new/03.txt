#title The Book

The Kita Ward Police headquarters stood on the main avenue below Ōji station, a long train ride back up from Marunouchi, and it did not look like a tax office. It looked like a police station, which in its own way was worse.

[img off_police.png | Kita Ward Police]

Rei had worked there for eighteen months as a criminal investigation intern. That sounds more exciting than it was. Mostly it meant photocopying, and filing, and carrying boxes up and down stairs, and making tea for detectives who did not say thank you. Sometimes it meant going out to a scene with one of the senior officers and standing behind the tape holding a clipboard while they did the real work. He liked those days and hated them in equal amounts. He liked them because he was good at it. He would stand at the edge of a room where something bad had happened, and before anyone told him anything he would know where it had happened, and roughly how, and sometimes roughly why. The detectives thought he had a good eye. He hated them because of what came home with him afterward. Eighteen months of other people's worst days, soaked into him a little at a time, the way smoke soaks into a coat. He dealt with that the way a lot of twenty-year-olds deal with things. Badly, and with cigarettes, and by not thinking about it.

He had never told Gin about the scenes. Gin had never asked. Gin had simply started leaving a clean shirt on the back of the bathroom door on the evenings Rei came home from one, and a pot of hōjicha on the table, and had never said a word about either. The Uedera brothers were extremely good at not saying things. It was, in a way, the family business.

He was late. He had never been late before. The sergeant on the front desk looked at his face and at his creased shirt from the night before and said nothing, and waved him through.

[break The form]

His supervisor was a tired inspector in his fifties named Kumagai, who had a desk by the window on the second floor and a framed photograph of a very ugly dog. Rei waited until Kumagai had finished his coffee, because that was the rule on the second floor, and then stood in front of his desk and asked for three days' leave, and for a missing person report.

Kumagai looked at him over his glasses.

"Who's missing?"

"My brother. Uedera Gin. Twenty-eight. He didn't come home last night. He doesn't answer his phone. His key was outside our door at quarter to four this morning."

"Your brother's the CVD one."

"Yes."

Kumagai took his glasses off and rubbed the red marks on either side of his nose. He was not an unkind man. He was a man who had been doing this job for thirty years, and the job had worn him down to the shape of the job.

"Uedera," he said, "it's Obon. Your brother works for the Civil Veil Division. In Obon week those people don't sleep, they don't eat, and they don't call their mothers. He's been gone, what, one night?"

"He always comes home."

"He's twenty-eight." Kumagai leaned back in his chair until it creaked. "Twenty-eight, single, works nights, and it's a festival. Come on, Uedera. You've been here long enough. You know how many grown men we get reported missing on a Friday night who turn up on Saturday morning with a headache and a girl's phone number written on their hand?" He smiled, not unkindly. "He went for a drink after his shift. Somebody bought him another one. He fell asleep on a friend's sofa in Akabane. Happens every week."

"Gin doesn't drink like that."

"Everybody drinks like that at Obon."

"He doesn't. He has one beer and then he goes home. He hates bars. He says they're loud and the toilets are always wet." Rei heard his own voice getting faster and could not stop it. "He reads every message I send him. Every single one. Even when he's working. Last night he didn't read any of them. He didn't read *one*."

"So his phone died."

"His key was outside our door."

"So he dropped it." Kumagai spread his hands. "He was drunk, he dropped his key on the way out, he went back down the stairs to look for a taxi, and he's asleep in some love hotel in Ikebukuro right now with his shoes on. I've done it myself. Not the love hotel. The shoes."

"He didn't go out. He went to *work*. He left at six in the morning. He never came back."

"Uedera." Kumagai's voice went patient, which was worse. "Listen to yourself. Your big brother didn't come home for one night, and you've been up all night working yourself into a state, and now you've come in here looking like you've been dug up, asking me to open a missing person file on a CVD officer in the middle of Obon. You know what they'd say upstairs if I wrote that? *Adult male, did not come home after a public festival. No sign of a crime.* They'd laugh me out of the building."

"I haven't called his office yet."

"Then call his office. They'll tell you he's on assignment." Kumagai picked up a pen, which on the second floor meant the conversation was over. "He's on assignment. Or he's in a love hotel. Either way he's a grown man and he's not my case. Take today. Go home. Get some sleep. If he's not back in three or four days, come and see me and we'll think again."

"Three or four days."

"Adults don't go missing after one night, Uedera. They go out."

Rei stood in front of the desk with his hands at his sides. He could feel the key in his trouser pocket, against his leg, cold through the cloth. He wanted to put it on the desk next to the photograph of the ugly dog and say, *feel that, feel it, tell me that's a man who went out.* He did not. He had seen enough people do that kind of thing at that kind of desk, over eighteen months, to know exactly how it ended. They ended up in the small room downstairs with a cup of tea and a female officer being very kind to them.

"Thank you, sir," he said, and bowed, and went.

[break The front desk]

He did not go home. He went down to the front desk and filled in the missing person form himself, every box, in his neatest handwriting, the way he had watched a hundred frightened people fill it in. Name. Age. Height. Clothing when last seen. Distinguishing features. *Long black hair past the jaw. Tattoos on both arms. Carries a black notebook.* He signed it and handed it over.

The sergeant on the front desk was a big cheerful man called Ono who had worked there for twenty years and called all the interns *kid*. He read the form slowly, from the top to the bottom, moving his lips a little. Then he looked up at Rei with a kind, crinkly, completely useless smile.

"Kid," he said. "Kumagai just rang down. He said three or four days."

"It's been one night."

"One night." Ono nodded, very sympathetic. "It's Obon, kid. Half the men in Kita are sleeping it off somewhere right now. Your brother probably met someone at the festival and went home with her, and now he's too embarrassed to call." He winked. "Good-looking man, is he? Long hair? Well, there you go. Give him till Monday. He'll walk in with a hangover and a stupid grin, and you'll feel like an idiot, and we'll all have a good laugh."

He slid the form back across the counter.

Rei looked at it.

He thought about the plate of ginger pork going grey on the counter. The word *Delivered* that would not change. The brass hook with nothing on it. The key that had been cold since quarter to four and had not warmed up once. Someone at the festival. A hangover and a stupid grin.

He picked up the form and folded it in half, very neatly, along the line, and then in half again. He put it in his pocket, next to the key. And something inside him that had been holding very still since three o'clock that morning, very carefully, the way you carry a glass that is full to the brim, tipped over.

"Fuck this," he said. Quite quietly. And then not quietly at all. "Fuck this. Fuck the form. Fuck Monday. My brother didn't go home with any woman. He's not like that. He's gone."

Ono stared at him with his mouth open. Two officers by the door turned round. A woman sitting on the bench by the wall with a little boy put her hands over the boy's ears.

Rei stood at the counter breathing hard, with the folded form in his pocket and his hands shaking. He made himself breathe slower. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. He felt his face go hot, and then cold.

"You know what?" he said, much more quietly. "Forget it. I'm going home. I'll wait."

"Sorry," said Rei to the woman. He meant it. He bowed to her, and to the boy. Then he turned round and walked out of the Kita Ward Police headquarters, and he did not bow to anybody else on the way.

[break Arai]

He stood on the pavement outside with the folded form in his pocket and the traffic going past, and took out his phone, and wrote to Itsuki.

*kumagai says wait three or four days. ono says he went home with a woman.*

The answer came at once. *ono is a potato.*

Rei stood there for a moment. Then he wrote more than he meant to.

*arai heard me shouting. he didnt even come down. eighteen months i sat in that little room with him deciding which cases were ours and which ones went up to the cvd. he trusts my eye. he doesnt believe gin is gone. nobody in there does. the police cant do anything. its not their kind of missing.*

There was a long pause. The three little dots came and went twice.

*then it's ours,* wrote Itsuki. *go home. open the book.*

[break The konbini]

He walked home the long way, up the hill, past the konbini at the bottom of the street where Gin bought his coffee every morning. He went in and asked the girl at the till if she had seen a tall man with long hair, in a dark coat, who always bought the same black canned coffee and said thank you in a voice like a radio announcer. She thought about it. She said he had come in yesterday, early, about six. He had bought his coffee. He had said thank you. He had seemed fine.

*He had seemed fine.*

Rei walked up the hill and let himself in and stood in the genkan and looked at the empty brass hook for a long time.

[break The book]

He went home and stood in the doorway of Gin's room.

It was two in the afternoon. The flat was hot and still, and the curtains were drawn the way Gin always left them, and the lamp with the green shade was off, and the book lay where it always lay, closed, squared to the edge of the desk, waiting.

Rei had never opened it. In all his life, not once. He had watched Gin write in it at the kitchen table a thousand times, page after page in that small exact hand, and Gin had always closed it, gently, without hurry, when Rei came near. And Rei had never minded. It was Gin's. Some things you leave alone because you love the person they belong to, and that had always been enough.

It was not enough now.

He sat down in Gin's chair. He switched on the lamp. The circle of yellow light fell on the black cover, worn soft and pale at the corners from years of being carried in a coat pocket.

"Sorry," he said, out loud, to nobody. To Gin. "Sorry. You can shout at me later."

He opened it.

[img res_archive.png | Gin's book]

The first page was not about ghosts. It was not about the CVD or the veil or anything that had happened on the twelfth of August. It was dated a long time ago, years ago, and it began with coffee.

[doc Foreword. Written in the morning.]
Coffee going cold on the left. I almost forgot my keys again today. I do this every morning. I put them on the hook by the door because I read somewhere that building habits around physical anchors helps people like us stay grounded. People whose minds tend to slip sideways before the rest of them catches up. I put them on the hook. I make coffee. I smoke half a cigarette on the step outside because Rei complains about the smell in the apartment even though he leaves his own cigarettes everywhere. I come back in lightheaded and slightly late and reach for the keys and they are exactly where I put them and I am surprised every time.

Rei thinks this is funny. He has started moving them slightly, just a few centimetres, to see if I notice. I notice. I don't tell him I notice. It's the most reliable part of my morning.
[/doc]

Rei stopped reading.

He sat in his brother's chair with both hands flat on the desk on either side of the book, and he did not cry, because he did not know how to start and was afraid that if he did he would not know how to stop. He sat there and breathed. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. The latch dropped in his chest. He took the key out of his pocket and put it on the desk, in the circle of lamplight, next to the book, and it lay there, cold, with its little bead of water.

Then he turned the page.

[doc Foreword, continued.]
If you are reading this and I am not available to explain it, use it. All of it. The official sections are accurate. The personal notes are more accurate. The things I have not written explicitly but have gestured toward in the margins, those are the most accurate of all, and I trust you to read them correctly.

If you are reading this and I am still around, hello. Stop reading my notes. Go eat something.

Rei, if this is you, and I know there is a reasonable chance this is you, the rent is paid through the end of the month. The contact number on page 47 is someone who will help you if you ask correctly. Do not lead with what you need. Ask how they are first. They will respect that.
[/doc]

Rei read that part three times.

*Ask how they are first.* That was Gin. That was exactly Gin. He had written it years ago, on some ordinary morning with his coffee going cold, for a day he hoped would never come, the way people write down where they keep the spare fuses. Rei could almost hear him saying it, in that low patient voice. *Question seven, Itsuki. Ask how they are first, Rei.*

He found page 47. There was a name on it, and a phone number, and the name meant nothing to him at all. He wrote it on the back of his hand in biro, the way Gin would have hated, and kept reading.

Then he picked up his phone.

He looked at the name on the back of his hand for a long time. *Do not lead with what you need. Ask how they are first.* He typed, and deleted it, and typed again, and in the end he wrote it the way Gin would have written it, short and polite and with nothing in it that was not true.

*Good afternoon. My name is Uedera Rei. I am Uedera Gin's younger brother. I found your number in his book. I hope you are well, and I am sorry if this is a bad time.*

He did not write what he needed. He sent it.

It said *Delivered*. He watched it for twenty minutes. It did not say *Read*.

He put the book back in the middle of the desk, squared to the edge, the way it always lay. Then he stood up. If Gin was on assignment, somebody in that grey building in Marunouchi knew where.

[break The tax office]

He took the Keihin-Tōhoku line twenty minutes south to Tokyo station, and walked out of the Marunouchi exit into the heat.

The CVD had its headquarters in Marunouchi, the business district between Tokyo station and the moat of the Imperial Palace, and the building looked exactly like a tax office, because it was meant to.

Marunouchi is where Japan keeps its money. Glass towers, banks, insurance companies, men in dark suits walking fast with their phones against their ears. In the middle of all that glass, the Division was a grey block of no particular shape, many storeys of concrete and dusty windows, with a row of bicycles chained to a railing and a vending machine by the door that sold hot corn soup even in August. Your eye slid off it. Thousands of office workers walked past it every day on their way to the station and never once looked up. Gin had taken the train down to it every morning for six years, with his coffee and his half cigarette inside him and his key on its hook at home, and come back again every night, and that had been the whole of what Rei knew about it.

It was nearly four in the afternoon when he went in.

The lobby was cool and grey and smelled of floor polish and cheap coffee. There was a row of plastic chairs along one wall, and a rack of leaflets about what to do if the dead came into your kitchen, and a poster of the white owl in its little police cap, smiling. Behind a long counter a young man in a white shirt and a lanyard was arranging pens in a pot. He looked up and smiled at Rei with the bright, smooth, untroubled smile of someone whose whole job was to be the first face people saw when they were frightened.

"Good morning! How can I help you today?"

"My name is Uedera Rei," said Rei. "My brother is Uedera Gin. He's a field officer here. Kita district." He heard his own voice come out flat and careful, the way he talked at the police station when he was reading a statement back to someone. "He didn't come home last night. He doesn't answer his phone. I'd like to speak to someone in Field Operations, please."

The young man's smile did not change at all. But his hand, arranging the pens, stopped for about half a second, and then went on arranging them.

"Of course," he said. "Please take a seat. I'll just call up for you."

Rei sat on a plastic chair. The young man picked up the phone on his desk and turned away a little, the way people do when they do not want you to read their lips, and spoke into it very quietly for a long time. Then he put it down and typed something. Then he picked it up again and spoke for longer. A woman with a stack of folders came down the stairs and went past the counter and glanced at Rei, once, and went out of the front door. A man in a creased suit came in from the street with two coffees and went up the stairs without looking at anyone. The white owl on the poster went on smiling.

Rei held the key in his pocket. It was still cold.

After twenty-five minutes the young man came round from behind the counter. He did not sit down. He stood in front of Rei with his hands clasped politely in front of him and the same smile, very slightly more careful now, the way you carry a full cup.

"Mr. Uedera, thank you so much for waiting. I've checked for you. Officer Uedera is currently on assignment. During Obon our field officers often work extended operations, and sometimes they're not able to contact family for a little while. I completely understand how worrying that must be. I'm sure he'll be in touch as soon as he's able."

"On assignment where?"

"I'm afraid I can't share operational details. I hope you understand."

"Can I speak to someone on his team? His supervisor?"

"Everyone in Field Operations is out on Obon duties this afternoon." The smile did not move. "But I've passed your concern up, and someone will contact you if there's anything you need to know."

"His key was on the walkway outside our door," said Rei. "At quarter to four this morning. It was wet. It was cold. It's still cold. Feel it." He took it out of his pocket and held it out. "Please. Just feel it."

The young man looked at the key on Rei's palm.

He did not touch it. He looked at it for a little too long, and something went across his face very quickly, the way a cloud goes across a car park, and then the smile came back.

"I'll make a note of that for you," he said gently. "Is there anything else I can help you with today?"

Rei looked at him. He looked at the stairs, where the man with the two coffees had gone up. He looked at the poster of the owl.

"No," he said. "Thank you."

He stood up and bowed, because Gin had brought him up properly and there was no point letting that go to waste on a day like this one, and walked out through the glass doors into the heat.

He stood on the pavement outside for a while with the key in his fist and the trains going past behind him. Then he looked up, without quite knowing why, at the grey face of the building. Rows and rows of dusty windows. On the third floor, in one of them, someone was standing with a cup in their hand, looking down at the street. At him. When he looked up, they stepped back from the glass, and the window was empty.

It was half past four in the afternoon. Thirteen hours had passed since a cold key turned up on the walkway, and the building where Gin had worked for six years had just told his brother, very politely, to go home and wait.

Rei went home to wait for Itsuki.

[break Night one]

Itsuki came at seven with his laptop and two bags of food from the konbini and his hair still full of blue paint.

He did not say anything when Rei opened the door. He just looked at him, and put the bags down in the genkan, and put both arms round him and held on, hard, for about five seconds, with his chin on Rei's shoulder and his laptop bag banging against Rei's back. Then he let go and went into the kitchen and started taking things out of the bags as if nothing had happened, talking about a man on the train who had been eating a whole cabbage like an apple.

That was Itsuki. You got exactly five seconds, and then a cabbage.

He set the laptop up on Gin's desk, next to the lamp with the green shade, beside the closed black book. He did not touch the book. He moved it very carefully two centimetres to the left to make room, with one finger, and then looked at Rei, and moved it back.

"Sorry," he said. "Reflex."

The program was on Gin's old computer, the one he used for the case notes he brought home. Itsuki typed. Itsuki swore. Itsuki typed more. Rei sat on the end of Gin's bed with his knees up and watched him and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say.

"Okay," said Itsuki at last, very quietly. "Last file he opened. Wednesday. Two fourteen in the morning."

He turned the screen round.

It was a scan of one of his own drawings. Rei knew it from the folder in the studio. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table in the middle, and nothing else in it at all.

"I hate that drawing," said Itsuki.

"Why?"

"I don't know." He closed the laptop. "It makes me feel like I've been there."

They sat in the kitchen until late, not saying much. They ate the konbini food without tasting it. Rei smoked three cigarettes by the open window, one after another, and Itsuki took one from the packet and smoked half of it and then stopped with it halfway to his mouth, because he had remembered whose habit that was. He put it out very quickly against the windowsill and did not light another.

At midnight he fell asleep with his head on his arms on the kitchen table, the way he used to when they were fourteen and Gin had kept them up doing maths. Rei put a blanket over him. Then he sat across from him for a while in the dark with the key on the table between them.

Then he went back into Gin's room, and switched on the green lamp, and opened the book again.

He read all night. He did not understand most of it. There were pages about salt and iron and chalk, about thin places and the dead who did not know they were dead, about the Division and its forms and its floors. There were drawings of Itsuki's glued in between the pages, the staircase and the cat and the hand with the teacup. There were notes in the margins in Gin's small exact hand that seemed to be about Rei, and he did not let himself read those yet. Every hour he looked at his phone. The message to the name on the back of his hand still said *Delivered*.

At a quarter to four in the morning Rei turned a page and found a single line, written in a slightly different ink, as if it had been added later, at the bottom of a page about how thin the veil grows in August.

He looked at the clock on the desk.

It was 3:47.
