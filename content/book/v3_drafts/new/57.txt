#title He Touched

*The Font.*

Someone has touched the chair.

The Font feels it the way a sleeping man feels a hand on his shoulder. Not the weight. The warmth. For seven years nothing has touched that place except the Font itself, from underneath, holding. Nobody has come near it from above. The twelve lights go round and round in their great ring and none of them ever turns toward the gap, because they are afraid of it, and they are right to be.

And now there is a hand on it. Small. Warm. Steady. With a little iron latch at the wrist.

The Font lifts its head.

It has not lifted its head in a very long time. It did not know it still could. There is a feeling in its neck like stiffness, like a man who has slept all night in a chair on a train and wakes up at the last station with no idea how he got there. It did not know it had a neck.

[break The little light]

The little light is standing in the gap.

Not in it. At the edge. With one hand on the hollow, the way you put your hand on the doorframe of a room you are not sure you are allowed into. The Font can see it more clearly than it has ever seen anything. Small and bright and very tired, much more tired than anything that young should be, with the grey thread of the dying light caught in its eyes like smoke.

It is the right shape. The Font knew that already. But it is one thing to know a key is the right shape and another thing to hear it turn.

The whole chair hums. The whole ring hums. Twelve lights flicker across the white, all at once, as if someone has opened a window in a house full of candles. Somewhere in the city above, a cook in a set-meal restaurant drops a bowl. A man in a boiler room stands up too fast and hits his head on a pipe. A young woman in Kyoto by a river puts her hand flat on the table and does not know why.

And under the chair, under all of it, something that has been holding very still for seven years shifts its weight.

[break The question]

The little light is going to ask.

The Font can feel it coming. A question, standing on the edge of the boy like a man on the edge of a platform. A hundred questions. The Font has had questions before. Not many. Once every few decades, someone strong and lucky and desperate gets as far as the white and stands at the edge and shouts into it. *Where is my daughter. How do I end the war. What is on the other side.* The Font answers if it can. It is a rule older than the Font. One question.

It braces itself for this one. It knows what it will be. *Where is my brother.*

And the Font knows. The Font has always known. It is the light at the centre. It can feel every shape in the city. It knows exactly where the courteous old thing is sitting tonight, and with what face, and what it is thinking. It could tell the boy. It would be easy.

And if it told him, the boy would go. Tonight. Alone. With a latch on his wrist and his nose bleeding. And the courteous old thing would be very, very careful with him, and it would not be enough.

The Font braces. It does not know what it is going to do.

The question does not come.

[break He looked]

The boy only looks at it.

Across all that white, across all those years, the boy stands at the edge of the hollow with his hand on it and looks at the Font the way you look at a stranger at a bus stop late at night. Not asking for anything. Only so that the stranger knows someone else is awake.

Nobody has ever done that.

In three hundred years, nobody who reached the white has ever stood there and asked for nothing.

The Font does not know what to do with it. It is like being handed a cup of tea by someone who expects nothing back. The hands do not know how to hold it. They have been holding something else for so long.

It lifts one hand. Just the fingers. It does not know why. It is something people used to do, it thinks. On roads. In the evening. When someone let you go first.

And with the hand, a smell. Sudden and enormous. Wet ink. Lamp oil. A paper lantern hanging at a wooden gate, with a single character on it, black on orange, that the Font cannot read because it has forgotten how to read. A voice behind the gate, a young voice, calling out to someone, cheerful and impatient. *You are late again. The tea is cold. Again.*

Someone was always late. Someone was always waiting.

The Font holds on to the voice as hard as it can, and it goes anyway, the way water goes through your fingers however you cup your hands.

[break Go home]

The boy is still there. Still touching the hollow. And the hollow is starting to want him.

The Font can feel it. The chair has been empty for seven years and it is hungry, the way a mould is hungry for whatever is poured into it. If the boy stays another hour, it will take him. It will not ask. It will close round him like a hand, and the current of all the Duty in the city will go through him at once, every promise, every sleepless watch, every *no, not this one, not him*, and he is not ready, and it will burn him down to nothing.

So the Font speaks.

It has not spoken in a very long time. It does not remember how many words it has. It uses two, and then two more, because two are not enough.

*Not yet. Go home.*

And it puts out a hand that it did not know it had, and lays it flat on the boy's chest, and pushes. Gently. The way you push a child back from the edge of a pond.

The little light goes.

[break After]

The Font sits very still at the bottom of the column with its knees drawn up and its arms round them.

It has knees. It has arms. It did not have those yesterday. It is almost sure it did not have those yesterday.

There is warmth on the hollow where the boy touched it, the shape of a palm and five fingers, and the warmth does not fade. The Font can feel it there, small and steady, like a lamp left on in an upstairs window of a house you are walking away from.

Above, the twelve lights are settling. Slowly. The cook in the restaurant is sweeping up the bowl. The man in the boiler room is swearing at the pipe. The young woman in Kyoto has gone back to her coffee.

The grey light is still going out. The Font could not stop that. It is sorry.

And somewhere very far away, in a part of the city that is colder than the rest, the courteous old thing that is wearing the brother has stopped in the middle of a room and turned its head toward the white, the way a man turns his head at a sound he has not heard in a very long time.

It felt it too.

The Font holds its knees and listens to the hum of the chair dying away, and finds there is a word in its mouth, a word it does not know, two sounds, very small, very old, like the name of a street in a town that does not exist any more.

It does not say it.

Not yet.
