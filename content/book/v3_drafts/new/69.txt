#title One Minute

*Gin.*

He had been asking for a minute since the bridge.

He had asked before that, too. For months. Since the kite market, since he had said *my brother* out loud in a shrine yard and watched the boy bleed for a stranger. He had asked every time the boy came near. *One minute. Let me talk to him. Just one.* And every time the voice had said the same thing, in the same polite flat way, like a clerk reading out a rule.

*No. If I step back, I am weaker. If I am weaker, she feels it. If she feels it, she looks. And if she looks at you, she sees him. She has not looked at him properly yet. Do not make her.*

It had been true. He had known it was true. He had hated it and he had known it was true.

But tonight the old man was dead, and the grey man in Ochanomizu was dead, and the voice had told him its name over three cups of tea, and on the bridge the boy had stood three metres away and said *Gin* to a face that did not answer.

Gin had not asked, after the bridge. He had not had to. He had come up from the bottom of the dark so hard and so fast that the voice had stumbled in the street, in Gin's body, like a man tripping on a kerb.

*Stop,* said the voice.

*No.*

*She is close. The river is full of her. I can feel her from here.*

*Then she can feel me too,* said Gin. *I'm not asking any more. I'm telling you. I'm going to him.*

The voice was quiet for a long time. They were walking fast, down through the back streets toward the old temples, toward Yanaka, where the graves are.

*He is drawing,* said the voice at last. *The painter. In the cemetery. I can feel the pencil. He has drawn us already. He knew we would come.* A pause. *Very well. I will step back. I do not know how long I can hold the rest of you while I do. When I say, you come back down. You do not argue.*

*One minute,* said Gin.

*It will not be one minute,* said the voice. *It never is with you.*

[break The cemetery]

*Rei.*

Itsuki was sitting on a low stone wall at the edge of Yanaka cemetery, under a cherry tree, with his sketchbook open on his knees and a stub of pencil in his fist.

It was nearly dark. The cemetery went away up the hill in rows of grey stones and wooden grave tablets, and on almost every grave, tonight, there was a little paper lantern, white, lit, so that the whole hillside was full of small soft lights among the trees. Families were moving between them, slowly, with buckets of water and bunches of flowers, saying goodbye. Somebody was burning incense. The smoke hung in the still air.

Itsuki looked up when Rei came through the gate with Kato behind him.

He did not say anything. He turned the sketchbook round and held it up.

On the page, in pencil, very fast, there was this cemetery. This wall. This cherry tree. And a tall man in a grey jacket standing on the path in front of the wall, among the lanterns, with his hand on top of somebody's head.

"An hour ago," said Itsuki. His voice was very flat. "On the train. I drew it on the train." He put the sketchbook down on the wall beside him, face up, and put his hand flat on it. "I've been sitting here for twenty minutes. I've been sitting here deciding whether to hit him."

"Itsu..."

"Don't." Itsuki stood up. "Just don't."

Kato had stopped on the path. He was looking up the hill, between the graves.

"He's coming," he said. Very quietly. "Rei. He's coming down the path. And it's..." He stopped. His face did something strange. "It's loud. It's so loud. It's in capitals."

[break Sixty]

He came down the path between the lanterns.

He walked differently. Rei saw it at once, from fifty metres. Not straight. Not like an officer waiting to be inspected. He walked fast and a little crooked, with his head down and his shoulders forward, like a man walking into rain, and he stumbled once on the uneven stones and caught himself with one hand on a grave, and said something under his breath that was definitely a swear word, and kept coming.

He stopped on the path in front of the wall.

His eyes were moving. Everywhere. Over Rei's face, and down, and up, and over Itsuki, and Kato, and the crow on the cherry branch, and back to Rei's face. Quick and dark and sideways first and then straight, noticing everything, as they had noticed everything every day of Rei's life.

The mouth opened. Closed. Opened.

"*Rei*," said Gin.

It was his voice. Not the polite one. Not the careful one setting each word down like a cup. Rough and dry and a little too fast, with a crack in the middle of it.

Rei could not move.

"Don't," said Gin. "Don't stand there. I don't know how long I've got. Don't waste it standing there like an idiot." His hand came up. It was shaking. It came up slowly, as if it weighed a great deal, and stopped in the air between them. "Can I. I don't know if I can do it on my own."

Rei stepped forward. Into it. Under it.

Gin's hand came down on his head.

Heavy. Warm. Shaking. On the top of his head, as it had when he was four and frightened of the thing at the window, and when he was eight at the kitchen table learning to close the door in his chest, and the morning of his police exam, ruffling his hair so hard he had to fix it again in the train window. The fingers moved once. Not ruffling. Just holding. Just checking he was there.

"You're so thin," said Gin. His voice broke in half. "You're so *thin*. What have you been eating. Don't tell me. I know. Rice and hot water and those horrible pickled plums, standing at the counter..."

[break Itsuki]

Itsuki hit him.

Not hard. Not with a fist. He came off the wall and shoved Gin in the chest with both hands, so that Gin rocked back a step on the stones and his hand came off Rei's head.

"*Where were you,*" said Itsuki.

Gin looked at him.

"Where were you," said Itsuki again. His voice was shaking so much it hardly came out. "A year. A whole year. You could walk. I've seen you walk. You walked past my drawings every week. You went to the old man's house and drank his tea. You went to the bank. You bought a *rice cooker*." He shoved him again. Gin did not stop him. "You could have come to the flat. You could have stood at the bottom of the stairs for one second. You could have sent one message. One. He's been sleeping on the sofa with his foot off the end for a year waiting for the door. Did you think we weren't looking? Did you think he'd just stop?"

"Itsuki," said Kato quietly.

"*No.*" Itsuki was crying now, furiously, with his fists clenched. "No. He gets to hear it. He gets to hear all of it. I've been drawing him in the dark for a year and I didn't know if he was alive." He turned back to Gin. "Why didn't you come?"

Gin stood on the path among the lanterns and let himself be shouted at.

"I couldn't," he said. "At first. It had me under the floor. I couldn't move a finger. I couldn't see." He swallowed. "And then I could, a little. And then more. Since the spring, I could have. Some nights. It would have let me, some nights." He looked at Rei. Not at Itsuki. At Rei. "And I didn't. Because there's a woman in Asagaya who doesn't know what he is yet. And the second she sees me walk up to him, she'll know he's the thing I'd come back for. And she'd take him. To get at us. Like she took the old man."

Itsuki stood very still.

"So I stayed away," said Gin. "I walked past your drawings. I drank the old man's tea. I stood at the bottom of the hill some nights and looked up at the walkway and went away again." His voice cracked. "You think I didn't want to? I could hear him through the walls. I could hear him every night. I counted the nights."

Itsuki looked at him for a long time.

Then he sat back down on the wall, very suddenly, as if his legs had gone, and put his face in his hands.

"You're an idiot," he said into his hands. "You're the stupidest man I've ever met."

"Yes," said Gin. "I know. Ask Haruki. He's got a list."

[break Everything]

Rei told him.

He did not know how long they had. Nobody did. So he told it fast, standing in the path with the lanterns all round them and the families moving between the graves, in the order it came out. The key on the walkway at 3:47. The police. The Division. Kamiya and the white. The Kuroda. Itsuki in Room Nine. The tattoo. The kite market, and the old woman with the scissors, and the cut on his arm. The team. The temple. Kyoto. Tendo. The Capturer. Kōhei. The radio. The flat. Asagaya. The cup he pushed away. Katagiri's pins. The old man on the step. Watanabe in the chair. The thread. Catching it like a moth.

Gin listened to all of it with his eyes on Rei's face and did not interrupt once.

When Rei got to Tanizaki, Gin closed his eyes.

"I felt it," he said. "From a bench. Two streets away. He turned the cup." He opened them. "He told us not to come. So we didn't."

"He saved you," said Rei. "He knew she'd come for you. He sat on the step instead."

"I know." Gin's face was wet. "I know he did. He always did."

When Rei got to the freight yard, and the sending fires, and the white flowers on every thin place in the city, Gin's face went very still.

"Tonight," he said.

"Tonight."

Gin looked up the hill, at the lanterns, at the dark coming down over the cemetery.

"I can't leave you," he said. "Either of you. I can see that now. I can't go back down there and leave you two standing on your own in the road again." He looked at Itsuki on the wall, and at Rei, and at Kato standing on the path with his hand in his pocket on the knight. "But I promised it. The thing in me. I promised I'd help it end her. It's the only reason it came through. It's the only thing it wants. And it's kept every promise it's ever made to me." His jaw set. "I'm not breaking mine. Not tonight. Not to her."

"Then don't," said Rei.

Gin looked at him.

"Help it," said Rei. "Tonight. We'll be there too. Everybody will. We'll be there anyway." He swallowed. "And after. After, we sort it out. You and me and Itsuki and it. All of it. At the kitchen table."

Gin was quiet for a long moment.

"After," he said. "Yes. We'll sort it out after." He almost smiled. "It's going to hate the kitchen table. It doesn't like chairs with wobbly legs."

[break When]

The body swayed.

Like a man on a train when it brakes. Gin caught himself with one hand on the wall beside Itsuki, and Rei saw his eyes start to go still. From the edges in. Like water freezing on a pond.

"Rei," said Gin quickly. "Quick. You still have my key?"

Rei pulled the cord out of the neck of his shirt. The brass key on its plain steel ring. The little dent where it had caught in a car door, years ago, and Gin had sworn about it for a whole afternoon.

Gin looked at it.

"Good," he said. "Keep it. Don't put it on the hook." He swallowed. "I'll need it. To get in. When I come home."

Rei's fist closed round the key. "When."

"*When*," said Gin. His hand came round, slowly, shaking, and closed over Rei's fist, over the key, as it had closed over Rei's hand on the way to school when he was small and they had to cross the big road at the bottom of the hill. "When. Not if. Say it."

"When," said Rei.

"Good." Gin's eyes were nearly still now. Only the very centre of them was still moving, still looking, still trying to write it all down. "Tell Haruki to spend the money. Tell Kato I can hear him too. Tell..."

He stopped.

The hand on Rei's hand went heavy. Then still. Then careful. And the fingers opened, slowly, one at a time, very politely, like someone letting go of something that does not belong to them.

The tall man straightened up on the path among the lanterns, and stood very straight, with his weight even on both feet.

He looked at Rei. Straight. Not sideways first.

"That was more than one minute," he said.

"Yes," said Rei.

"He said it would not be." The tall man looked down at his own hand, the one that had held the key, as if it belonged to somebody else. Then he looked up the hill, east, toward the river, toward something only he could feel. "She felt it. She knows now. She is looking for you." He looked back at Rei. "Go to the river. Stand where I tell you, when I tell you. Do not stand in front of me. I will not stop for you."

He turned and walked away up the path between the lanterns, and the families stepped aside for him without knowing why.

Gorō dropped off the cherry branch onto Rei's shoulder.

Itsuki was still sitting on the wall. He had his sketchbook on his knees. He had turned to a new page, and he was not drawing. He was looking at it. White. Empty.

"I can't see what happens next," he said. His voice was very small. "I've tried. Since the train. It's just white."
