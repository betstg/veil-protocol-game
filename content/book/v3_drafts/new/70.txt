#title What the Director Knows

The Director's message came while they were still in the cemetery. Not to Rei. To Kato, on the Division phone, in four words.

*Bring him. Fourth floor.*

"Now?" said Itsuki. "Tonight? He wants a *meeting* tonight?"

"He doesn't do meetings," said Kato. He was already walking to the gate. "He does four words. I've had four words from him three times in two years. Every time, it was the right four words."

They drove into Marunouchi through the emptying city with the sending fires coming out on the doorsteps of every street they passed, one after another, small and orange, and the smoke going straight up. The Division building was nearly dark. Obon. The lobby lights were on and the guard at the desk was eating noodles out of a pot and did not look up. The lift was empty. On the fourth floor the corridor was dark except for one open door at the end, with yellow light coming out of it.

Adachi was not at the window.

He was sitting behind his desk. Rei had seen him sit down perhaps twice. He sat with his hands flat on the blotter and the worn train pass square to the edge between them, and Riki on his cushion by the desk with his scarred leg out, awake, watching the door. The Director's grey suit was the same grey suit. His face was grey and tired and older than Rei remembered from the spring, as if someone had been pressing on it from the inside.

"Sit," he said. "Kato, you too. Kuroda, there's a chair by the bookcase. Bring it."

Itsuki stopped in the doorway.

"I'm not Division," he said.

"No," said Adachi. "You're the one who drew where my officer would be every day for a month before he got there, and never put it in a report." He looked at Itsuki with his pale resting eyes. "Bring the chair."

Itsuki brought the chair.

[break The roof]

"I'm going to tell you some things," said Adachi. "Most of them I've never told anyone. Some of them I should have told your brother three years ago, and didn't, because I was being careful." He put one finger on the train pass. "Being careful is a thing I'm very good at. It's how I've stayed in this office for twelve years. It's also part of the reason your brother is where he is tonight."

Nobody said anything.

"I was a field officer," said Adachi. "Before this. For twenty-two years. I was quite good." He looked at the pass. "One winter, a long time ago, I was on a roof in Kita, in the rain, at two in the morning, on a case that had gone badly wrong. A family on the floor below, and something in the walls, and I'd been holding it back for an hour and I was nearly done. And a man came up the fire escape."

Riki's ears went up.

"An old man. Very ordinary. A cardigan. Slippers, I think. He walked across the roof in the rain and put his hand on my shoulder, and the thing in the walls stopped. Just stopped. As if somebody had told it to go home, and it had remembered it had one." Adachi's voice did not change. "He told me what he was. He told me there were thirteen chairs in an old room in Ochanomizu, and thirteen people who sat in them, and that he sat in the one at the end that nobody ever gave a name to. He said nobody needed to. He said you could tell who belonged in it by watching who stayed."

Rei had gone very still.

"I asked him what he meant," said Adachi. "He said, *you've been on this roof for an hour, in the rain, holding a door, for people you'll never meet. Everybody else went home. You stayed. That's the shape.*" He turned the pass over. "Then he said I wasn't quite it. Close. Not quite. I stayed because I was angry. The shape stays because it said it would."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I visited him after that. Once a month. For years. In a little flat behind the shopping street in Akabane, with a cat and a garden on the balcony and a job doing the accounts for a tofu shop. We drank tea. He never told me anything I asked. He told me a great many things I didn't." The pass stopped. "He rang me one night at four in the morning and said *come and sit with me*, and I went. He'd been ill a long time and told nobody. I held his hand. And when he went, I felt the chair. Looking. Going round the little room like a dog that has lost its owner. Sniffing at the cat. At the curtains. At me." His voice was very level. "And it went past me. And out of the window. And it has been empty ever since."

[break The shape]

"Your brother," said Adachi, "came into this building at twenty-two, and sat the assessment, and the readings said he was reliable and middling and nothing remarkable." He looked at Rei. "I watched him for a year. On cases. Once, in person, from across a street in Ōtsuka, while he talked a frightened woman out of a cupboard for three hours and missed his last train and walked home. And I knew. Not what he could do. What shape he was. He was holding himself down, every day, for reasons he never told me. And under all that, there was the shape I'd seen once before on a roof in the rain."

Rei could not breathe properly.

"I knew," said Adachi. "Three years ago. I should have called him up here and closed the door and said *there is a chair in an old library in Ochanomizu with dust on the seat, and I think it's been waiting for you.* I didn't. I was being careful. I thought there was time. I thought, let him find it himself." He looked down at his hands on the desk. "Last July he came up to this floor and asked my secretary for an appointment. She gave him the first Monday in September. I didn't move it. I had a budget hearing."

The room was very quiet. Outside, very faintly, a train went over a bridge.

"Then he was taken," said Adachi. "And for months I told myself the chair had lost him. And then his little brother walked into my building in December with a crow and a key round his neck and tore up a paper crane in the toilets on the second floor, because it was making him feel things he hadn't agreed to feel." Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "And I watched you. And it took me a great deal less than a year."

"Why both of us?" said Rei. His voice came out very small.

"Because it isn't a gift," said Adachi. "It isn't passed in the blood like the Kuroda doors. It's a shape. It's what you do. It's two boys in a flat in Jūjō with nobody else, and the older one standing in the doorway at three in the morning saying *no* to whatever was at the window, every night, for years. And the younger one watching him do it. And learning." He looked at Rei steadily. "He made you the same shape as him. Without meaning to. By staying."

Itsuki, on the chair by the bookcase, had put his hand over his mouth.

"That chair called him," said Adachi. "And then it called you, when he was gone. You touched it. You didn't sit. You came back." He picked up the train pass. "Tonight a woman in Asagaya is going to open every door in this city at once. I don't know what that does to an empty chair. Nobody does. But I didn't want you to walk down to that river without knowing what I knew, and when, and what I did about it. Which was nothing." He set the pass down square again. "That's all of it. Now you know too."

[break The phone]

He stood up.

He went to the window. He stood with his back to the room, and Rei watched his shoulders, and saw them go down, very slightly, like a man who has been carrying something heavy for a long time and has just put one corner of it on a table.

"There are people," said Adachi, without turning round, "who used to work in this building. Who retired, or left, or were moved, or were never on the books in the first place. They aren't on any register. They don't answer to Public Confidence or to the families." He paused. "They answer, sometimes, to me. Because I asked them once, a long time ago, if they'd be willing to be asked."

He took a phone out of his inside pocket. Not his Division phone. An old one. A folding one, grey, scratched, the kind nobody had carried for ten years.

He pressed a number. Held it to his ear. Waited.

Somebody answered.

"Kurosawa," said Adachi. "It's me. I'm asking." A pause. "Tonight. The old freight yard past Tabata, by the river. After dark." Another pause, longer. "Yes. That one. Bring whoever will come." A pause. "No. Not an order. I don't give you orders." He listened. Something moved at the corner of his mouth. "Thank you."

He closed the phone and opened it again and pressed another number.

"Shimizu. It's Adachi. Every field team we have, to the river past Tabata. Now. Yes. Moriya too, if he can stand. Tell Okada to bring the cars." He closed it. Opened it. Another number. "Fujiwara-Kage-san. I'm sorry to call you at home on the last night of Bon. I'd like Internal Affairs to observe an operation tonight. Yes. I'd like it written down accurately." He closed it.

He turned round.

"Before you go to the river," he said, "there's a room on the sixth floor I'd like you to see. It hasn't got a number. I've never taken anybody into it." He picked up Riki's lead from the desk, and the old dog got up off the cushion, stiffly, and came to his side. "Your brother never saw it either. I'd like to take that off the list of things I didn't do."

Rei stood up. He did not know what to do with his hands. He bowed. Low. Lower than he had ever bowed to anyone in the building.

"Don't," said Adachi. "You'll embarrass us both." He clipped the lead to Riki's collar. "And when you see him, Uedera. When he's himself. Tell him the appointment's still open. Tell him I moved the budget hearing."
