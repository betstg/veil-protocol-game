#title Obon

[img place_kita_obon_lane.png | Obon lanterns in a lane in Kita]

On the first night of Obon, the dead came home to Kita, and Uedera Rei went out to watch them dance, which tells you most of what you need to know about Uedera Rei.

It was the twelfth of August, 2015. All day the narrow lanes of the ward had been filling up with paper lanterns. White ones for the families who had lost someone that year, hung by the door so the new dead would know which house was theirs. Round red ones strung along the fronts of the shops. Small square ones on bamboo poles beside the gates of the old wooden houses, swinging in a hot wind that came up off the railway lines and cooled nothing. By evening the whole hill smelled of incense and grilled corn and the bitter green smoke of the little fires people lit on their doorsteps to show the way.

Obon is the summer festival when, people believe, the dead come back to visit. For three or four days the living clean the graves and leave rice and fruit on the family altar and light those small fires at the door, so that grandmother can find the house in the dark. Then, at the end, they send her back again, with more fires and with lanterns set floating down the rivers to carry her away. It is a gentle festival. It is mostly about missing people.

In most of Japan it is a custom. In Tokyo, in 2015, it was a little more than that.

[break What everyone knows]

Here is the first thing you need to understand about this city. Everyone knew that the dead were real.

Not the way people know ghost stories, with a shiver and a nervous laugh. The way people know about weather. Between the world of the living and whatever lies on the other side there is a thin boundary, and people called it the veil. Most of the time the veil holds, the way skin holds. Sometimes it grows thin in one place, the way skin grows thin and shiny over an old scar. And sometimes, through the thin places, things come through.

Most of what comes through is small and sad. A grandmother who keeps walking into the kitchen of the flat where she died, looking for her reading glasses. A cold patch on a staircase where a boy fell forty years ago, which every new tenant learns to step around without ever knowing why. The smell of one particular brand of cigarette in a car that has been sold three times and cleaned by every owner. Some of what comes through is not small. Some of it is hungry, and patient, and very old, and it does not want to go back.

The government had an office for this, the way it had offices for fires and floods and stray dogs. It was called the Civil Veil Division, and everyone called it the CVD. Its officers went into the houses and streets where the veil had gone wrong and put things right, or tried to, or filled in a form explaining why they had not. There were posters in every train station in Tokyo. *Felt something strange? Call the CVD. Do not investigate alone.* There was a cartoon mascot, a round white owl in a little police cap, and children drew it at school and stuck it on the fridge. Nobody at the CVD liked the owl, and nobody had ever been able to find out who approved it.

And everybody knew that in Obon week the veil grew thinner than at any other time of the year. The CVD put every officer it had on the streets. People who had lost someone stayed home with the lights on and the door unlocked, just in case.

Rei's older brother, Uedera Gin, was one of those officers. He had been a field officer for six years. In Obon week he hardly came home at all, and when he did he came home smelling of salt and incense and something burnt, and slept on top of the covers in his clothes.

[break The dance]

Itsuki and Haruto were waiting at the bottom of the hill, on the low wall outside the shrine, and arguing.

Rei could hear them from halfway down the lane. Haruto was holding a stick of grilled squid like a knife and pointing it at Itsuki's face to make his point. Itsuki had a sketchbook open on his knee and was drawing the crowd without looking up, and every few seconds he said something short and calm that made Haruto point the squid harder.

Kuroda Itsuki was twenty, a year older than Rei, and he had been Rei's best friend since the first week of middle school, when he had sat down next to Rei at lunch without asking and eaten half of his rice. He had more or less grown up in the flat on the hill after that. His own family lived in a very old, very large house in Nezu full of people who could do things with the veil, and none of them had ever had much use for a boy who would rather paint, so Gin had fed him, and helped him with his homework, and turned up at his school when nobody from Nezu did, and never once made a fuss about it. Now Itsuki studied oil painting at the most famous art school in the country and talked about it, when he talked about it at all, as if he had got in by mistake and was waiting to be found out.

[img char_kuroda-itsuki.png | Kuroda Itsuki]

He was wearing an enormous grey hoodie over a white shirt that had obviously cost more than everything in Rei's wardrobe put together. There was a pair of headphones round his neck that he never seemed to listen to, a pencil pushed through the knot of hair at the back of his head, and an unlit cigarette tucked behind one ear. There was blue paint dried in the creases of his knuckles, because there was always paint somewhere on Itsuki. People who had known him for years had never seen his hands clean.

Nagai Haruto was seventeen and belonged to nobody in particular. Itsuki had found him in the spring, under the railway bridge by the river, where Itsuki liked to sit and draw the trains and Haruto liked to sit and not go home. His mother worked nights, and the flat in the public housing block was small and hot and full of his little sister's crying, and so Haruto spent most of his evenings outside, by the river or on the steps of the konbini, with his hood up and his phone in his hand. He had decided, after about a week of Itsuki drawing him without asking, that Itsuki was weird but harmless. He had decided, after about a week of meeting Rei, that Rei was the coolest person he had ever stood next to. He would have died rather than say so.

He had thick black hair that fell messily over his forehead, the ends gone a faded brown from a dye he was quietly letting grow out. He wore a dark jacket with a hood over a plain white T-shirt, scuffed trainers, and an expression that was calm and level and much older than seventeen, the look of a boy who has learned to check who is watching before he says anything at all.

[img char_nagai-haruto.png | Nagai Haruto]

"Rei," said Haruto, the moment he saw him. He jumped off the wall. "Tell him. Tell him melon's the best ice. He's saying lemon. *Lemon.* Who picks lemon?"

"I pick lemon," said Itsuki, not looking up. "I'm an artist. I make brave choices."

"It's not brave, it's sour."

"Strawberry," said Rei.

Haruto stared at him as if Rei had personally betrayed him in front of his whole crew. Itsuki's mouth twitched at one corner.

"Two against one," said Itsuki. "That's democracy, Haruto. I'm sorry. You can complain to the grandmothers."

"Strawberry isn't even a side. Strawberry's just pink." Haruto stabbed the air with the squid. "And you're late. Itsuki said you'd be late. I said no way, Rei's never late, Rei's like a train. And then you were late, so now I owe him a cigarette."

"You don't smoke," said Rei.

"I smoke sometimes."

"You stole one off a taxi driver last week and coughed for ten minutes," said Itsuki pleasantly, turning a page. "I drew it. It's my best work."

Haruto went red to the ears and pulled his hood further down over his face, and Rei sat on the wall beside Itsuki and took the last piece of squid off the stick before Haruto could object. It was cold and chewy and sticky with sweet sauce, and it was perfect.

"Where's your brother?" said Haruto, from inside the hood.

"Working. All night, probably. It's Obon."

The hood came straight back down. Haruto's eyes had gone wide and bright, and for a second he forgot to look hard at all.

"Wait. Like, right now? He's out there catching ghosts right *now*?" He swung round to look at the dark lanes behind them, at the lanterns swinging by the doors, as if he might see a CVD officer go past at a run. "What does he do, when he finds one? Does he fight it? Has he got, like, a gun for them?"

"He fills in forms about them," said Rei. "Mostly. That's what he says."

"Forms." Haruto looked deeply betrayed. "That's the worst thing I've ever heard."

"He also says the forms are the dangerous part."

"Did he forget his keys again?" said Itsuki.

Rei laughed. "No. I moved them last night. Two centimetres to the left on the hook. He found them this morning anyway. He didn't say anything, but he moved them back two centimetres to the right."

"You two are so weird," said Haruto, with deep respect.

[break The circle]

The Bon dance was in the shrine grounds, the way it was every year. In the middle stood a tall wooden tower hung with lanterns, red and white, with a big drum on top and an old man in a twisted cotton headband beating it with two sticks, slowly and steadily, like a heart. Round the tower, in a wide slow circle, people danced. Grandmothers in light cotton summer kimonos printed with fish and fireworks and morning glories. Small children in tiny ones, copying the hand movements one beat too late. A group of high school boys pretending they were only there to laugh at it, and then forgetting to laugh. Two young policemen at the edge of the crowd, standing very straight in their uniforms and absolutely not tapping their feet.

The dance itself was very old and very simple. Clap. Step forward. Turn your hands in front of you, slowly, as if you were scooping water out of a stream. Step back. Clap again. It went round and round the tower all evening without stopping, because it is a dance for the dead, and the dead, people say, like things that go round and come back to where they started.

"I'm not dancing," said Haruto at once.

"Nobody asked you to dance," said Itsuki, closing his sketchbook. "We're going to dance. You're going to stand at the side looking hard, and in ten minutes a grandmother is going to pull you in by your hood, and it's going to be the best thing that happens to me all year."

It took six minutes.

They joined the circle between a grandmother with a paper fan and a little boy of about six in a kimono printed with blue goldfish. Itsuki was terrible at it, which surprised nobody, because he was concentrating so hard on his hands that he forgot to move his feet. Rei was slightly less terrible, because he was watching the grandmother very closely and copying her. Haruto lasted exactly six minutes at the edge of the crowd, scowling, with his hands in his pockets, before the grandmother with the fan reached out of the circle without even turning her head and took a firm hold of his hood and pulled him in beside her.

"Hands out of your pockets," she told him. "You're not at the station."

"I don't know how to..."

"Then learn. Scoop. Like water. Soft." She slapped his wrist lightly with the fan. "Softer. You're not hitting anybody."

Haruto looked over his shoulder at Rei with an expression of pure despair. Rei laughed so hard he lost the rhythm completely, and the little boy in the goldfish kimono, who had been watching Rei's hands and copying them very seriously, lost the rhythm too, in exactly the same place, so that Rei had to pull himself together at once, out of pure responsibility, so that the child would not learn the dance wrong for the rest of his life.

By the second time round the tower Haruto had stopped scowling. By the third time round he was doing it properly, scooping the water, his face hidden in his hood but his hands soft and careful, and the grandmother nodded at him once, sharply, the way a teacher nods at a good answer. Rei saw his ears go red again.

It was hot, and loud, and the drum went on and on, and Rei found that he was smiling, and that he had been smiling for a while without noticing.

[break Shaved ice]

Afterward Haruto paid his debt to Itsuki with a shaved ice instead of a cigarette, because Itsuki said he would rather have the ice and also would rather Haruto did not die of lung cancer before he finished the painting. They bought three at the stall by the gate, melon for Haruto, strawberry for Rei, lemon for Itsuki, and sat on the stone steps of the shrine with their knees up and their backs against the warm stone and watched the dance go round below them.

"How did you meet him?" said Haruto suddenly, pointing his spoon at Itsuki and looking at Rei. "He never says. He just says *a long time ago*, like an old man."

"He sat next to me at lunch," said Rei, "on the first day of middle school, and ate half my rice."

"I was hungry," said Itsuki.

"He didn't ask."

"You'd have said no."

"I would have said no," Rei agreed. "Then he came home with me after school because he said his house was boring. And my brother made him dinner. And then he just kept coming back."

"Gin makes very good curry," said Itsuki, as if that explained everything, and in a way it did. He was looking at the dance, and something in his face went very quiet for a second, and then came back. "He used to make me do my maths homework at your kitchen table before he'd give me any. I hated him so much. He'd stand there with the plate in his hand, and he'd say, *Question seven, Itsuki,* in that voice."

"That voice," said Rei, and they both did it at the same time, low and patient and completely immovable, *Question seven, Itsuki,* and Haruto laughed so hard that green syrup came out of his nose.

When he had stopped choking, and wiped his face on his sleeve, and pretended it had not happened, he said, a bit too casually, "Is your brother scary? He sounds scary."

Rei thought about it.

"No," he said. "He's the least scary person I know. He's just very hard to argue with."

"He's scary," said Itsuki. "He just doesn't know it. He's scary like a mountain is scary. He doesn't *do* anything. He's just there and you can't move him."

"I want to meet him," said Haruto.

"You will," said Rei. "When Obon's finished. He said he'd take us to the river on the last night, to see the lanterns. Last year he got called out. This year he promised."

"For real?"

"He promised," said Rei. "He doesn't break those."

[break Her]

Haruto went back down to the stall to try to talk the old man into a second ice for free, and Itsuki opened his sketchbook again while Rei finished his. He did it without thinking, the way some people take out their phones, the way you scratch an itch. The pencil came out of his hair, and his hand began to move, and his face went far away, as if part of him had stepped out of the room.

He was drawing the dance. The tower, the lanterns, the turning hands. The grandmother with the fan. The little boy in the goldfish kimono. Quick loose lines that caught the way people moved better than any photograph could.

Rei watched the circle go round below them, and that was when he saw her.

She was dancing between a man in a dark blue kimono and a girl with a paper fan. A small old woman with white hair pinned up in a neat bun, in a faded summer kimono with a pattern of morning glories on it, pale blue and violet. She danced beautifully. Better than anyone in the circle. Every movement of her hands was slow and exact and full of quiet pleasure, as if she had danced this dance every summer for seventy years and had never once got tired of it.

But she was dancing to a different drum.

When the drum on the tower beat, everyone stepped. She did not. She stepped half a beat later, every time, in time with something nobody else could hear. A drum that was far away, or a long time ago. And when she turned under the lanterns, the red light went through her a little, the way light goes through a paper screen when someone stands behind it.

Nobody looked at her. The man in the dark blue kimono danced right beside her, so close that their sleeves should have touched, and never once turned his head.

Rei felt the familiar pressure start behind his eyes. It always began there, a soft weight, like the feeling just before a headache, and it always grew. If he let it, the colours of the festival would go thin and pale, and the music would sound as if it came from the far end of a long corridor, and the old woman would turn her head, slowly, in the middle of the dance, and see that he could see her.

So he did what his brother had taught him when he was small.

He breathed in for four counts. He held it for four. He breathed out for six, slowly, through his nose. And on the out-breath he imagined a door inside his chest, a heavy old wooden door with an iron latch, swinging shut, and he heard the latch drop.

[img scene_kitchen_lesson.png | Gin and Rei at the kitchen table]

Gin had taught him that at the kitchen table when Rei was eight years old and could not sleep because of the things he saw at night. *It's a trick for bad nights,* Gin had said, sitting across from him with a cup of tea going cold between his hands, very seriously, the way he taught Rei everything, as if it were as ordinary and important as tying your shoes. *Close the door. Nothing comes in that you don't invite.* For years Rei had believed it was only a trick to help him sleep. At nineteen he knew better. He did not know exactly what it was that he knew. He only knew that the door worked, and that he had no wish to find out what happened on the day it did not.

The pressure eased and stopped. The festival came back, loud and bright and close. When he looked down at the circle again there was a gap between the man in the dark blue kimono and the girl with the fan, about the width of one small old woman. The dance went round. The gap went round with it, under the lanterns, and then the circle closed up, and it was gone.

She only came home to dance, Rei thought. It's Obon. She's allowed.

Beside him, Itsuki's pencil had stopped.

Rei looked at the sketchbook. Itsuki was frowning down at his own drawing with a puzzled expression, the pencil hanging forgotten in his fingers, the way you look at a word you have written a hundred times when it suddenly seems to be spelled wrong.

It was the dance. The tower, the lanterns, the grandmother, the boy, the man in the dark blue kimono. And between the man and the girl with the fan, in the middle of the circle, there was a small old woman with her hair in a bun, turning her hands in the air, drawn so lightly and so faintly that you could almost see the paper through her.

"Huh," said Itsuki quietly.

"Who's that?" said Rei, very carefully.

"No idea." Itsuki tilted the sketchbook toward the lantern light, and then away. "I didn't see her. I just drew." He laughed, a short uneasy laugh, and did not look at Rei. "My hand does that sometimes. Puts people in who aren't there. My teachers think it's a style. They call it *haunted* and give me good marks for it." He shut the sketchbook with a snap and pushed the pencil back into his hair. "Don't look at me like that. It's nothing. I've been awake since yesterday."

Rei did not say anything. He looked at the place in the circle where the old woman had been. One day, he thought, he was going to have to tell Itsuki about the door in his chest. Not tonight. Tonight was shaved ice and goldfish and Haruto's red ears. But one day.

[break Home early]

He left at half past eight.

"It's *Obon*," said Haruto, who had come back up the steps without a second ice and with the injured face of someone who had been refused it. "Nobody goes home at half past eight at Obon."

"There's going to be karaoke," said Itsuki. "There's a man over there with a guitar and a very sad face. I need to know what he sings. I need to know it more than I've ever needed anything."

"I've got work tomorrow," said Rei.

"You're an *intern*," said Itsuki. "Interns don't have work. Interns have photocopying and crying in the toilets."

"Then I've got photocopying." Rei hesitated, and then said the real reason, because there was never any point lying to Itsuki, and Haruto would only have guessed. "Gin might come home early."

Haruto stopped with the empty ice cup halfway to the bin.

"In *Obon*?"

"He might."

"You said he never comes home in Obon."

"He might," said Rei.

Itsuki looked at him for a moment, and his face went soft, the way it almost never did when other people were around. Then he reached over and pushed Haruto's hood down over his eyes, to stop him saying anything else.

"Go on, then," he said. "Go home and keep the rice warm for your big brother." He grinned, to take the sting out of it. "I'm going back to the studio to paint. Text me when he gets in. So I know you're not sitting by the door all night like an abandoned dog."

"I don't do that."

"You do exactly that," said Itsuki and Haruto at the same time, from under the hood, and Haruto pushed it back up just to see Itsuki's face, delighted.

[break The flat]

Rei walked up the hill under the lanterns with the drum of the Bon dance following him and getting fainter, and the cicadas still screaming in the trees of the park, even in the dark.

The flat was on the third floor of an old concrete building near the top of the hill above Ōji station. Like most small apartment buildings in Tokyo it had no inside hallway. Every front door opened onto a long concrete walkway that ran along the outside of the building, with a metal railing on one side and the street three floors below. Rei climbed the outside stairs, let himself in, and stepped out of his shoes in the genkan. In a Japanese home the genkan is the little entrance just inside the front door, one step lower than the rest of the floor, where you leave your shoes so that the dirt of the street stays outside. Rei's shoes, as usual, landed on their sides. Gin would straighten them in the morning without a word, as usual.

On the wall of the genkan, by the door, there was a brass hook. It was empty. That was where Gin's keys lived. Gin hung them there the moment he came in, before he took off his coat, before he even said hello. He had read somewhere, he told Rei once, that putting your keys in the same place every day helps people whose minds slip sideways to stay where they are. Rei had laughed at him for that. Then he had started moving the keys a few centimetres on the hook, every few nights, to see if Gin noticed. Gin always noticed. Gin never said. It was, both of them would have admitted under torture, their favourite game.

The brass was worn bright in one small place where Gin's thumb always went.

[img res_apartmentlivingroom.png | The Uedera flat]

The flat was two small rooms and a kitchen. Gin had the room with the desk and the window. Rei had the other room, in theory, but most nights he slept on the living-room sofa with his feet hanging off the end, because he liked to hear the trains going past at the bottom of the hill, and because from the sofa he could see the front door. The kitchen was the heart of the place. A small table with two chairs. A gas stove with two rings. A rice cooker with a crack in its lid that Gin had been saying he would replace for three years. A tin ashtray on the windowsill with Rei's cigarette ends in it, which he always swore he would empty and never did, and which Gin always emptied without comment.

Rei did the things he always did on the nights Gin worked late.

He made ginger pork, because it was Gin's favourite. He sliced the pork very thin and fried it in the pan with soy sauce and mirin and a spoonful of sugar and far too much fresh ginger, much more than any recipe said, because that was how Gin liked it, until the whole kitchen smelled sweet and sharp and the window steamed up. He ate his own half standing at the counter. He put Gin's half on a plate with a mound of rice, turned a second plate upside down over it to keep it warm, and left it on the counter by the stove, exactly the way Gin always left dinner for him.

He took Gin's work shirts off the drying rack on the balcony and ironed two of them, not very well, and hung them on the back of Gin's bedroom door. He filled the kettle. He put Gin's slippers in the genkan with the toes pointing in, toward the house, so that Gin could step straight out of his shoes and into them without turning round.

He looked into Gin's room to hang the shirts. It was the way it always was. The bed made with square corners. The curtains drawn. The desk by the window, clear except for the lamp with the green glass shade, and a pot of pens, and the book.

Gin's book lay closed in the middle of the desk, squared to the edge, the way it lay every day. A thick notebook with a soft black cover gone pale at the corners from years of being carried. Gin wrote in it every night. At the kitchen table, at the desk, once or twice on the sofa with his feet on Rei's legs. He had been writing in it for as long as Rei could remember, page after page in his small exact handwriting, and when it was full he started another, and when Rei came near he closed it, gently, without hurry, and smiled.

Rei had never opened it. Not once, in all those years. He had never even wanted to, very much. It was Gin's. Some things you leave alone because you love the person they belong to, and that was the beginning and end of it.

He hung the shirts on the back of the door, and closed it.

At nine o'clock he sent a message. *Dinner's on the counter. Ginger pork. Don't eat it cold, use the microwave, I mean it.*

No answer. That was normal. In Obon week Gin worked until two or three in the morning, or later. He did not always answer. But he always came home.

That was the thing about Gin. He was late, sometimes. Sometimes he was very late. Sometimes Rei woke up on the sofa at four in the morning and found that the television had been switched off and a blanket had been put over him, and the keys were back on the hook, two centimetres to the right of wherever Rei had moved them, and Gin's door was shut, and the only sign that his brother had come home at all was the blanket, and a plate rinsed and turned upside down in the drying rack. But in nineteen years there had never been a night when Gin did not come home in the end. Not one.

Rei sent Itsuki a message. *Home. He's not back yet.*

The answer came at once. *shocking. go to sleep. he'll wake u up at 3am eating ur pork with his hands like a raccoon.*

And a minute later, from a number Rei had saved under *Haruto DO NOT GIVE MONEY*, a single message with no words in it at all, just a photograph of a second shaved ice, melon, bright green, held up in triumph against the lanterns. The old man at the stall had given in.

Rei smiled at the screen. He lay down on the sofa with the television on and the sound off. A cooking programme. An old man making soba noodles by hand, folding the dough over and over on a wooden board and cutting it with a great square knife into threads as thin as string. Rei watched it without really seeing it. Down at the bottom of the hill, very faint now, he could still hear the drum of the Bon dance, going round and round.

At some point, without meaning to, he closed his eyes.

[break After three]

He woke because the drum had stopped.

The room was dark except for the cold blue light of the television, which had given up on the soba and gone to a screen of coloured bars. His neck ached from lying on the arm of the sofa. He was hot and sticky and his mouth tasted of strawberry syrup.

There was no blanket over him.

He lay still and listened. No drum. No trains. Not even the cicadas. It was the hour at the very bottom of the night when even Tokyo goes quiet, when the last trains have stopped and the first have not started and the whole enormous city seems to be holding its breath. He turned his head and looked at the green numbers on the clock on the stove, because it was the only clock he could see from the sofa.

3:08.

He got up and switched the television off. The room went fully dark. He went to the genkan and switched on the small light.

The brass hook was empty. Gin's shoes were not there. Gin's slippers were exactly where Rei had left them, side by side, toes pointing in.

He went into the kitchen and switched on the light over the stove. The plate was still upside down over the other plate on the counter. He lifted it. The ginger pork had gone cold and grey, the sauce set into a thin brown jelly, the rice dried hard and yellowish at the edges. Nobody had touched it.

He looked at his phone. No messages. Under his own message, *Don't eat it cold, use the microwave, I mean it,* it said *Delivered* in small grey letters.

It did not say *Read*.

And that, more than the empty hook, more than the cold plate, was what turned his stomach over. Because Gin read everything. Even when he was too busy to answer, even in the worst nights of Obon, he read. Rei could watch the little word appear under his messages at midnight, at one, at half past two, and know that somewhere out there in the dark city his brother had pulled his phone out of his coat for one second, and looked, and put it away again. It was such a small thing. It was the kind of small thing you never notice until the day it is not there.

He called. It rang four times, and then Gin's recorded voice came on, calm and very slightly bored, the voice of a man who considered talking to machines beneath him but was too polite to say so. *This is Uedera. Leave a message.*

Rei did not leave a message. He put the phone face down on the kitchen table and sat in his own chair and looked across at Gin's empty one.

He's working, he told himself. It's Obon. Something happened somewhere and he's in the middle of it and his phone is in his coat and his coat is in a car. He'll come in at five with salt on his shoes and dark rings under his eyes and say sorry, sorry, I know, and eat the pork cold out of the pan with his fingers even though I told him not to.

He waited.

3:15. A taxi went past on the main road at the bottom of the hill, and the sound of its engine climbed up through the open window and faded away. 3:22. Somewhere in the building a toilet flushed, and the old pipes knocked inside the wall. 3:30. He got up and filled a glass of water at the sink and stood holding it and did not drink it. 3:36. He called again. Four rings. *This is Uedera. Leave a message.*

He sat in the dark kitchen with the cold plate in front of him and the phone face down on the table, and he was nineteen years old, and he felt about eight.

[break 3:47]

At a quarter to four he could not stay in the kitchen any longer.

He went to the front door and pushed his feet into his shoes without tying them, and opened the door, and stepped out onto the walkway to look down at the street. It was what he used to do when he was small and Gin was late. He would stand at the railing in his pyjamas and watch the corner at the bottom of the hill, where the road came up from the station, and wait for his brother to come round it under the street lamp with his coat over his arm. Gin always looked up. He always saw him. He always lifted one hand.

The night air was warm and heavy and smelled of incense from the doorstep fires. The walkway was lit by one orange lamp at the top of the stairs, and a moth was beating itself against it, over and over, with a small dry tapping sound. Two doors down, the Yamadas' futon was still hanging over the railing to air, the way it always was, because the Yamadas never took it in. Below, the street was empty. The corner at the bottom of the hill was empty. Far down the hill a single white lantern was still burning outside a house, swinging very slightly in no wind at all.

Rei stood at the railing and waited for someone to come round the corner.

Nobody came.

He turned to go back in. And something on the concrete caught the orange light, just outside the door, a little to one side of the doormat, where he had stepped right over it without seeing.

It was a key.

A single brass door key on a plain steel ring, with a small dent in the ring where it had once been caught in a car door, years ago, and Gin had sworn about it for a whole afternoon.

It was Gin's key. The one from the hook.

Rei crouched down and looked at it for a long time without touching it. Then he picked it up.

It was cold.

Not cool, the way metal is cool at night. Cold. Properly, painfully cold, like a can from a vending machine on a summer afternoon, so cold that his fingers ached around it, on an August night when the concrete under his shoes was still warm from the sun. And it was wet, with a thin film of water on it, as if it had been lying in a fridge.

He stood up very slowly and looked left along the walkway, and right, and down at the street, and up at the stairs. There was no one. There was no sound of footsteps on the stairs. There was no car pulling away at the bottom of the hill. There was nothing at all, and later that was the part that stayed with him the longest. How completely nothing else there was.

He looked at his phone. The time was 3:47.

And then, as he stood there with his brother's cold key in his fist, the strangest thing happened. The walkway felt steadier under his feet. The fear that had been rising in his chest since three o'clock stopped rising. It did not go away. It stopped where it was, the way a wave stops at the top of a beach and hangs there for a moment before it slides back. As if the key were a nail, and Rei were a picture hanging on a wall, and as long as he held on to it he would not fall.

He did not notice that. Not then. He was too busy being afraid. It would be months before he understood what that small cold piece of metal had started doing for him on the walkway at 3:47, and by then it would be far too late to put it down.

He went back inside, and closed the door, and locked it, and put the chain on. Then he stood there for a moment and took the chain off again, in case Gin came. He did not hang the key on the brass hook. He kept it in his fist. He went back to the kitchen and sat down in his own chair, across from his brother's empty one.

Then he picked up his phone and wrote to Gin.

*Where are you?*

He watched the screen for a long time. The message said *Delivered*.

It never said *Read*.
