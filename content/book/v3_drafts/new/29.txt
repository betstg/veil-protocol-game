#title The Director

Rei did not own a suit. Okada lent him the money, and he bought one at a big chain store by Akabane station for nineteen thousand eight hundred yen. Dark grey, a little too long in the arms. A white shirt. A black tie he tied four times in front of the mirror in the shop toilet before it looked like anything.

At nine o'clock on the twenty-first of December he walked into the grey Division building in Marunouchi through the front door.

[break Operations]

The ground floor was the Operations floor. Rei had only ever seen the lobby, the long front desk and the smiling young men. Behind the lobby, through a pair of heavy glass doors that opened when somebody behind the desk pressed a button, it was a different place.

It was one enormous room, the size of the whole building, with a low ceiling and strip lights and no windows. Desks in long rows, hundreds of them, with grey partitions between them. Phones ringing everywhere, softly, all the time, like insects. People in plain grey clothes with the small white owl on the chest walking fast between the rows with files under their arms. On one wall, a huge screen showed a map of Tokyo covered in small coloured dots, some of them blinking. On the opposite wall a row of clocks showed the time in Osaka, Sapporo, Fukuoka and Naha. The air smelled of coffee and photocopier toner and, under that, very faintly, of salt.

Everybody who came into the Division came in here first. Visitors. New staff. People who had seen something on a train and did not know who else to tell. A woman at a desk by the door took his name and his form and stamped it with a small red stamp and pointed him at a lift.

"Assessment and Registration," she said. "Third floor. They're expecting you. Good luck."

Okada was waiting for him when the lift doors opened. She looked at the suit. She looked at the tie for one second, and Rei saw her decide not to say anything about the tie.

"I'm not allowed in the room," she said quietly, walking him down the corridor. "I'm Investigation. This isn't mine. But I'll be out here." She stopped by a glass door. "Remember what I said."

"Just tell them what I feel."

"Yes."

[break Assessment]

[need ch29_assessment | A small bright white assessment room in an office building, a table with a row of objects on it, a brass bowl, a child's red shoe, a folded handkerchief, an old wristwatch, a white stone, and a flat grey machine with two hand pads, a nervous young man in a too-big dark suit sitting in front of it]

The room was small and white, with a glass wall onto the corridor, like the rooms in a hospital where they take blood. In it there was a chair and a table and, on the table, a row of objects. A brass bowl. A child's red shoe. A folded handkerchief. An old wristwatch. A small white stone. And a machine Rei had never seen, a flat grey box with two pads on top like the pads for your hands at a passport gate, and a screen on the side covered in slowly moving green lines.

The assessor was a woman of about sixty, with half-moon glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of porridge. She had the slow, kind, bored face of somebody who had measured ten thousand people and would measure ten thousand more.

"Uedera Rei," she said, reading from her tablet. "Twenty. Unregistered. Referred by Investigation." She looked at him over her glasses. "Sit down, dear. It doesn't hurt. Do you need anything before we start? Water? The toilet? People always need the toilet. Nerves."

"The toilet," said Rei. "Please."

"Of course." She pressed a button. A young man in grey came to the door. "Pockets first, I'm afraid. It's the rule. Nothing goes in or out."

Rei emptied his pockets onto a plastic tray. His phone. His wallet. The key on its cord, which he lifted over his head and put down very carefully, and which the young man looked at for a moment and did not touch. The young man opened the wallet and went through it with one finger. A few notes. Okada's card. A photograph of a cat. A small white paper crane, a little yellow with age.

"Origami," said the young man, and smiled, and put it back. "My grandmother does those."

He walked Rei to the toilet at the end of the corridor and waited outside.

In the cubicle, with the door locked, Rei took the crane out of the wallet he had been allowed to take with him and held it in his fingers. It weighed nothing. *Don't rush,* Shizue had said. He thought of Gin, two years ago, sitting on the floor of a room full of paper cranes, frightened, and not saying of what.

He tore it in half.

Nothing happened. There was no sound and no light. Only, very faintly, as if somebody had turned down a radio in the next room, the pressure behind his eyes went a little quieter. He put the two halves of the crane in his pocket and flushed the toilet, for the young man outside, and went back.

He put his hands on the pads.

The green lines on the screen moved. They rose, steadily, and settled, and held. The assessor looked at them and nodded and wrote something.

"Now the objects," she said. "One at a time. Tell me what you feel. Don't try. Just tell me."

He did it as Sōun had taught him. A finger's width. And underneath, the old door he had been closing since he was a child, the one Gin had taught him at the kitchen table without ever calling it anything, closed itself as it always did in a room full of strangers.

The brass bowl was from a temple. Calm. Old. Nothing in it. The child's shoe had a small sad weight on it, faint, very old, like a dried flower in a book. The handkerchief had been held by someone crying, once, at a wedding, and they had been happy. The wristwatch was heavy and cold and he did not like it, and he put it down quickly. The white stone was nothing. Just a stone.

He said exactly that. No more.

The assessor wrote it all down. She looked at the green lines on the screen. She looked at him over her half-moon glasses for a long moment, the way a woman looks at a cake that has come out of the oven exactly as the recipe said, a little flat.

"Postulate," she said. "Clairsentience. Clean signature, no noise, nothing strange." She tapped her tablet. "Registered. Welcome to the list, dear. Lots of very useful people are Postulates." She smiled. "Your brother was a Ritualist, it says here. Never mind. It's not a race."

When he came out into the corridor, Okada was leaning on the wall with two paper cups of tea. She gave him one. She did not ask what the number was. She looked at his face for one second, and then at the glass wall, where the assessor was typing.

"Postulate," Rei said.

Okada drank her tea. Something went across her face very fast and was gone.

"Then you're a Postulate," she said. "Drink your tea."

[break Internal Affairs]

The second interview was on the sixth floor, in Internal Affairs and Audit.

It was a long quiet corridor with thick carpet that swallowed footsteps and doors made of dark wood instead of grey metal. Halfway along, between two numbered doors, there was a door with no number at all. Plain dark wood, with a brass lock of a shape Rei had never seen, like a flower with too many petals. He looked at it as he passed. The key under his shirt did not go cold. It did not do anything. That was what made him remember it.

The interview room had a window over Marunouchi and a desk with nothing on it but a closed file and a glass of water. Behind the desk sat a young man of about twenty-four in a perfect dark suit, with a face like a closed file.

"Uedera-san," said Fujiwara-Kage Sena. "We meet again. You pour tea very well."

Rei sat down.

"Internal Affairs interviews everybody who joins," said Sena. "It's a formality. I like formalities. They tell you a great deal about people." He opened the file. It was very thin. "Postulate. Clairsentience. No noise. Very clean." He looked up. "Your brother's reading was never clean. There was always something in it the machine didn't like. He said it was the coffee."

Rei said nothing. He kept his hands on his knees, as he had at Tanizaki's.

"You walked in," said Sena. "On your own. Through the front door. In a suit." He closed the file. "The council asked for you to be watched, not approached. And then you approached us." A small, correct smile. "That was very helpful of you. The council will be pleased."

"I want to find my brother."

"Of course you do." Sena stood, which meant the interview was over. "And now you'll be looking for him from inside a building where I can see you. Everybody wins." He held the door open. "Welcome to the Division, Uedera-san. Don't do anything I'll have to write down."

[break The Director]

The third interview was not on the schedule.

At four o'clock, when he had filled in nineteen forms and had a photograph taken for a lanyard and been told where the canteen was, a young woman with a bead bracelet came to find him in the corridor.

"Uedera Rei?" She was about twenty, small and quick, with a calm face and a bracelet of ceramic beads on her left wrist that she turned round and round with her right hand while she talked. "I'm Nakamura. Field Operations. The Director would like to see you." She smiled. It was a real smile, quick and warm. "I'm not supposed to say this, but I was really looking forward to meeting you. Your brother talked about you."

"Everybody says that."

"Because he did. A lot." She led him to the lift. "He was the only senior officer who ever asked how I was and waited for the answer." She pressed four. "Well. Him and the Director."

Rei remembered a page in Gin's book. *Nakamura Aoi. Asks how you are and waits for the answer.* He wondered if she knew that Gin had written that. He decided not to tell her yet.

The Director's office was cold, as it had been in August. The same bare desk, the same telephone and pen, the same old train pass lying face down. The same grey boxes on the shelves. Adachi stood at the window with his back to the room, and turned round when they came in, and looked at Rei with his pale resting eyes.

"Thank you, Nakamura," he said.

Aoi bowed and went out. The door closed.

"Sit down, Uedera."

Rei sat. Adachi sat. On the desk in front of him there was a single sheet of paper, Rei's registration, with the red stamp on it.

"How are you?" said the Director.

"Tired," said Rei. "My ribs still hurt. My best friend is in a sealed room in your basement with something living in him. I've been measured, and I don't know if I'm pleased or not." He thought about it. "I had rice for breakfast."

Adachi nodded slowly.

"Postulate," he said, looking at the sheet of paper. He looked at it for a long time. Longer than there was anything on it to read. "Your brother was registered as a Ritualist."

"The lady said it's not a race."

"It isn't." Adachi turned the train pass over on the desk with one finger, face up, and then face down again. "It is a number. Numbers are useful. They are also only numbers." He did not say anything else about it. Rei had the strange feeling that he had decided not to.

"I am going to give you to Field Operations," said Adachi. "You'll start on the Operations floor like everybody, and you'll go out with a partner, like everybody. There is one field officer in this building without a partner. His last partner disappeared in August, and nobody else will work with him." He looked at Rei. "Do you know why?"

"Because he's a telepath," said Rei. "And people think he's always listening."

"Yes."

"He is always listening."

"Yes," said Adachi. For the first time, something softened very slightly round his mouth. "He works in the Arsenal when he isn't in the field. You'll find him in the sub-level. He'll pretend he wasn't told you were coming."

Rei stood up. At the door he stopped.

"Director," he said. "In August you said there were parts of this building that don't answer to you."

"Yes."

"Are they the ones who decided my brother is on assignment?"

Adachi did not answer at once. He looked at the face-down train pass on his desk.

"I don't run this Division alone, Uedera," he said at last. "There are people with a say in it whom I have never met. I have asked to. I will keep asking." He looked up. "Go downstairs. Do your work. Don't lie to me. That is all I can ask you for today."

[break The sub-level]

The Arsenal was two floors under the street, at the end of a corridor that smelled of oil and salt. A steel counter ran across the end of a long low room, and behind it, on racks and shelves going back into the dark, were things Rei had only read about. Knives in leather sheaths with writing etched up the blades. Coils of chain with a silver sheen. Sacks of coarse grey salt. Rows of glass jars of iron filings, labelled by hand. Bottles of water with paper seals round the necks. No guns anywhere. Not one.

Behind the counter, in a dark suit with the jacket off and the sleeves rolled to the elbow, Kato Ryusei was cleaning a short blade with a cloth, very carefully, and did not look up.

"You're late," he said. "I was told you'd be down at four."

"You were told?"

"No." Kato put the blade down and lined it up with the edge of the counter. A strand of hair had come loose over his forehead. "I wasn't told anything. I'm pretending." He looked up at Rei, at the suit, at the tie, for a long moment. "That tie is the worst thing that has ever come into this room. And I've had a man bring in a sword with a dead hand still holding it."

Rei laughed. It came out of him all at once, the first time all day.

Kato did not laugh. But he took the ivory knight out of his pocket and set it on the counter between them, standing up, the way he had in the family restaurant, and left it there.

"Partners," he said. "Apparently."

"Apparently."

"Don't think nice things at me," said Kato. "I'll go and stand in the salt store until you stop."
