#title What Remains

The evidence archive was in the second basement, one floor below Reintegration, and trainees were sent there as a punishment.

Not officially. Officially it was *inventory*. Every year, someone had to go down and check every box and every bag and every object on every shelf against the list, and every year, the job went to the most junior people in the building. In the second week of January it went to Rei and Kato.

"It's because I made a joke in the canteen," said Kato, carrying a box of files down the stairs. "About the ninth floor. Somebody laughed. Somebody else wrote it down."

"What was the joke?"

"I'm not telling you. It wasn't that good." Kato pushed the heavy door open with his shoulder. "It was quite good."

[need ch34_evidence_archive | A long low basement evidence archive under fluorescent strip lights, endless grey metal shelves of labelled boxes and plastic bags, two young trainees in suits with clipboards, one holding a small old wristwatch up to the light]

The archive was enormous. It was one long low room, stretching away under the building, with grey metal shelves from floor to ceiling in rows so long that you could not see the end of them. Fluorescent lights buzzed overhead. It was cold, and it smelled of dust and cardboard and something faintly sour, like old pennies. Every shelf was full of boxes, and every box was full of things. Clothes in plastic bags. Toys. Kitchen knives. Photographs. Letters. A wedding dress in a long white bag. A child's bicycle. Every object that had ever been part of a case in Kita, for forty years, labelled and numbered and put away.

Rei's wrist was cold from the moment he walked in. It stayed cold all day.

"Don't read anything," said Kato. "Seriously. Shimizu said. Half of this stuff still has someone in it. Just check the numbers."

"I'm not reading anything."

"You're thinking about reading the wedding dress. I can hear you."

They checked numbers. For two days, they checked numbers. They found a box of letters that should have been in row twelve in row forty. They found a teddy bear that was on the list but not on the shelf, and spent an hour looking for it, and found it on the wrong shelf, sitting on top of a box marked KITCHEN KNIVES, looking at them.

"I didn't put it there," said Kato.

"Nobody put it there," said Rei.

They put it back, and did not talk about it.

[break 3:14]

On the third day, in row fifty-one, Rei found the watch.

It was in a small clear plastic bag on a shelf by itself, with a white label. *Case 04-1187. Satō. Wristwatch, gentleman's. Do not remove from archive.* Under the label, in pen, in handwriting Rei knew at once, someone had added a note. *Removed twice. Returned both times. Leaving it here. Monitoring. G.U.*

It was a plain old watch with a steel case and a brown leather strap, worn soft and dark at the buckle. The glass was scratched. The hands had stopped. They said fourteen minutes past three.

Rei had read about it. Months ago, in Gin's book at the kitchen table, in the chapter on objects. *There is a watch in the Kita evidence archive from the 2004 Satō case. It stopped at 3:14am on the night of the case and has not run since. I have replaced the battery three times. It keeps returning to the evidence archive. I have removed it twice. It is there when I come back.*

"Don't," said Kato, behind him.

"I'm only looking."

"You're thinking about touching it. You're thinking really loudly about touching it."

"Gin replaced the battery three times," said Rei. "He took it out of here twice. He wrote about it in his book. He was trying to work out what it was." He looked at the watch. "He was about to write a whole chapter on it. The Satō case. He said so. He said he'd write it, and then he'd look at the family files. And then he disappeared."

Kato was quiet.

"I'm going to read it," said Rei. "A finger's width. That's all. You can pull me out."

"How?"

"Hit me."

"I'm not going to hit you."

"Then shout at me."

"I can do that." Kato put down his clipboard. "I'm a very good shouter. Okay. A finger's width. If you start talking in a different voice, I'm shouting."

Rei took the watch out of the bag.

[break The hospital]

It was very cold. Not like the key. The key was cold the way a stone in a river is cold, clean and steady. This was cold like the inside of a hospital at night. Cold like linoleum. Cold like a hand that has been holding another hand for a long time.

He opened the door in his chest a finger's width, and the latch held it there.

A hospital room. Night. 2004. A narrow bed with metal rails. A machine beeping, slow and slower. In the bed, a man of about sixty, thin and grey and very ill, with a tube in his nose and a watch on his wrist, this watch, too big for his thin wrist now, sliding round. His wife was asleep in a chair beside the bed with her head on the blanket. The man was awake. He was looking at the ceiling. He was dying, and he knew he was dying, and he did not want to.

He did not want to so much that Rei felt it like a hand round his own throat.

*Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.* The man was saying it inside himself, over and over, with everything he had left. *Not yet. I'm not finished. She's not ready. I can't. I won't. Not yet.*

And something was passing.

Rei felt it go by. A soul. Someone else, somewhere else in the hospital, in another room, on another floor, someone who had just died, peacefully, and was crossing. Rising. Going on toward the light. Passing by the window of this room the way you pass a lit window on a dark street.

And the man in the bed, with his *not yet, not yet, not yet*, pulled.

He did not know he was doing it. He did not mean to. His wanting was so strong, so desperate, that it made a kind of hole in the air of the room. A drain. And the passing soul, going by, was caught in it. Pulled sideways. Pulled in. Into the room. Into the bed. Into the man.

The watch on the thin wrist stopped. Three fourteen.

The machine beside the bed beeped faster. The man's eyes opened wider. His wife woke up and lifted her head and saw him looking at her and began to cry, because the doctor had said he would not see morning, and here he was, looking at her, his colour coming back.

But Rei, with his finger's width of door, could see what she could not. There were two people in that bed now. The man. And someone else. A young woman, by the feel of her. Confused. Terrified. Trapped. Pressed into a body that was not hers, beside a mind that was not hers, with no idea what had happened to her. Screaming without a mouth.

The man lived for eleven more years.

The young woman lived with him.

[break Absorbed]

"*REI!*"

Kato's voice hit him like a slap. Rei was on his knees on the cold concrete floor of the archive with the watch in his fist, and his face was wet, and his voice was saying something in a high, thin, frightened voice that was not his. *Where am I where am I who is this who is this whose hands are these.*

He let go of the watch. It fell on the floor. He stopped talking.

Kato was crouched in front of him holding his shoulders, white as paper.

"You did the voice," said Kato. "You did the voice. I said I'd shout if you did the voice."

"You shouted."

"I shouted really well."

Rei sat back on his heels against the shelves and breathed. In for four. Hold. Out for six. The latch on his wrist was burning cold. Slowly, slowly, it eased.

"It's called absorption," he said, when he could talk. "Gin wrote about it. A dying person wants to live so much that they pull another soul into them. One that's passing. They don't mean to. The other one doesn't choose. And then there are two people in one body." He looked at the watch on the floor. "Mr. Satō lived for eleven years after that night. With a young woman inside him who never knew who she was."

Kato looked at the watch too. He did not touch it.

"What happened to her?"

"I don't know." Rei picked up the watch, carefully, by the strap, and put it back in its plastic bag. "That's why it keeps coming back here. That's what it wants. Somebody to find out what happened to her."

[break Fujimoto]

That evening Okada found them in the canteen. She sat down at their table with her tea and her three notebooks and looked at Rei's face.

"You read the Satō watch," she said.

"How did you know?"

"Because you look like your brother did the day he read it." She opened the middle notebook. "There's something I need to show you. I've been watching an anomaly in the district numbers for four years. Every two months, in the assessments, the readings across Kita jump. Just for a day. Just a little. Nobody else sees it as a pattern." She turned the notebook round. Columns of numbers, very small and neat. "It started in 2011. And the centre of it, the place the jump comes from, every time, is a flat in Ōtsuka."

"Whose flat?"

"A man called Fujimoto Kenji." She closed the notebook. "He's in the Vessel Program. He's been their model case for seven years. Voluntary cohabitation. A man and a spirit sharing a body by contract. The Program shows him to visitors. They call him a success." She looked at Rei. "Your brother went to see him in July. He came back and didn't say anything about it for three days. Then he asked me, *Shizuka, how do you tell the difference between a man who has a guest in him and a guest who has a man in him?*" She drank her tea. "I didn't have an answer."

[need ch34_fujimoto | A cluttered scholar's flat in Ōtsuka, papers in tall stacks in a logic only one person understands, a cold room with the window open in winter, a medium-built man in his thirties sitting very still at a desk with a cup of tea, smiling politely at visitors]

They went to Ōtsuka the next day, Rei and Kato and Okada, on a Division visit. *Routine wellbeing check.*

The flat was full of paper. Stacks of it, on the floor, on the chairs, on the table, in towers that leaned against each other, in an order nobody but the owner could see. It was very cold. The window was open, in January, and the room was colder than the street. A man sat at a desk by the window. Medium build. Unhurried. In his thirties. He stood up when they came in, and bowed, and smiled a polite, pleasant, slightly absent smile.

"Officer Okada," he said. "How nice. And trainees. Please sit, if you can find a chair. I'm afraid I'm not very tidy."

"Mr. Fujimoto," said Okada.

"Yes," said the man. And then, after a very small pause, as if checking, "Yes. That's right."

Rei felt Kato go still beside him.

They sat on the edges of chairs among the paper. The man made tea, a particular green tea from a particular tin, with great care. He talked about his work. He catalogued old letters for a university. He loved it. He talked about the Program, which was very kind to him. He talked about the cold, which he preferred. He was polite and clever and easy to talk to.

And all the time, Rei felt his wrist go colder and colder. And all the time, Kato sat beside him without saying a word, with his king piece clenched in his hand so hard that his knuckles were white.

"Mr. Fujimoto," said Rei at last. "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Is Kenji there?"

The man stopped with his tea cup halfway to his mouth.

He did not drop it. He did not change. He put it down, gently, on the desk. He looked at Rei. The polite, pleasant, slightly absent smile stayed exactly where it was.

"Kenji is in the filing," he said.

The room was very quiet.

"He loved the filing," said the man, softly. "He made it. All of this. The order of it. He was so organised. So curious. So lonely. His parents died when he was twenty-five, and he had no one, and he was afraid of being alone in a room. So he signed the contract. Seven years ago. *Cohabitation*. *Partnership*. *You will never be alone again.*" The smile did not move. "I promised him that. In the contract. In writing. *You will never be alone again.* And I have kept my word. I have kept every word." He touched a tower of paper beside him, gently, the way you touch the hair of someone asleep. "We are not two any more. We are one. He is never alone. Not for one second. Not ever."

Rei could not speak.

"Abe," said Okada quietly. "Your name is Abe Takeshi."

"Yes," said the man. "And also Kenji. Both. Neither." He picked up his tea again. "I am a spirit, Officer. Not a demon. I want you to know that. I died eight years ago, in a fall, and when I crossed I saw the light, and I did not want it. I chose this instead. A body, and a mind to share it with, by contract. I am honest about contracts." He drank. "I simply did not understand, when I signed, what *never alone* would mean. Neither did he."

He smiled at them over the cup. It was a perfectly kind smile.

On the stairs going down, Kato stopped halfway and sat on a step and put his head in his hands.

"There's only one," he said, through his fingers. "In there. Only one layer. One voice. I listened the whole time. For Kenji. For anything. Underneath." He lifted his head. His eyes were wet. "There's nothing underneath, Rei. He kept his promise. There's nobody left to be alone."
