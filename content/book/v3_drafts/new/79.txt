#title Together

*Gin.*

The policewoman at the cordon went white when she saw his face.

Gin watched her do it through his own eyes. A young woman in a summer uniform with her cap pushed back, standing by a sign that said GAS LEAK, who looked up as they came down the road from Tabata station in the last of the orange light, and saw him, and went the colour of paper. She knew him. Not well. From the front desk at Kita police station, a year ago, when he had come in twice about a case and joked with her about the vending machine.

She knew he was missing. Everybody at Kita station knew he was missing. His brother had made very sure of that.

*She is frightened,* said the voice.

*She's not frightened,* said Gin. *She's working out whether she's seeing a ghost.*

*Ah.* A pause. *Is she?*

*Ask her.*

The voice did not ask her. It stopped the body in front of the tape, very politely, and put one of Gin's hands on the shoulder of the sixteen-year-old girl in a white shirt who had walked beside them from the station without saying a word, and turned her, very gently, toward the policewoman.

"Ono-san," it said, in Gin's voice. "This is Yua. She has been somewhere unpleasant for thirty-six days. She needs a hamburger, and a phone charger, and somebody who will not ask her anything until tomorrow. Will you look after her?"

Ono stared at him.

Then she looked at Yua. At the white shirt. At the face.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice shook. "Yeah. Course I will. Come here, love."

Yua did not go to her. She turned round and looked up at Gin's face. Not at the voice. At him. Gin felt her do it. She looked past the stillness and the borrowed politeness straight into the back of his eyes, where he was, the way his brother looked at things.

"You owe me forty hamburgers," she said. "Your brother does. Same thing."

*She is talking to you,* said the voice, surprised.

*I know,* said Gin. *Let me answer.*

The voice let him.

It was not like the minute on the walkway. It was not stepping back. It was moving over. The way you move over on a bench to let somebody sit. Gin felt his own mouth come under his own control, and the voice still there, right beside him, not holding him down, not holding on, just there, the way somebody sits next to you on a train.

"Forty," said Gin. His own voice. Rough. Dry. "Fine. Forty. I'll pay you in instalments. Go with Ono."

Yua looked at him for a long moment. Her face crumpled, just for a second. Then she pulled her sleeves down over her hands and turned and went to the policewoman, and did not look back.

Ono lifted the tape.

[break The road]

They walked down the road to the river together.

That was the only word Gin had for it. Together. Not one of them wearing the other. Not one of them locked in a room behind the eyes while the other drove. The voice had moved over on the bench, and Gin had sat down, and they walked down the road in one body with two people in it, the way two people walk down a road side by side when they are going to the same place and are not sure they will both arrive.

It was strange. It was the strangest thing that had ever happened to him, and he had been worn like a coat for a year.

He could feel what the voice felt. The voice could feel what he felt. When the streetlights went out one by one along the road, he felt the voice notice it, and file it, and set it aside the way a careful clerk sets aside a receipt. When they passed an old man's empty bicycle lying in the gutter with its wheel still turning, the voice felt Gin feel sad about it, and was puzzled, and then, slowly, very slowly, was a little sad as well.

*Is it always like this,* said the voice. *Being a person. Feeling things about bicycles.*

*Mostly,* said Gin. *You get used to it.*

*I do not think I will.*

*No,* said Gin. *Probably not. Rei never did.*

They walked on.

*I have something to tell you,* said the voice, after a while. *Before we get there.*

*Go on.*

*When she says sit, I may not be able to refuse.* It said it very plainly. *I want you to know that before we go through the gate. I have been learning. You have been teaching me. The old man taught me. The girl taught me, just now, with a packet of tissues. But it is a very old debt, and it is the only thing I have ever been, and when she says the word I do not know what will happen.* A pause. *If I sit, you will be in the chair with me. I will not be able to let you go. I want you to know that.*

Gin walked for a while without answering.

*I know,* he said. *I've known for a year.*

*And you came anyway.*

*You didn't give me much choice.*

*No.* The voice was quiet. *I am sorry. I have said that before. I find I mean it more every time.*

*Rei's going to say your name,* said Gin. *When she says sit. I told him to. On the walkway. He's going to say it louder than her.*

*I know. I heard you.*

*Then listen to him.*

The voice did not answer for a long time.

*I will try,* it said at last. *That is all I can promise. I have never in my existence listened to anybody but her when she used my name.*

*Then this'll be a first,* said Gin. *You're having a lot of those tonight.*

And the voice laughed.

It did not use his mouth. It laughed inside, low and dry and surprised, and Gin felt it go through his own chest like warm water, and he laughed too, out loud, alone on a dark road by the river, with his jacket over his arm, and did not care who heard.

[break The gate]

The old dog was lying across the gateway.

She lifted her head as they came. She looked at them for a long time with her old flat considering eyes. Then she put her head back down on her paws, and did not growl, and did not move, and they stepped over her.

Gin saw the yard.

He saw the thousands of hollow ones, walking toward the river with their flowers held out like candles. Fewer than there should be. Some of them were standing still in the weeds, with their heads lifted, as if listening to a bell somewhere far off. He saw the great white door painted on the side of a rusted freight car, and a boy with paint to his elbows holding it open with both hands and another boy with a silver ring holding him up from behind, and through the door, pouring, the lights. Home. Thousands of them. He saw a broad man in dark clothes sitting in the weeds with a girl in a pink hoodie asleep against his chest. He saw a big man in a black jacket standing in the middle of the yard with the air round him gone completely still.

He saw his team. Shimizu with her hand on the red dog's collar. Kato with his king. Aoi with her seals. Moriya with his notebook. A young man in a suit he did not know. Okada.

Okada.

*She has been watching for him,* said the voice. *For five years. You asked her to.*

*I know.*

*She kept it.*

*I know.* Gin's throat was very tight. *She always keeps everything.*

He saw the grey beach at the bottom of the yard where the old lines ran into the river. He saw the low white table, and the cushions, and the woman standing beside it in a soft white dress, with her hands closed into fists, looking at him.

And he saw Rei.

Rei was standing in the weeds halfway between the gate and the grey beach. He had just arrived. Gin could tell. He was swaying a little, the way you sway when you have come off a train too fast. His nose had bled and dried and bled again. There was blood at his ears. His clean shirt was filthy. Gin's key hung on its cord outside it, on his chest, catching the white light from the river.

And where his door should have been, there was nothing.

Gin saw it. He had always been able to see it. He had made it. At a kitchen table, when Rei was eight, with his hand on his brother's back, saying *like a door, like this, close it, close it, good*. He had made it so his brother could sleep. So the things at the window would stay outside. So Rei would never have to stand in the middle of everything with nothing between him and all of it.

It was gone. Off its hinges. Laid down in the weeds.

And Rei was standing in the middle of all of it, with everything coming in, holding it.

*He took it off,* said Gin. His voice inside was very small. *He took it right off.*

*Yes,* said the voice. *He did that two hours ago. Before you could stop him.*

*I spent eleven years teaching him to keep it shut.*

*Yes.* A pause. *And he kept it shut for eleven years. And then, tonight, when it mattered, he knew exactly where the hinges were.* The voice was quiet. *Somebody taught him that, too.*

[break Brothers]

They walked across the yard.

The hollow ones did not stop for them. They walked past, toward the river, with their flowers and their dark eyes. But they did not walk into them. They parted round Gin's body the way water parts round a stone in a stream, without looking, without knowing why.

Rei turned his head.

He saw them coming. Gin watched his brother's face change. Watched it go from the flat, swaying, bleeding exhaustion of a boy who has been everywhere at once to something else. Something Gin had seen on that face exactly once before, a long time ago, through a car window, at the bottom of the hill, on a night when Gin had been very late and had come round the corner under the street lamp and looked up, and there had been a small boy at the railing in pyjamas, waiting.

They stopped in front of him.

Close. Closer than on the walkway. Close enough that Gin could smell the smoke on him, and the blood, and the iron filings, and under all of it, faint, the soap they had always bought, the cheap one, because Rei liked the smell.

Neither of them said anything.

The voice did not move his hand. Gin did not move it either. They moved it together. Gin felt them do it. Two people lifting one hand, slowly, carefully, as if it were very heavy and very valuable, and putting it on the top of Rei's head.

Rei closed his eyes.

"Hey," said Gin. His own voice. With the voice right beside it, not speaking, just there. "You took your door off."

His eyes stayed shut. "Yeah. Sorry."

"Don't be." Gin's hand moved, once, on his brother's hair. Not ruffling. Holding. Checking he was there. "I'm going to kill you later. But don't be."

Rei laughed. It came out wet and broken.

Then he opened his eyes and looked past Gin, at the grey beach, at the woman by the low white table. And Gin felt the voice look too. And felt the woman look back. At both of them. At the two people in one body standing with a hand on a boy's head in the middle of a freight yard full of the walking dead.

She smiled.

Not the kind smile. A different one. One Gin had never seen on that borrowed face. Old. Very old. Older than the river. The smile of a creditor who has waited a very long time and sees, at last, the debtor walking up the path.

*Ah,* she said, in the voice from the bottom of a lake. Not out loud. In the air. In the weeds. In the space between Gin and the thing beside him. *Ah. There you are. Both of you.*

The voice went very still.

And then, for the first time in a year, Gin felt it reach for his hand. Not to move it. Not to take it. To hold it. The way a child reaches for somebody's hand at a crossing, without looking, because the road is very wide and the traffic is very fast.

Gin held on.
