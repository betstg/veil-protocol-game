#title Itsuki at the Grid

The November Gathering was in an old bowling alley in Itabashi that had closed in 2008.

It still had its lanes. Twenty-four of them, long and pale and dusty under the camping lanterns, with the pins still standing at the far ends in neat white triangles, as if the last game had been called off in the middle. The scoring screens hung dead above each lane. There was a counter where you used to rent shoes, with the shoes still on the shelves behind it, rows and rows of red and blue bowling shoes, cracked and stiff, waiting for feet that were never coming. People sat on the plastic seats by the lanes and on the floor and on the counter. The air smelled of dust and old carpet and the tea in Switch's flask.

[need ch23_bowling | A closed old bowling alley lit by camping lanterns, dusty lanes with pins still standing, rows of old red and blue bowling shoes on shelves behind a counter, a crowd of ordinary people of all ages sitting on plastic seats, two young men standing near the entrance]

Itsuki stood in the doorway and looked at it all for a long time.

"This is it?" he said. "This is the dangerous criminal underground?"

"Last month it was a swimming pool."

"I thought there'd be more leather." Itsuki looked at an old woman knitting by lane seven. "That lady looks like my piano teacher."

"Maybe she is your piano teacher."

"My piano teacher was terrifying. If that's her I'm leaving." But he did not leave. He walked in slowly, with his hands in the pockets of his huge grey hoodie, looking at everything. Rei watched him look. He had seen Itsuki look at a painting like that. Carefully. All the way through. Trying to see what was really there.

Hara Setsu came over from the shoe counter with two paper cups of tea, very upright, in her indigo apron under a grey wool coat. She looked at Itsuki for one second, all the way through, and then at Rei.

"Good," she said, and gave them each a cup. That was all she said. Rei thought it was exactly the right amount.

[break The meeting]

The meeting was the same as before. Switch on the counter now instead of a diving board, hands in pockets. *Nobody records. Nobody photographs.* Business. A man needed help with a house in Saitama. A woman had a list of three names of people who had gone to the Far Shore tea house and not come back, and wanted to know if anyone knew more. Nobody did. Rei held his cup very tightly while she talked, and Itsuki, beside him, felt it and moved a little closer, so that their shoulders touched.

Then Switch said, "Seventeen."

The room went quiet.

"Somebody brought me a list last month," said Switch. "Seventeen thin spots in Kita and Itabashi that the Division saw and decided to leave alone. We've closed nine of them." A small sound went round the room, a murmur. "Four were nothing. Three were bad. Two were very bad. One of them was the empty flats in Higashi-Jūjō." Switch looked straight at Rei across the room. "Somebody went in on their own and nearly didn't come out. And somebody else got them out from the street, with a pencil." A pause. "I'd like to know how."

Several heads turned to Itsuki. He looked at the floor.

[break Sei]

"I can tell you how," said a quiet voice from the dark at the end of lane twenty-four.

Everyone turned.

A young man was sitting on the floor at the end of the far lane, with his back against the wall below the dead scoring screen. Rei had not seen him. Nobody seemed to have seen him. He was slight and narrow, about twenty-two, with black hair grown long enough to tie back and tied with a rubber band. His clothes were dark and plain and clearly very expensive. On his right hand he wore an old silver ring with a seal on it. He was sitting with his knees up and his arms resting on them, and he was looking down the length of the dusty lane at Itsuki.

He had Itsuki's face. Older. Thinner. Harder. But the same narrow shape, the same quick tired eyes.

"He's a Kuroda," said the young man. "That's how. The Kurodas have hands that know things." He did not get up. "He's my brother."

Itsuki had gone so white that Rei put a hand on his arm, in case he fell.

"Sei," said Itsuki.

The murmur went round the bowling alley. Different now. Colder. *Kuroda.* Rei felt Itsuki's shoulder go stiff against his. People who had been sitting relaxed were sitting up. The man in the expensive suit, the one whose sister was in the Vessel Program, had stood. The old woman by lane seven had stopped knitting.

"A Kuroda," said a hard voice at the back. A man of about forty, thick-armed, in a work jacket, coming forward between the lanes. "At the Gathering. Who brought a Kuroda to the Gathering?"

"I did," said Rei.

"And I let him," said Hara Setsu, from the shoe counter, very calmly.

"The Tower Families sit in their houses and their gardens and take money from the Division and look down at us," said the man in the work jacket. "And when the Division wants someone found, who do they ask? They ask the families. Who do you think tells them where we live?"

"I don't tell anybody anything," said Itsuki. His voice came out thin. "I don't even talk to my family."

"That's what they all say."

"It's true," said Sei, from the end of the lane. "He's very good at not talking to the family. He's been doing it since he was ten. I should know. I taught him." He looked at the man in the work jacket. "Leave him alone, Hamada. He's not a spy. He's a painter. He doesn't care about any of us. He doesn't even care about the family. Isn't that right, Itsuki?"

It was a test. Rei could feel it in the room. The whole Grid was watching.

Itsuki stood very still. Then he took his hands out of his pockets.

"I don't love my family," he said. His voice was shaking, but it was loud enough for everyone to hear. "My father talks to me like I'm a bad investment. My brother sells our house's secrets for a couch to sleep on and hasn't called me in four years." He did not look at Sei when he said it. "I didn't come here for them. I came here for him." He tilted his head at Rei. "His brother's missing. The Division isn't looking. You're the only people who are. So I'm here."

The room was quiet.

"Then promise," said Switch, from the counter.

Itsuki turned.

"Promise," said Switch, "that nothing you see here goes back to Nezu. Not a name. Not a face. Not where we met. Not that we exist. Not to your father. Not to anyone with your name." Their square face showed nothing. "You break that, and you're done. And so is he." A small nod at Rei. "And Setsu will be very disappointed in all of us."

Itsuki looked at Rei. Rei could not tell him what to do. He did not try.

"I promise," said Itsuki.

"Say it to him," said Switch, and nodded down the lane at Sei.

Itsuki turned and looked at his brother. For a long moment neither of them said anything.

"I promise," said Itsuki. "I won't tell them anything. I won't be like you."

Something moved across Sei's face. Very fast. Rei saw it only because he had spent ten weeks on soft old boards learning to see things. It looked like something being hit.

Then it was gone, and Sei almost smiled.

"Good," he said. "Don't be."

He got up off the floor, in one smooth movement, the way his father did, without using his hands. He walked to the side door of the bowling alley, behind the dead lanes. At the door he stopped, with his back to the room.

"Itsuki," he said. "That sketchbook under your arm. The blue one, with the corner gone soft."

Itsuki's arm tightened round it.

"I bought you that," said Sei. "At the stationery shop by Nezu station, when you were nine, with my New Year money. You wanted the red one. They didn't have the red one. You cried." He opened the door. "You still carry it. Good."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the door swung shut behind him with a soft click.

[break After]

At the door of the bowling alley, Hara Setsu put a hand on Itsuki's arm as they passed. Lightly, for one second.

"You said a cruel thing to him," she said. "And it hurt you to say it. That's how you know you're not like him." She took her hand away. "Go home. Eat something."

Afterward, in the street, Itsuki walked very fast with his hands in his pockets and his hood up and did not say anything for ten minutes. Rei walked beside him and did not say anything either.

At the station, Itsuki stopped under the lights.

"He remembered," he said. "The sketchbook. I'd forgotten he bought it. I've carried it every day for eleven years and I forgot."

"Yeah."

"Four years. Four years he didn't call. And he remembered the stupid sketchbook."

"Yeah."

Itsuki turned and looked at Rei. His face was wet under the hood. He was not trying to hide it.

"I said *I won't be like you,*" he said. "In front of everyone. I meant it to hurt him."

"I know."

"It did. I saw it." He wiped his face hard with his sleeve. "I've wanted to hurt him for four years and I finally did it and it feels horrible."

Itsuki stood under the station lights and looked at Rei.

"Can I sleep on your sofa?" he said. "I can't be in my flat tonight. It's too quiet."

"You'll have to fight me for it. It's my sofa."

"I'll sleep on the floor. I don't care." Itsuki pulled his hood down. His eyes were red and his nose was red and he looked about fourteen. "I'm not in the Grid, Rei. And I'm not in the family. I don't know what I'm in."

"You're in the trio," said Rei. "Me, you, and Kato."

Itsuki stared at him. Then he laughed, wetly, and shoved him, gently, on the shoulder. The opposite of the shove in the studio.

"Kato doesn't even know he's in it," he said. "That's the worst trio in the history of Tokyo."

"I know."

"Okay," said Itsuki. "Okay. I'm in."

Rei took out his phone, there under the station lights, and sent Kato a cat. The answer came in six seconds. *It's midnight. What happened. Who's dead.*

*nobody,* Rei typed. *you're in a trio now. congratulations.*

A long pause. Then. *Noted.*

A minute later, another. *Who's the third.*
