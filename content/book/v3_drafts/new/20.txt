#title Two Sides

Without Itsuki the days had a hole in them. Rei filled it with work.

He went to the Kuroda training hall every morning at six and knelt on the soft old boards and was told he was doing it wrong. He went to Tanizaki on Saturdays and sat for an hour, and then to Tsuru-no-yu and sat in the hot water and then in Hara Setsu's kitchen and told her how he was. Properly. *Not fine.* On Fridays he went to Nezu and drank Haruki's coffee. On Tuesdays he went to Café Asahi, and Matsuda Keiko made the coffee for the empty stool and moved it across to him, and neither of them said anything about the window.

The work came through Hara Setsu now, written on the backs of bath house tickets in her small upright hand. Grid work. A family in Ōji whose new baby screamed every night at three o'clock, in the room where the grandmother had died. A taxi driver whose car kept smelling of a perfume his dead wife used to wear, and who wanted it to stop, and then cried when it did. A primary school in Itabashi where the third-floor toilets went cold every afternoon at a quarter past three. They paid ten thousand yen, twenty, once forty. Sometimes they paid in food. The family with the baby gave him a whole box of mandarin oranges and a hand-knitted hat for Gin.

He was getting better. He did not fall over afterwards any more. He opened the door in his chest slowly, a finger's width, as Sōun had taught him, and closed it slowly, and only shook a little. He went to the konbini after every job and ate something hot under the bright lights and felt himself fill up again.

It was still not enough money.

[break The watch]

[need ch20_pawn_shop | A small pawn shop in Ueno at evening, glass counter full of old watches and cameras, an old man with a jeweller's loupe examining a plain silver wristwatch, Rei on the other side of the counter not looking at it]

The rent for November was due on the twenty-seventh of October. On the twentieth Rei counted everything he had and was thirty-one thousand yen short. There was half a bag of rice in the kitchen and nothing else, and he had been eating the free tofu the old woman at Kamiya gave him for two days.

He sat for a long time at the kitchen table that night. Then he went into Gin's room and opened the top drawer of the desk.

Gin's watch was in there. A plain silver Seiko with a black leather strap, the kind every salaryman in Japan owns. Gin had bought it on his first day at the Division, six years ago, with his first salary, and worn it every day since. He had taken it off on the twelfth of August, the morning before he disappeared. Rei did not know why. He had found it on the desk next to the lamp, face down.

He held it in his hand. It was not like the key. It was not cold. It did not steady him. It was only a watch.

That was what he kept telling himself, all the way to Ueno.

The pawn shop was in a narrow street behind the station, between a shop that sold fishing rods and a shop that sold wigs. An old man with a jeweller's glass screwed into his eye looked at the watch for a long time, and turned it over, and opened the back with a tiny knife, and closed it again.

"Thirty-five thousand," he said. "It's a good one. Somebody looked after it."

"Yes," said Rei. "Somebody did."

He signed the paper. He took the money. He walked out into the street and stood by the fishing rod shop with the notes in his pocket and felt sick. He told himself he could buy it back. He told himself Gin would understand. He told himself Gin would have done the same.

All of that was true. It did not help at all.

[break The cat]

That night he sent Kato a cat. The answer came in a minute. A photograph of a different cat, very fat, asleep in a flowerpot. Under it, *Wednesday. Same place.*

Kato was already in the corner booth when Rei came in, in a dark overcoat over a dark suit, with a cup of black coffee he had not touched. The ivory knight stood on the table, lined up with the edge of the napkin. He looked up as Rei sat down.

"You sold something," he said.

"Don't."

"You're thinking about it very loudly." Kato looked at him for a moment. "It was his."

"I said don't."

"All right." Kato picked up the menu and opened it and looked at it, though Rei was fairly sure he knew it by heart. From behind it he said, "Order the curry. You haven't eaten properly since Tuesday."

Rei laughed, despite himself. It came out shaky.

He heard himself laugh and was surprised. A month ago he had sat on the other side of this table with his hands on his knees like a boy at a job interview. Kato heard him be surprised. A strand of his hair came down over his forehead, and he left it there, and turned a page of the menu he was not reading.

[break The trade]

When the curry had come and gone, Kato took a folded paper from the inside of his jacket.

"Everybody in the building brings their kit back to my counter," he said. "And when they bring it back, they stand there and think about where they've been, and where they're going next, and where they aren't going, because nobody's told them to." He put the paper on the table and squared it with the edge. "This is every thin spot reported in Kita and Itabashi in the last month. Thirty-one of them. Fourteen are getting a response. Seventeen are marked *monitor only*. Seventeen places where something is coming through and nobody official is going to go."

Rei looked at the paper. Then at Kato.

"You want me to give this to the Grid."

"I didn't say that." Kato stood and buttoned his overcoat. "I said I was giving it to you. What you do with it is your business." He put a thousand-yen note under the edge of the plate and straightened it. "But if somebody in an empty swimming pool happened to know where seventeen thin spots were, a lot of old people would sleep better."

"And what does the Grid give you back?"

Kato stopped with one hand on the knight.

"There's a woman in Shin-Ōkubo who moves faster than anyone the Division has ever trained," he said. "Choi something. She worked with Gin once. If she ever wanted to tell somebody what she knows about how the Division recruits from the street, I'd want to listen." He put the knight in his pocket. "I'm not asking for anything. I'm just saying what I'd want. If I were asking."

Rei watched him go out under the green sign.

He understood, sitting there with the paper in his hand, what Switch had meant. *You can't sit on the edge of the pool forever.* He was not sitting on the edge. He was standing in the middle, with one foot on each side, carrying things across. From the Division to the Grid. From the Grid to the Division. Losing a little every time.

He thought of Itsuki. *They're going to use you.*

He thought, maybe I'm using them too.

He did not know if that made it better or worse.

[break Kabukichō]

He gave the list to Switch on the Thursday, by hand, in the doorway of a closed launderette in Higashi-Jūjō that was that week's message point. Switch read it standing up, all of it, without any expression. Then they folded it into their coat.

"Seventeen," they said. "That'll keep us busy."

"I want something back," said Rei.

Switch looked at him with mild interest, as if a cat had asked for the newspaper. "Go on."

"Kanna-sensei. Where she is. How she finds people."

"I don't know where she is. She moves." Switch was quiet for a moment. "But I know how she finds people. She sends someone to Kabukichō. To the kids."

Then Switch was gone, round the corner, and Rei was alone in the doorway of the closed launderette.

He had not looked at it properly. Now he did. It was a narrow shop with a glass front, the glass dusty, a hand-written sign taped inside it. *Closed. Thank you for many years. Nishida.* Through the glass he could see two rows of big white machines, their round doors open like mouths, and a long folding table at the back with a plastic chair beside it. On the table there was a cardboard box of lost socks. On the wall above it hung a calendar from a rice shop, still on March.

He would have walked away. But the door in his chest was open a crack, as it always was now after work, and something on the other side of the glass had turned round to look at him.

An old woman. Small, in a flowered apron and a cardigan, sitting on the plastic chair by the folding table with her hands in her lap. Rei could not see her. He could feel exactly where she was, and the shape of her, and the way she was leaning forward to see him better.

*Oh,* she said. Not out loud. *Oh. You've got his face.*

Rei put his hand flat on the cold glass.

*Thursdays,* she said. *He came on Thursdays. Every week, since spring, after I passed. He didn't bring any washing. He'd sit on the bench by number four and eat his dinner out of a konbini bag and tell me about his day. Nobody else talks to me. They walk past.* She leaned further forward. *Why did he stop coming?*

"He's missing," Rei said quietly, to the glass. "Since August."

The old woman was quiet for a moment.

*The last time,* she said, *he was frightened. I've never seen him frightened. He stood in the door there, where you are, and he didn't come in. He looked at me and he didn't say hello. He looked at me like he wasn't sure who was looking.* Her hands moved in her lap. *Then he went away. He didn't even say goodnight.*

Rei stood very still with his hand on the glass. He thought of Gin's book, the last pages, the ones he had not been able to read yet, because the handwriting changed and he did not know why.

*Is there anything,* he said, *anything at all I can do for you, Nishida-san?*

She thought about it. *Nobody locked the back,* she said at last. *When they took me out. They were in a hurry. Thirty-one years I locked that door every night. I can't go home with the back open.*

The back door was in the alley. It was a plain steel door, and it was unlocked, and it opened onto a dark little room with a mop in a bucket and a kettle on a shelf. A ring of keys hung on a nail just inside, with a plastic tag shaped like a fish. Rei stepped out, and pulled the door shut, and found the right key on the third try, and turned it, and heard the lock go across.

He hung the keys on the outside handle, where somebody would find them.

"It's locked, Nishida-san," he said. "You can go home."

When he came back round to the front, the plastic chair by the folding table was only a plastic chair.

[need ch20_toyoko | Kabukichō at night, the plaza by the cinema tower crowded with Toyoko kids, teenagers in dark hoodies and pale make-up sitting on the pavement with phones, neon reflected on wet ground, a stocky sixteen-year-old girl with buzzed sides and a hoodie looking up warily]

Kabukichō is the entertainment district on the other side of Shinjuku station, the opposite of Ni-chōme. It is enormous and loud and bright all night, full of bars and host clubs and karaoke and love hotels and huge glowing signs. In the middle of it stands a tall cinema tower with a giant model of Godzilla's head looking down over the roofs. And on the pavement at the foot of that tower, every night, sit the Toyoko kids.

That is what the city calls them. Teenagers who have run away from home, or been thrown out, or have a home they do not want to go back to. They come from all over Japan. They sit on the ground by the cinema in dark clothes and heavy make-up, in groups, with their phones, and they sleep in net cafés or on friends' floors or in the rooms of strangers who are not their friends. Some of them are fourteen. The police move them on. They come back.

Switch had given him a name. Rei found her on the third night, sitting on the kerb with her back against a lamp post, eating crisps out of a bag.

She was sixteen. Stocky and solid, in a huge black hoodie, with her hair shaved short at the sides and longer on top. She had three phones lined up on her knee. She did not look up when his shadow fell over her.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked anything."

"You're going to. Everyone's going to. Answer's no." She ate a crisp. "Unless you're paying. Then the answer's maybe."

"Switch sent me."

That made her look up. Her eyes were small and sharp and very tired, and they went over him fast. The plain hard look of someone who has had to read strangers to stay alive.

"Yua," she said, after a moment. "Five thousand for the first answer. Cash. And you buy me a hamburger."

"What if the answer's no good?"

"Then you've bought a nice girl a hamburger." She stood up and put the phones in three different pockets. "Come on. I'm starving. And you look like you are too."

They sat in the window of a hamburger place and she ate two burgers and a large fries and he paid five thousand yen of Gin's watch and she told him about the woman.

"She comes on Sundays," said Yua. "Nice coat. Nice voice. Thirties. She doesn't go for the loud ones. She goes for the quiet ones. The ones who sit on their own. The ones who say they hear things, or see things, or feel things nobody else does." She ate a fry. "She gives them a card with a little white flower on it and says, there's a place where people like you can rest. Where nobody will laugh at you. A tea house. A teacher who understands."

"Do they go?"

"Some of them." Yua looked out of the window at the crowd going past. Her face did not change, but her hand stopped halfway to the fries. "My friend Mina went. In June. She hears voices. Real ones, I mean. Not crazy. She came back in August." She ate the fry. "She doesn't hear them any more. She doesn't hear anything any more. She sits in the corner of the net café and smiles at the wall."

Rei felt the cold come up his back.

"Do you have the card?"

Yua looked at him for a long moment. Then she reached into her hoodie and took out a small square of thick white card and put it on the table. On it was printed a single small white flower. A chrysanthemum. Under it, in tiny grey letters, three words.

*The Far Shore.*

"That one's free," said Yua. "For Mina." She picked up her drink. "Don't go there. People who go there come back empty." She sucked on her straw and looked at him over the top of it, and for one second she looked her age. Sixteen. "You look like you'd go anyway."

"I might."

"Then buy me another hamburger first," said Yua. "In case you don't come back."
