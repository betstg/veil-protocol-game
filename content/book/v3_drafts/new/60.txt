#title A Year

On Monday morning Rei went up to the fourth floor with a list of questions about the Vessel Program written on the back of a dry-cleaning receipt, and knocked on the Director's door.

He had told himself all weekend that the questions were for Usami. Some of them were. How long the Program had been running. Whether the people in it were ever allowed to leave Tokyo. Why the officers who took Usami down for his tests every month never looked at his face. He had written them out in a column in pencil. At the bottom, smaller, squeezed in under the last line, there was one more. He had written it on Sunday night at the kitchen table and then sat looking at it until the trains started.

"Come in," said Adachi.

Riki lifted his head from the cushion by the desk, saw who it was, thumped his tail twice on the floor and went back to sleep. His cast was off now. The leg underneath was thin and shaved, with a long pink scar down the outside, and he held it a little out from the others even lying down.

The Director was at the window with the worn train pass in his fingers. The blinds were half down against the heat. Outside, Marunouchi shimmered. The office smelled of dog and of the green tea that went cold in a white cup on the corner of the desk every morning and that Rei had never once seen him drink.

"Sit," said Adachi. "You have a piece of paper. Ask."

[break The questions]

He asked them in order. Adachi answered them in order, standing, without looking at the paper.

The Program had been running since 1971. There had been forty-three Vessels in that time. There were nine now. They could leave Tokyo with an escort and a signed form, and almost never asked to, because the form took three weeks. The officers who took them down for tests did not look at their faces because they had been trained not to, years ago, by a man who had believed that if you looked a Vessel in the eye the thing inside it could reach you. The man was dead. The training was not.

"It is wrong," said Adachi. "I have known it is wrong for eleven years. I have changed some of it. Not enough." He turned the train pass over. "Usami asked you to ask me that."

"No. I asked myself."

"Then I am glad you did." The Director looked at him with his pale resting eyes. "Next."

Rei looked at the bottom of the receipt.

"What happens," he said, "if there isn't a contract."

The room was very quiet. Riki snored.

"If something from the far side is in a person," said Rei. He kept his voice level. He kept his eyes on the paper. "And there's no contract. Nothing signed. Nothing agreed. It just went in and put them on. And it doesn't want to share." He made himself look up. "How long does the person last?"

Adachi did not answer at once.

He went to his desk and sat down behind it, slowly, as if his back hurt, and put the train pass flat on the blotter in front of him and his hand on top of it.

"A year," he said.

Rei did not move.

"About a year. It does not happen all at once. The person is pushed down. Further every month. They stop being able to see out. Then they stop being able to hear. Then they stop being able to remember that there was ever anything to see or hear." He said it plainly, as he had said everything else. "At the end, there is no one left underneath. The thing is not wearing the person any more. It is the person. You could cut it out and there would be nothing to give back."

"Always?"

"I have been in this building for thirty-four years. I have read every file we have." Adachi looked at his hand on the train pass. "I have never seen anyone last past a year. Not with a strong medium. Not with a family that came every day. Not with a good heart." He was quiet. "Sometimes less. If the thing does terrible things with the body, the person stops wanting to be in it, and lets go sooner. Sometimes a little more. Never much."

Rei folded the receipt in half. And in half again.

"Thank you, sir," he said.

Adachi looked at him for a long time across the desk. Rei waited for him to ask. *Why do you want to know. Who is it. When did it start.* The Director had the kind of face that already knew the answers to questions before he asked them, and Rei sat very still and kept his head as quiet as he could, as if Kato were in the room.

Adachi did not ask.

"Go down to Containment," he said instead. "Ask for Moriya. Tell him I said to show you the east corridor." He picked up the train pass and put it in his breast pocket. "Look at it properly. Then come and tell me what you want to do about the Vessel Program. I would like to know."

[break The east corridor]

Moriya met him at the bottom of the stairs on his crutches. The brace was shorter now, knee to ankle, and he could put some weight on the leg, and he had a new walking rhythm that went thump, swing, thump, swing down the polished floor of the long corridor. He did not ask why Rei had been sent. He looked at him once, slowly, all the way through, as he did with everybody, and said, "All right," and turned left where Rei had only ever turned right.

The east corridor was shorter than the one with Room Nine in it. Older. The floor was not polished. The lights in the ceiling were the old kind that buzzed. There were eight doors, four on each side, steel, with seals pasted round the frames in Uruma's hand and Tomoe's, layer over layer, and small square windows of thick glass at head height.

"Possession without consent," said Moriya. "Long term. People we found too late to get it out of them, or couldn't get it out at all." He stopped at the first door. "We keep them here because nobody else will. Some of them have families who come. Most don't any more."

Rei looked through the glass.

A woman of about sixty sat on the edge of a white bed in a white room, in a cardigan, with her hands in her lap. Her hair had been brushed. Somebody had put a small vase of flowers on the shelf by the bed. Daisies. She was looking at the wall.

"Four months," said Moriya. "She was a cleaner at a hospital in Itabashi. It came in through the mortuary. Her daughter comes on Saturdays and reads to her."

Rei lifted the latch a finger's width.

Two. A woman, still there, a long way down, small and frightened, like someone at the bottom of a well looking up at a circle of sky. And on top of her, heavy, a dull grey thing that did not want anything much except to sit.

"She's still there," said Rei.

"Yes." Moriya moved on. Thump, swing. "Next one."

A young man of about twenty-five, lying on his back on the bed with his eyes open.

"Seven months."

Rei looked. The young man was there. Further down. Much further. Rei could hardly feel him. A faint warmth at the very bottom, like the last coal in a grate that someone has forgotten to rake out.

Next one. A man in his forties, sitting on the floor in the corner with his knees up, rocking very slightly.

"Ten months."

Rei looked, and looked, and did not find anyone underneath at all, until right at the end, when he was about to give up, something moved. Very small. Very far. Like a fish turning over once in deep water.

"He's there," said Rei. His voice was strange. "Just. He's just there."

"Yes," said Moriya. "We think so too. Sometimes he says his daughter's name in his sleep." He moved on.

The last door on the left.

The room behind it was the same as the others. White bed. White walls. Seals round the frame. A man of about fifty stood in the middle of it in grey Containment pyjamas, quite still, with his arms at his sides, looking straight at the little window.

He was looking at Rei.

He smiled.

It was a pleasant smile. A neighbour's smile. And there was nothing behind it. Rei opened the door in his chest wider than a finger, wider than two, and went looking, down, and down, past the smile and past the thing that wore it, down to the bottom where the person should be.

There was no bottom. There was the thing all the way down. It had grown into every corner of him, like water into a sponge. There was nothing left to tell apart from it. There was no warmth, no coal, no fish turning over. There was nobody to give back.

"Fourteen months," said Moriya quietly. "His name was Kanzaki Hiroshi. He was a bus driver in Adachi ward. He had two sons." He put his hand flat on the cold steel of the door. "We'll keep him here until the body stops. That's all we can do now. There's nobody in there to save."

The man in the room went on smiling at Rei through the glass.

Rei shut the latch. He shut it all the way. He turned round and put his back against the wall of the east corridor and slid down it and sat on the old unpolished floor with his knees up, like the man in the corner of the third room.

Moriya did not say anything. He lowered himself down beside Rei, slowly, with the braced leg out straight in front of him and the crutches across his lap, and sat there.

[break Counting]

It had been the twelfth of August.

Rei had always known the date. It was on the police report, and on the CVD file Haruki had seen, and in the margin of Gin's book in his own handwriting, and on the card under the photograph in Kanna-sensei's white room. *Uedera Gin. August.* The twelfth of August. Obon week. The night the key came home without him.

Today was the seventh.

He had known that too. He had looked at the date on his phone every morning for a year. He had never once put the two numbers next to each other and subtracted them. He sat on the floor of the east corridor and did it now, as slowly as a child doing sums, and the answer was five.

Five days.

He thought of a tall man under a tree in Asagaya, setting a bottle of barley tea down very carefully on the pavement so it would not fall over. He thought of the kite market, and a rough voice shouting *my brother* across a shrine yard. That had been February. Six months ago. Six months is a long way down.

He thought of the fish turning over in deep water.

"How many days have you got?" said Moriya.

Rei looked at him.

"I'm not going to ask who," said Moriya. He was looking at the door opposite, not at Rei. "I've been reading bodies for fifteen years. You've been carrying something since I met you that's the exact weight and shape of a person. And the Director sent you down here." He turned the crutch over in his hands. "How many days?"

"Five."

Moriya nodded slowly.

"Then you've got five days," he said. "Not none." He looked at the door with the smiling man behind it. "And Adachi said *about*. I've known him nine years. He doesn't say *about* unless he means it."

[break The kitchen]

That night he told them.

Itsuki and Kato, at the kitchen table in Jūjō, with the window open on the hot night and the fan going round and round on top of the fridge and Gorō asleep on the back of Gin's chair. He told them about the receipt and the Director and the east corridor and the man who had been a bus driver in Adachi ward. He told them the number.

Kato already knew. He had heard it from the lift. He sat with the knight in his hand and did not turn it.

Itsuki did not know. Itsuki sat very still while Rei talked, with his hands flat on the table, and when Rei had finished he got up and went to the wall by the fridge, where the calendar hung, the free one from the rice shop with a picture of Mount Fuji for August. He took a pen out of his pocket. He drew a small neat circle round the twelfth.

Then he stood and looked at it.

"Five," he said.

"About," said Rei.

"About." Itsuki put the pen back in his pocket. His hand was shaking. He did not try to hide it. "And the flat's the tenth."

"The flat's the tenth."

Itsuki turned round. His eyes were wet and very angry, and not with Rei.

"Then we don't sleep," he said. "Not properly. Not till the twelfth. We find him. We find him before the twelfth, and we find out what's left, and if there's anything left we hold on to it with both hands." He looked at Kato. "You hear everything in this city. Start listening for a tall man who doesn't know what a ticket gate is."

Kato looked up.

"He knows what a ticket gate is," said Kato. "That's the problem. He's learned." He put the knight down on the table. "But I'll listen."

Gorō woke up on the back of the chair, and looked at the three of them, and at the calendar on the wall with its circle, and put his head under his wing again.

Rei sat at the table after they had gone and looked at the circle for a long time.

Then he got Gin's book out of his bag and opened it at the back, at the empty pages after his own notes, and wrote the date at the top of one, and under it, in pencil, everything he knew about where his brother had been seen in the last six months. Every street. Every station. Every day. A tree in Asagaya. A shrine in Ōji. A footbridge at Ogikubo that Kato had heard, faintly, at two in the morning on the night of the white gate.

It was not very much. It filled half a page.

He sat and looked at it until he could see the shape it made.
