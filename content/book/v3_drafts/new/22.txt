#title The Message

The building was on Switch's list of seventeen. Number eleven. A five-storey block of flats in Higashi-Jūjō, empty since the spring, waiting to be knocked down. *Monitor only*, the Division had written. *Low activity.*

Two Grid people had gone in on Wednesday to check it. A woman who did wards and a man who read rooms. They had come out after forty minutes, white-faced, and refused to go back. They said the stairs went wrong. They said that on the fourth floor the corridor kept going after it should have stopped. They said they heard a child crying behind a door, and when they opened the door, there was the same corridor again, and the crying was behind the next door. Switch offered the job to anyone at forty thousand yen. Nobody took it.

Rei took it.

He did not tell Haruki. Haruki would have said no, or come with him, and Rei did not want either. He told himself it was because of the money, and some of it was. The rent for December would come round soon, and Gin's watch was in a pawn shop in Ueno, and the forty thousand would buy it back.

But some of it was the white room. Every night since Kabukichō he had closed his eyes and seen a girl he had never met, smiling at the wall of a net café. He had needed to do something with his hands. Something he could finish.

[need ch22_flats | An empty five-storey Tokyo apartment block at night waiting for demolition, outside walkways, broken windows, a single young man with a torch and a backpack climbing the outside stairs]

He went in on Saturday night at nine, after the bath.

The fourth floor was a long concrete walkway with doors along one side and a railing on the other, exactly like the building Rei lived in. The railing looked out over the street. He could see the lights of a konbini on the corner, and a man walking a dog. Ordinary. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

It was cold. Not the wet cold of the platform. A thin, sad, grey cold, like a room where the heating has been off for years. And under it, very faint, a sound. A child crying. Small, tired, hopeless crying, the kind a child does when it has been crying for a long time and does not expect anyone to come.

It came from behind the fourth door.

He did everything Gin's book said. He laid a salt line across the walkway behind him, so he had a way back. He wrote a ward in chalk on the concrete by his feet. He held the key on its cord in his left hand. Then he opened the fourth door.

There was a corridor behind it.

Not a flat. A corridor. The same concrete walkway, with the same doors along one side. But no railing on the other side. A wall. A blank grey wall where the street should have been. And the crying was coming from behind the fourth door along.

Rei turned round.

The door he had come through was there. He opened it. Behind it was the same corridor again. Doors. A blank grey wall. Crying behind the fourth door. His salt line was gone. His chalk ward was gone.

He had been in the building for nine minutes.

[break The corridor]

He tried for an hour.

He opened doors. Every one opened onto the same corridor. He walked to the end of the corridor and there was a staircase, going down, and he went down it, and at the bottom there was the corridor again, with the same doors and the same wall and the same crying. He went up. The same. He counted the doors. Twelve. He opened all twelve. Twelve times, the same corridor.

He opened the door in his chest wider, to read the place, and that was a mistake. The building opened up around him like a wound. He felt what it was. Not a demon. Not one spirit. Something worse and sadder than either. Gin's book called it a *suffering being*. Many small griefs, from many years, from many people who had lived in these flats and been unhappy in them, all poured into one place and stuck together like old chewing gum, until they made something that had a shape and a hunger. It did not want to kill him. It did not want anything. It just did not want to be alone. And it had folded the building round him, the way a child holds a toy, so he could not leave.

The crying went on and on behind the fourth door. He did not open it again.

By the end of the hour his legs were shaking. The tunnel had started at the edges of his eyes. He could feel himself running out, the way you feel a phone battery dying, the screen getting dimmer. Every door he opened cost him a little. Every minute he held the door in his chest open cost him more. He sat down on the concrete with his back against the blank grey wall and made himself breathe.

He took out his phone. One bar of signal. Then none. Then one.

He did not call Haruki. He did not call Okada. He knew what he needed, and it was not either of them. He needed a door. A real door. A door that went somewhere else.

He typed with his thumb, very slowly, because his hands would not keep still.

*itsuki. I'm stuck. higashi-jujo, the empty flats behind the shrine, 4th floor. the doors all go to the same place. I need a door that goes somewhere else.*

He looked at it. He added one more line.

*I'm sorry I shoved you.*

He pressed send. The bar of signal flickered. The message said *Sending*. It said *Sending* for a long time.

Then it said *Delivered*.

Rei closed his eyes. He was so tired. The crying behind the fourth door had gone softer. It sounded almost like a lullaby now. It would be so easy to stay. To sit here against the wall and let the building hold him. It was not trying to hurt him. It only wanted company.

His phone buzzed in his hand.

*Read.*

And then, a second later, in lowercase:

*you absolute fucking idiot. stay there. don't open any more doors. I'm coming.*

[break Itsuki]

It took Itsuki twenty-six minutes. Rei found out later that he had come back from Kamakura on Thursday without telling anybody, and that he had been lying on the floor of his flat near Ueno looking at the ceiling when the message came, and that he had run to the station in his slippers and taken the Keihin-Tōhoku north and run from Higashi-Jūjō station to the building, still in his slippers, with his oldest sketchbook under his arm, because he always took it, the way other people take their keys.

Rei did not know any of that. He knew only that he was sitting against the blank wall with his eyes closing, and the crying had become a lullaby, and then his phone buzzed again.

*I'm outside. I can see the 4th floor. I can see you. you're sitting on the walkway with your back against the railing. you look like shit.*

Rei opened his eyes. He was sitting against a grey wall. There was no railing.

*I can't see you,* he typed. *there's a wall.*

*there's no wall. there's a railing. you're looking right at me and you can't see me.* A pause. *ok. ok. hold on. I'm going to draw it.*

*itsuki this isn't the time for art.*

*shut up. it's always the time for art. stay there.*

A long pause. Rei watched the screen. The battery was at four percent.

Down on the street, under the one working streetlight, Kuroda Itsuki sat on the kerb in his slippers with his oldest sketchbook open on his knees and a pencil in his hand, and drew the building. Fast. Not looking at the page much. Looking up at the fourth floor, and down, and up. He did not think about what he was drawing. He never did. He let his hand go, and his hand put in the walkway, and the railing, and the row of doors, twelve of them, and the small dark shape of his friend sitting on the concrete with his knees up.

Then his hand did something he did not tell it to do.

It drew eleven of the doors shut. Flat, plain, closed. And the ninth door it drew standing open. Wide open. And through the open doorway it drew, not a dark flat, but a railing, and a street, and a streetlight, and a small figure sitting on a kerb under the light with a book on its knees.

Itsuki looked at it for a long time. His hand had gone cold.

Rei's phone buzzed. A photograph. A pencil drawing of the building, quick and sure, every line right. Eleven doors shut. The ninth one open, and through it, impossibly, the street outside.

*my hand did that,* Itsuki wrote. *I didn't. I don't know what it means. door 9.*

*the crying's behind door 4.*

*I know. my drawing doesn't care about door 4. door 9. go.*

Rei got up. His legs nearly gave way. He walked along the corridor, past the fourth door, where the lullaby went on and on, and he did not open it. He counted. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

He put his palm flat on the ninth door. It was cold and slightly damp, like the side of a fish tank.

He opened it.

Cold air came in. Real air, November air, smelling of exhaust and wet leaves and somebody's cooking. Through the doorway was the walkway, the real one, with the green-painted railing and the street below. And under the streetlight on the far kerb, a skinny figure in a grey hoodie and slippers, with a sketchbook on his knees, looking up.

Rei stepped through. Behind him, the corridor folded shut like a wet newspaper, and the crying stopped, and there was nothing behind him but a dark empty flat with a broken window and a calendar on the wall from a year that had finished long ago.

[break The kerb]

He did not remember the stairs. He remembered sitting down on the kerb next to Itsuki, very suddenly, because his legs had decided for him.

Itsuki was shaking from head to foot. His nose was running in the cold. He was holding the sketchbook open on his knees and staring at the drawing.

"I didn't do that," he said. His voice was high and strange. "I mean I did. But I didn't. I drew the building. I was drawing the building. And then my hand drew that door open, and I didn't tell it to, and I didn't know why, and it was *right*." He looked at Rei. "Rei. It was right."

"I know."

"How was it right? How does my hand know which door?"

Rei thought of a small wet canvas in a studio at Geidai. A walkway at night, and a key lying on the concrete. Finished at midnight. Four hours before the key was there.

"It does that," he said. "Your hand. It always has. You just don't usually see it happen."

Itsuki looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he closed the sketchbook and held it shut against his chest with both arms, the way a child holds a book it does not want taken away.

"You're wearing slippers," said Rei.

Itsuki looked down at his feet. Then he looked back at Rei. And he laughed. It came out of him all at once, a huge, helpless, hiccupping laugh, and he could not stop it, and it set Rei off, and the two of them sat on the kerb outside an empty building in Higashi-Jūjō at eleven at night, Itsuki in his slippers with a runny nose and Rei with salt on his knees and four percent battery, and laughed until they could not breathe. A man walking a dog crossed to the other side of the road.

When the laughter ran out, they sat side by side with their backs against a lamp post, looking at the empty building.

"I'm sorry I shoved you," said Rei.

"You said that in the message."

"I'm saying it with my mouth."

"Okay." Itsuki wiped his nose with the back of his hand. "I'm sorry I said Gin was gone." He did not look at Rei. "I didn't mean it. I've never meant anything less in my life. I said it because I was scared and it was the worst thing I could think of."

"I know."

"Don't *I know* me. Let me be sorry."

"Okay. Be sorry."

"I'm being sorry." Itsuki pulled his knees up. "And you're still an idiot. You went into a folded building on your own. On your *own*, Rei. With chalk. You didn't tell Haruki. You didn't tell anyone."

"I told you."

"After! After you were stuck!" Itsuki turned and glared at him. "If you'd died in there I'd have had to explain to Gin, when we find him, that his brother died because he was too proud to send a text. And Gin would have killed me. And then I'd be dead too. And it would be your fault."

"That's a lot of people dead."

"It's a tragedy. It's a whole tragedy. I'd paint it." Itsuki put his head back against the lamp post. "Don't do it again."

"I won't."

"You will. But tell me first. Every time. Promise."

"I promise."

They sat for a while. A late train went over the bridge at the end of the street, a line of yellow windows, slow and bright.

Rei reached over and put his hand on top of Itsuki's, on the cold kerb between them, and left it there.

Itsuki looked at their hands. Then he looked at the building.

"Tomorrow," he said, after a while, "you're taking me to your stupid Grid."
