#title Glyphs

In the middle of July, Uruma decided Rei was going to learn to read.

"Not books," he said, sitting cross-legged on top of a table in the Sigil Room with a bowl of rice crackers in his lap. "You can read books. Badly, but you can. I mean marks." He waved a long black-nailed hand at the walls, at the hundreds of drying sheets pinned from floor to ceiling, black and red and grey. "Every one of those does something. Every one of them is a sentence. And you walk past them every day like a tourist in a temple, going *ooh, pretty*." He crunched a cracker. "Gin-kun could read a ward from across a street in the rain. You can't read the one on your own wrist."

"I know what it does."

"You know what it *does*. You don't know what it *says*." Uruma pointed at Rei's wrist with a cracker. "Big difference. A man can know what a knife does without being able to read the name of the shop it came from."

So for three weeks, every lunchtime, Rei sat on a stool in the Sigil Room with an inkstone and a brush and a stack of cheap practice paper and learned the alphabet of the far side.

It was not an alphabet. That was the first thing. There were no letters, only parts. A stroke that meant *closed*. A stroke that meant *this side*. A hook that meant *held*. A dot that meant *the one who made this*. You put them together the way you put together a sentence, and the sentence did what it said, as long as whoever wrote it meant it. Tomoe, behind her bead curtain at the other end of the room, called it grammar, and said that Uruma's was terrible and that he got away with it by meaning things very loudly.

"I heard that," said Uruma.

"You were meant to," said the curtain.

Rei was bad at it. His strokes were too heavy. His hooks were too open. He meant things too quietly, Uruma said, as he did everything, and the paper did not believe him. On the eighth day he wrote a simple ward for *quiet* that was supposed to make the space above the paper a little calmer, and instead the paper curled up at both ends and slid off the table on its own.

Uruma laughed so hard that he fell off his table.

On the ninth day he wrote it again. It lay flat. The air above it went still, very slightly, like a room when the radio is turned off in the next flat.

Uruma stopped laughing and looked at it for a long time.

"There," he said. "Now you've said something."

[break What the flat said]

The thing about learning to read, Rei found, is that once you can do it, you cannot stop.

He saw marks everywhere. On the backs of the ticket gates at Ōji station, very small, scratched into the metal by someone a long time ago. *Pass, but slowly.* Under the eaves of the shrine at the bottom of the hill, where the Bon dance had been, a whole sentence in faded red. *This ground remembers, be polite.* On the side of a vending machine outside the Division, in grey marker, a tiny crude word that Uruma later told him was a joke between two seal-makers that had been going on since 1987 and that was too rude to translate.

And at home.

He came up the stairs on a Thursday night at the end of July with his bag over his shoulder and Gorō on the railing, and stopped on the walkway, and looked at his own front door.

Above it, on the concrete lintel, so faint that he had walked under it ten thousand times without seeing it, there was a mark. Grey. Weathered. Old. Three strokes and a hook and a dot. *Closed to what is not asked in. Held. G.*

Rei stood on the walkway and could not move.

He went inside. He turned on every light. He went round the flat slowly, with a torch, as he had been taught to go round a crime scene, and he looked.

They were everywhere.

Under the brass hook by the door, on the wall, behind where the keys hung, a small one. *Come home.* On the frame of the kitchen window, the one he had stood at as a small boy and seen things standing in the dark outside looking in, a long line of them running all the way round, overlapping, layer over layer, years and years of them, some so old the pencil had almost gone and some, at the top corner, new. Still sharp. Written in the spring. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.* Under the tatami in his own corner of the living room, when he lifted the edge, one painted in ink on the boards beneath, very carefully, by someone kneeling on the floor in the night. *Sleep.*

And on the inside of the lid of the rice cooker, the one with the cracked lid that Gin had refused to replace for twelve years, small and neat and almost a joke, *Eat.*

He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge and the torch in his lap.

All his life. All his life, the things at the window had stood outside and looked in, and never once come in. He had thought it was the door in his chest. The one Gin had taught him to close at the kitchen table when he was eight. He had thought he was doing it himself.

He had not been doing it himself. Not all of it. His brother had been writing on the walls of their home since before Rei could remember, in the night, with a pencil and a brush and a pot of ink, while Rei slept on the sofa with one foot hanging off the end. Saying the same thing over and over to the dark. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.*

Gorō hopped down off the railing onto the windowsill and looked in at him through the glass with the blue eye.

"I know," said Rei. His voice did not work properly. "I know. I'm an idiot. I never looked up."

[break The radio]

There was a page at the very back of Gin's book that Rei had read a dozen times and never acted on, because he had not known what it meant.

*Case 14-09. The Radio. Talks. Cooperative. Worth more than most of my contacts.*

*A Panasonic RF-2200 shortwave radio, made in 1973, found in a police substation storage room during a routine clearance in my second year. The clearance team flagged it as a register anomaly, non-threatening, and moved on. I stayed.*

*He talks. Not in a voice. He changes the station. A word from the news, a word from a song, a word from the weather. You have to listen. He knows things about this district that I do not. He knew some of my contacts were not what they presented as, several weeks before I had enough to say so. He does not explain how he knows. He just changes the station.*

*Rei. If you are reading this, the radio is in the evidence archive, on the third shelf, marked Case 14-09, retain. Do not let anyone clear it. Ask him about the people who read as something other than what they are. He will know what you mean.*

Rei went down to the third basement on Friday after work, with a torch and a tin of rice crackers, because Uruma said nothing in the building liked to be visited empty-handed.

The evidence archive was as cold as it had been in February. Rows and rows of grey shelves going away under the humming lights. He found the third shelf in the row nearest the door, the one people walked past without looking because it was too obvious. And there, between a box of children's shoes and a bag of old house keys, was a radio.

It was big and square and silver and black, like a small suitcase, with a long telescopic aerial folded down along its back and a row of chunky dials and a big round tuning wheel and a window with a needle in it, like the speedometer of an old car. There was a strip of masking tape across the top with handwriting on it. *Case 14-09. RETAIN. Do not clear. G.U.*

Rei put down the tin of crackers on the shelf beside it, which felt stupid and also correct.

"Hello," he said. "I'm Rei. Gin's brother."

Nothing happened.

He pulled up the aerial. He turned the big dial on the front that said POWER. It clicked. A small orange light came on behind the tuning window, warm and old-fashioned, like a lamp in a farmhouse. Static came out of the speaker, soft and hissing, like rain on a window.

Then the needle in the tuning window moved.

Nobody was touching the wheel. The needle slid slowly to the left, and the static cut, and a woman's voice, very clear, a newsreader from some station somewhere, said one word.

*"...brother..."*

The needle slid right. Hiss. A man singing an old enka song, very far away.

*"...I've been waiting..."*

Hiss. Left again. A weather report.

*"...for a long time..."*

Rei sat down on the cold floor of the evidence archive, in front of the third shelf, with his back against the shelf opposite, and his heart going very fast.

"Gin's gone," he said. "Since August. Something's wearing him. I'm trying to get him back."

The needle moved. A children's programme. A cheerful voice.

*"...I know..."*

Hiss. A baseball commentator, very excited.

*"...he was here... on Tuesday... last year... he told me... if you came..."*

Hiss. Something classical, a woman speaking quietly between pieces.

*"...to tell you... about the clean ones..."*

[break The clean ones]

It took an hour. The radio could not say anything quickly. It had to find each word somewhere in the air of Tokyo, on some station, in some song or advert or news bulletin, and wait for it to come round, and catch it. Sometimes it caught the wrong one and went back. Sometimes a whole minute went by in static while it searched. Rei sat on the floor and waited, and ate three rice crackers, and listened.

"Gin's book says to ask you about people," he said. "The ones who read as something other than what they are."

The needle went to a station selling washing powder. A bright woman's voice.

*"...spotless... in twenty minutes..."*

Hiss. A news bulletin, very calm.

*"...public confidence... remains high..."*

Rei sat up.

He thought of the platform at Ōji in August, the first week. A man taken in front of him and dying on the concrete. And a team in grey overalls who came down the stairs before the ambulance and had the platform washed and the tape down and the trains running in twenty minutes. He thought of a woman in a grey suit at the families' meeting in Ōtemachi, standing in front of the screen, saying his brother's name in a voice that did not change at all.

"Hagiwara," he said.

The needle did not move for a long time. Then it slid, very slowly, to a station playing a piano, slow and soft, and a woman's voice between pieces said one word.

*"...careful..."*

Hiss. A sports report.

*"...not alone... others... high up... clean hands..."*

Hiss. Something that might have been a children's story.

*"...and dirty friends..."*

"Who?" said Rei. "Which friends?"

But the radio had gone back to static, soft and grey, like rain. He waited ten minutes. It said nothing more about it. He understood, sitting there with the cold coming up from the floor, that it did not know, or would not say, and that Gin had probably sat here in the same place and got the same answer.

[break The baby]

Before he left, it told him one more thing, without being asked.

The needle went all the way across the dial, slowly, from one end to the other, as if it were looking for something very particular. And stopped on a lullaby. An old one. A woman singing it on some programme for children, at the very edge of the dial, almost lost.

*"...in Nezu..."* said the radio. *"...a baby... in March... who came back..."*

"Came back?"

*"...somebody old... in somebody new... ask them..."*

Then the orange light went out.

Rei stood in the cold aisle of the evidence archive with the torch in his hand and turned the dial, and turned it again, and the radio did not come back on. He pushed the aerial down. He left the tin of rice crackers on the shelf beside it, open, because it seemed right. He put a hand on the cold silver case, very lightly, like a hand on the shoulder of someone who has fallen asleep in a chair.

"Thank you," he said. "I'll come back."

[break Grandmother Mitsu]

He had not been to the house in Nezu since December.

He went on Sunday, in the heat, with a box of peaches from the shop by the station because he could not think what else to bring. He stood outside the wall in the lane for a long time. The gate was the same. Old dark wood under a little tiled roof, and the stone lantern inside with moss on it, and the sound of water somewhere in the garden.

Mrs. Fujii opened it. She looked at him and at the peaches and said, "Oh," and then, "Wait there," and went away, and came back with Grandmother Mitsu.

The old woman was smaller than he remembered. She wore a dark summer kimono with a pattern of grey dragonflies, and her white hair was pinned up with one long black pin, and she was holding a fan she did not use. She looked at Rei for a long time with her sharp black eyes.

"You stopped coming," she said.

"Yes."

"Because of Itsuki."

"Yes."

"Good," said Grandmother Mitsu. "Somebody in this family should have stopped something because of Itsuki." She took the peaches out of his hands. "Come in. Take your shoes off. You're dripping on my step."

She took him into a small room at the back that he had never seen, with the screens open onto the garden and a bamboo blind half down against the sun. On the tatami there was a futon, and on the futon there was a baby asleep on his back, in a cotton vest, with his arms flung up beside his head. A young woman sat beside him fanning him slowly with a paper fan. Takuya stood in the doorway to the corridor with his arms folded, looking embarrassed and proud and tired, as new fathers do.

"My great-grandson," said Grandmother Mitsu. "Kuroda Haru. Born the eighteenth of March." She sat down on a cushion by the futon with a small grunt. "And my sister. Kuroda Fumi. Died the eleventh of March, in this room, at ninety-one."

Rei looked at the baby.

He did not need to lift the latch very far. He opened the door in his chest one finger's width and looked, and there it was, as plain as the peaches. A very small new person, bright and soft and hardly begun. And inside him, curled up very small, quiet, patient, someone old. Not wearing him. Not pushing. Sleeping in him, as a cat sleeps in a basket that is also used for laundry.

"How do you know?" said Rei.

"She did this," said Grandmother Mitsu. She leaned forward and touched the baby's left hand where it lay open by his head. The fingers curled at once round hers. The thumb was tucked in under the other fingers, very neatly. "All her life. Asleep. Thumb inside. Like a child holding a sweet. Nobody else in this family sleeps like that." She did not take her finger away. "And at the naming, when the priest said her name by mistake instead of his, he opened his eyes and laughed."

Takuya, in the doorway, made a small sound.

"Is that allowed?" said Rei. "Is she... will he be all right? Will he be him?"

Grandmother Mitsu looked at him with something that was almost approval.

"Sit," she said. "This is a lesson. You came at the right time. Nobody else in this house wants one any more."

[break The ends]

She told him, with the baby asleep between them and the cicadas going in the garden, the ways a person can end.

"Most cross," she said. "They die, and they feel the pull, and they go. On. Toward the light. We don't know what is past it and we don't ask. That is the end that is meant." She held up one finger. "Some stay. They are frightened, or angry, or they have not finished something, or nobody told them they could go. They stay in a place, or a thing. You know those. You sent the lady from the watch shop on with a bun and a handful of salt. Yes, we heard. Haruki told Sōun. Sōun pretended not to listen." A second finger. "Some are eaten. Something from the far side finds them while they are staying, lost and weak, and feeds on them until they are nothing. That is the worst end. There is no coming back from it." A third. "Some are taken in. A living person who is dying grabs at a soul going by, without meaning to. Or a living person is asked, and says yes. Two in one body. You've seen that, too. The man upstairs in your Division with the cooking programmes."

"Fujimoto."

"Fujimoto." She held up a fourth finger. "And some come back. Very rarely. Someone who has crossed, and gone on, and been there a while, comes back into somebody new. Most of them do not remember anything. It is a habit, sometimes. A way of laughing. A way of holding the thumb." She looked down at the baby. "My sister crossed. I watched her go. I was holding her hand. She went straight, the way she did everything. And then she came back a week later into my granddaughter-in-law's baby." She sniffed. "She always did hate to miss a party."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows." Grandmother Mitsu took her finger out of the baby's hand at last, and he made a small complaining noise in his sleep and tucked the thumb in again. "There are things about the far side that nobody knows. Not us. Not your Division. Not the old things that come through it. Your brother used to sit in this room and ask me *why* until I threw a cushion at him." She looked at Rei. "You ask the same way. With your whole face."

"Will he be him?" said Rei again. "Haru."

"Yes," said the old woman. "He will be Haru. Fumi is not wearing him. She is only keeping him company. When he is older, she'll go quiet, and he won't remember, and one day when he's an old man he'll fall asleep with his thumb in his fist and his granddaughter will laugh at him." She picked up the fan at last and fanned herself. "That is a good end, too. Not every story with a dead person in it has to be a sad one."

The baby woke up then, all at once, as babies do, and looked straight up at Rei with round dark eyes.

Rei did not know what he expected. Something old. Something knowing.

The baby looked at him for a while, very seriously. Then he sneezed, and was astonished by it, and began to cry, and his mother picked him up.

Grandmother Mitsu laughed so much that she had to hold on to the edge of the futon.

[break The kitchen window]

That night, at the kitchen table, he wrote down what the radio had said in the back of Gin's book, in pencil, under his brother's handwriting. *Hagiwara. Clean hands. Dirty friends. High up.* He wrote it very small. Gin's book said, in a margin, underlined, *never accuse on a radio's word alone.*

Then he looked up at the frame of the kitchen window, at the long line of faint marks running all the way round it, layer over layer, years of them. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.*

He got the brush and the ink stone out of his bag, the cheap practice ones from the Sigil Room, and stood on a chair, and in the top corner, beside the newest of Gin's, very carefully, with his strokes too heavy and his hook too open, he wrote one of his own. Three strokes, a hook and a dot.

*Not here. Not this one. Not him either.*

It was crooked. He meant it as loudly as he could. Then he got down off the chair and washed the brush and went to bed, and for the first time in a long while he slept with the light off.
