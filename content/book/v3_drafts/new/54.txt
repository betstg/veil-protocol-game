#title Glyphs

In the middle of June, Uruma decided Rei was going to learn to read.

"Not books," he said, sitting cross-legged on top of a table in the Sigil Room with a bowl of rice crackers in his lap. "You can read books. Badly, but you can. I mean marks." He waved a long black-nailed hand at the walls, at the hundreds of drying sheets pinned from floor to ceiling, black and red and grey. "Every one of those does something. Every one of them is a sentence. And you walk past them every day like a tourist in a temple, going *ooh, pretty*." He crunched a cracker. "Gin-kun could read a ward from across a street in the rain. You can't read the one on your own wrist."

"I know what it does."

"You know what it *does*. You don't know what it *says*." Uruma pointed at Rei's wrist with a cracker. "Big difference. A man can know what a knife does without being able to read the name of the shop it came from."

So for three weeks, every lunchtime, Rei sat on a stool in the Sigil Room with an inkstone and a brush and a stack of cheap practice paper and learned the alphabet of the far side.

It was not an alphabet. That was the first thing. There were no letters, only parts. A stroke that meant *closed*. A stroke that meant *this side*. A hook that meant *held*. A dot that meant *the one who made this*. You put them together the way you put together a sentence, and the sentence did what it said, as long as whoever wrote it meant it. Uruma called it Symbol Work. Tomoe, behind her bead curtain at the other end of the room, called it grammar, and said that Uruma's was terrible and that he got away with it by meaning things very loudly.

"I heard that," said Uruma.

"You were meant to," said the curtain.

Rei was bad at it. His strokes were too heavy. His hooks were too open. He meant things too quietly, Uruma said, the way he did everything, and the paper did not believe him. On the eighth day he wrote a simple ward for *quiet* that was supposed to make the space above the paper a little calmer, and instead the paper curled up at both ends and slid off the table on its own.

Uruma laughed so hard that he fell off his table.

On the ninth day he wrote it again. It lay flat. The air above it went still, very slightly, the way a room goes still when the radio is turned off in the next flat.

Uruma stopped laughing and looked at it for a long time.

"There," he said. "Now you've said something."

[break What the flat said]

The thing about learning to read, Rei found, is that once you can do it, you cannot stop.

He saw marks everywhere. On the backs of the ticket gates at Ōji station, very small, scratched into the metal by someone a long time ago. *Pass, but slowly.* Under the eaves of the shrine at the bottom of the hill, where the Bon dance had been, a whole sentence in faded red. *This ground remembers, be polite.* On the side of a vending machine outside the Division, in grey marker, a tiny crude word that Uruma later told him was a joke between two seal-makers that had been going on since 1987 and that was too rude to translate.

And at home.

He came up the stairs on a Thursday night in June with his bag over his shoulder and Gorō on the railing, and stopped on the walkway, and looked at his own front door.

Above it, on the concrete lintel, so faint that he had walked under it ten thousand times without seeing it, there was a mark. Grey. Weathered. Old. Three strokes and a hook and a dot. *Closed to what is not asked in. Held. G.*

Rei stood on the walkway and could not move.

He went inside. He turned on every light. He went round the flat slowly, with a torch, the way he went round a crime scene, and he looked.

They were everywhere.

Under the brass hook by the door, on the wall, behind where the keys hung, a small one. *Come home.* On the frame of the kitchen window, the one he had stood at as a small boy and seen things standing in the dark outside looking in, a long line of them running all the way round, overlapping, layer over layer, years and years of them, some so old the pencil had almost gone and some, at the top corner, new. Still sharp. Written in the spring. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.* Under the tatami in his own corner of the living room, when he lifted the edge, one painted in ink on the boards beneath, very carefully, by someone kneeling on the floor in the night. *Sleep.*

And on the inside of the lid of the rice cooker, the one with the cracked lid that Gin had refused to replace for twelve years, small and neat and almost a joke, *Eat.*

He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge and the torch in his lap.

All his life. All his life, the things at the window had stood outside and looked in, and never once come in. He had thought it was the door in his chest. The one Gin had taught him to close at the kitchen table when he was eight. He had thought he was doing it himself.

He had not been doing it himself. Not all of it. His brother had been writing on the walls of their home for nineteen years, in the night, with a pencil and a brush and a pot of ink, while Rei slept on the sofa with one foot hanging off the end. Saying the same thing over and over to the dark. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.*

Gorō hopped down off the railing onto the windowsill and looked in at him through the glass with the blue eye.

"I know," said Rei. His voice did not work properly. "I know. I'm an idiot. I never looked up."

[break The radio]

There was a page at the very back of Gin's book that Rei had read a dozen times and never acted on, because he had not known what it meant.

*Case 14-09. The Radio. Status: active contact established. Cooperative. High operational value.*

*A Panasonic RF-2200 shortwave radio, made in 1973, found in a police substation storage room during a routine clearance in my second year. The clearance team flagged it as a register anomaly, non-threatening, and moved on. I stayed.*

*He talks. Not in a voice. He changes the station. A word from the news, a word from a song, a word from the weather. You have to listen. He knows things about this district that I do not. He knew some of my contacts were not what they presented as, several weeks before I had enough to say so. He does not explain how he knows. He just changes the station.*

*Rei. If you are reading this, the radio is in the evidence archive, on the third shelf, marked Case 14-09, retain. Do not let anyone clear it. Ask him about the people who read as something other than what they are. He will know what you mean.*

Rei went down to the second basement on Friday after work, with a torch and a tin of rice crackers, because Uruma said nothing in the building liked to be visited empty-handed.

The evidence archive was as cold as it had been in January. Rows and rows of grey shelves going away under the humming lights. He found the third shelf in the row nearest the door, the one people walked past without looking because it was too obvious. And there, between a box of children's shoes and a bag of old house keys, was a radio.

It was big and square and silver and black, like a small suitcase, with a long telescopic aerial folded down along its back and a row of chunky dials and a big round tuning wheel and a window with a needle in it, like the speedometer of an old car. There was a strip of masking tape across the top with handwriting on it. *Case 14-09. RETAIN. Do not clear. G.U.*

Rei put down the tin of crackers on the shelf beside it, which felt stupid and also correct.

"Hello," he said. "I'm Rei. Gin's brother."

Nothing happened.

He pulled up the aerial. He turned the big dial on the front that said POWER. It clicked. A small orange light came on behind the tuning window, warm and old-fashioned, like a lamp in a farmhouse. Static came out of the speaker, soft and hissing, like rain on a window.

Then the needle in the tuning window moved.

Nobody was touching the wheel. The needle slid slowly to the left, and the static cut, and a woman's voice, very clear, a newsreader from some station somewhere, said one word.

*"...brother..."*

The needle slid right. Hiss. A man singing an old enka song, very far away.

*"...I've been waiting..."*

Hiss. Left again. A weather report.

*"...for a long time..."*

Rei sat down on the cold floor of the evidence archive, in front of the third shelf, with his back against the shelf opposite, and his heart going very fast.

"Gin's gone," he said. "Since August. Something's wearing him. I'm trying to get him back."

The needle moved. A children's programme. A cheerful voice.

*"...I know..."*

Hiss. A baseball commentator, very excited.

*"...he was here... on Tuesday... last year... he told me... if you came..."*

Hiss. Something classical, a woman speaking quietly between pieces.

*"...to tell you... about the baby."*

[break The baby]

It took an hour. The radio could not say anything quickly. It had to find each word somewhere in the air of Tokyo, on some station, in some song or advert or news bulletin, and wait for it to come round, and catch it. Sometimes it caught the wrong one and went back. Sometimes a whole minute went by in static while it searched. Rei sat on the floor and waited, and ate three rice crackers, and listened.

What it told him was this.

In March, in the maternity ward of the big hospital in Akabane, a baby had been born. A boy. Healthy. Normal weight. Normal everything. Except that the nurses on the night shift had started asking to be moved to days. And the cleaners would not go into that end of the ward after dark. And the boy's mother, a young woman from Jūjō, had stopped sleeping, because every night at exactly the same time her baby woke up, and lay in his cot with his eyes open, and looked at her.

Not the way a baby looks. The way an old man looks. Tired. Patient. Sad.

*"...the baby... was born... empty..."* said the radio, from a late-night talk show. *"...and someone... who was passing... went in..."*

"Like Mr. Satō," said Rei slowly. "The watch. Someone dying pulled a soul in."

Hiss. A different station. A weather forecast, very calm.

*"...no... not pulled... the other way... the house... was empty... and an old man... who had just died... in the same building... on the same night... walked in... to get out of the cold..."*

Rei thought about that.

"A soul without a body," he said. "And a body without a soul. On the same night. In the same hospital."

*"...yes..."* A pop song, very bright. *"...he didn't mean to... he's sorry... he can't get out... he doesn't know how..."*

"Does the baby... is there a baby? In there? As well?"

The needle stopped. For a long moment, only static, soft and grey.

Then, from somewhere very far away, a lullaby. An old one. A woman singing it on some programme for children, at the very edge of the dial, almost lost.

*"...not yet,"* said the radio. *"...there could be... if he leaves... soon..."*

Rei sat on the floor of the evidence archive and looked at the little orange light behind the tuning window for a long time.

"Who is he?" he said. "The old man."

Hiss. The needle went all the way across, slowly, from one end of the dial to the other, as if it were looking for something very particular. And stopped. And a voice came out of the speaker, a man's voice, an old recording, crackling, a programme about horse racing from a long time ago.

*"...Okamura... watches... and clocks..."*

Rei stopped breathing.

*The old woman outside the closed watch shop in Jūjō. Waiting in a grey hat for a husband who had gone out for cigarettes.*

"He came back," said Rei. "He came back and she wasn't..." He stopped. "No. She was waiting outside the shop. I sent her on. In August. With salt and anpan." His voice was strange. "And then he died. In March. And he couldn't find her. Because she'd gone. Because I sent her. So he went into the nearest warm thing."

The radio did not say anything for a while. The orange light glowed.

Then the needle moved, very gently, to a station playing a piano, slow and soft.

*"...not your fault,"* it said. *"...you didn't know... nobody knew... but you can tell him... where she went..."*

[break The other thing]

Before he left, Rei stood up and put the tin of rice crackers back under his arm, and then took it out again and left it on the shelf beside the radio, open, because it seemed right.

"Gin's book says to ask you something," he said. "About people. The ones who read as something other than what they are."

The needle did not move for a long time. The static hissed.

Then it moved, slowly, to a station Rei could not identify, where a woman was talking about pottery. About old bowls. About a way of mending them.

*"...broken..."* said the radio. *"...and mended... with gold..."*

The needle slid. A cooking programme.

*"...she sits... with the cats..."*

A long hiss. And then, from very far away, the end of a love song, a woman singing the last line alone with no music behind her at all.

*"...because she loved him..."*

The orange light went out.

Rei stood in the cold aisle of the evidence archive with the torch in his hand and turned the dial, and turned it again, and the radio did not come back on. He pushed the aerial down. He put a hand on the cold silver case, very lightly, the way you put a hand on the shoulder of someone who has fallen asleep in a chair.

"Thank you," he said. "I'll come back."

Upstairs, on the walk to the station, he thought about broken bowls mended with gold. He thought about someone who sat with the cats.

He thought about a kind woman in a grey cardigan with a mug in both hands, sitting under a high shelf beside a big dark cat, saying *nobody asks that in this building*.

He did not let himself think any further than that. Not yet. He was not sure, and Gin's book said, in a margin, underlined, *never accuse on a radio's word alone*.

But that night, at the kitchen table, when he wrote down what the radio had said in the back of Gin's book, in pencil, under his brother's handwriting, he wrote it very small.
