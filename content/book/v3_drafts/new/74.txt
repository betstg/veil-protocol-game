#title Guardian

The cordon was at the bottom of the road past Tabata, where the shops stopped and the warehouses began. Two police cars with their lights turning slowly. A line of orange cones. A sign on a stand that said GAS LEAK in red, and under it, smaller, *Please use alternative route. We apologise for the inconvenience.* And standing in front of it, in a summer uniform with her cap pushed back and her hands on her hips, Ono from the front desk of Kita police station, who looked at Shimizu's car as it pulled up and then looked at Rei in the back seat and broke into an enormous grin.

She bent and lifted the tape over the car roof with one hand. "*The monk*."

They drove through.

The road went down toward the river, and the streetlights went out one by one behind them, not because anyone had switched them off, but because they did not want to be on any more. The air in the car went cold. Shimizu's breath came out white. In the back seat, between Rei and Kato, Kuma put his head down on his paws and whined, very low, once.

"It's open," said Kato. His eyes were shut. His king was in his fist. "Not all the way. But it's started. I can hear it. Like a tap running in another room."

The sun was going down.

[break The gate]

Majima was at the gate.

He was standing exactly where he had stood the night before, by the broken chain-link with the bleached sign, with the old dog lying across the gateway. He had his dark clothes on and his hands open at his sides and his weight forward, and he did not turn round when the car stopped.

They got out. Shimizu and Kuma and Kato from her car. The others from Moriya's, which had followed them through the tape. Aoi, off her crutches, walking carefully with a canvas bag of seal papers over her shoulder and her face set. Moriya with a notebook in his top pocket. Sena, last, in a suit, exactly as if he were attending a hearing. Okada was already there, a little apart from Majima, with her three notebooks in their rubber band under her arm.

Mori came down the road on foot. He did not hurry. The air round him shimmered in the dusk like the air over a road in August, and the dry weeds along the verge went brown as he passed.

Rei walked up to the gate and stood beside Majima, and looked.

The yard had changed.

Last night there had been forty of them. Tonight there were thousands. They covered the yard from the fence to the river. They sat on the old tracks and stood among the weeds and crowded the open doors of every freight car, shoulder to shoulder, silent, still, with their faces turned toward the water and a white chrysanthemum in every hand. Thousands of them. More arriving every minute, through gaps in the fence, along the old lines, up out of the dark streets behind, walking slowly, without speaking, the way people walk in a funeral procession.

And above them, among them, through them, the white lights. Ten thousand of them. Twenty. Drifting toward the river like snow falling sideways. Every one with a small face in it, patient and puzzled and hopeful, coming home to the wrong house.

At the river's edge, where the old rails ran down into the water, the water was not water any more.

It was grey.

A grey beach, sloping down. A grey sea beyond it, flat and still, going away into a fog that had no far side. And on the beach, at the very edge of the old rails, a long low white room with no walls, and cushions, and a low table, and a woman kneeling at it in a soft white dress, pouring tea.

She did not look up.

She did not need to.

[break The meeting hour]

The sun went down behind the city.

The light went orange. Gold. Long. Every shadow in the yard three times the length of the person casting it. Thousands of long shadows lying across the weeds and the rails, all pointing the same way. Toward the river.

The meeting hour.

And the hollow ones stood up.

All of them. All at once. The way a flock of birds goes up off a field. They stood up from the rails and the weeds and the doors of the freight cars, and they turned toward the grey beach, and they began to walk. Slowly. Without speaking. Holding their flowers out in front of them like candles. And the white lights went with them, drifting down toward the water, ten thousand small faces turning toward the woman at the table, who looked up at last, and smiled, and held out her arms.

*Come,* she said. Not loud. Everywhere. In the air, in the weeds, in the ears of every living and dead thing in the yard. *Come and rest. You don't have to go home. Home is so far. And so cold. Come and sit down. I've made tea.*

Beside Rei, Majima took one step forward through the gate, and stopped.

His hand went flat over his jacket pocket. "Mika."

The girl in the pink hoodie was walking past them. Twenty metres away. Barefoot through the weeds, with her flower held in both hands and her face calm and blank and her eyes dark, toward the river.

Rei felt the latch on his wrist.

It was not cold. That was the first thing. All year it had been cold. Cold on the walkway at 3:47 with Gin's key in his fist. Cold in the Hotel Rosa. Cold on Tanizaki's step. Burning cold in the white room in Asagaya.

It was not cold now. It was warm. Warm the way the chair had been warm when he put his hand on it. Warm the way a door is warm when somebody on the other side has been leaning on it for a very long time, waiting for you to open it.

*You keep a door in your chest,* Sōun had said, in a training hall in Nezu, in September. *And you believe it was given to you so that you could sleep.*

*It was not.*

Rei lifted the latch.

[break All the way]

He had lifted it before. A finger's width, to read a form. Three quarters, in Asagaya. Half, in the Hotel Rosa, to see the black thing behind the waiting woman. Once, in Kyoto, by the river, all the way up, just for a moment, with Mio's hand on his.

He had never opened the door.

He opened it now. Not the latch. The door. The one under the latch. The one Gin had taught him to close at the kitchen table when he was eight, with a hand on his back, saying *like this, Rei, like a door, close it, close it, good*. The one he had kept shut, mostly, for eleven years, so that the things outside the kitchen window could not get in.

He opened it all the way. He took it off its hinges. He put it down.

And everything came in.

[break Six hands]

The first thing he felt was the yard.

All of it. At once. The way Shimizu felt a building. The structure. The load. Where it was strong and where it would break. He felt the thousands of hollow people as a single great weight, leaning toward the river like water leaning on a dam. He felt the far shore as a crack, a long grey crack running along the line where the rails went into the water. He felt exactly where the crack was thinnest, and where it was widest, and where, if someone pressed on it from the right side at the right moment, it would close.

*Clairsentience.* The hand that reads the structure of a thing. He had always had that one.

The second thing he felt was the people beside him.

Not their bodies. Their minds. Kato, terrified, holding his king so hard the edges cut his palm, thinking *Rei Rei Rei what are you doing your door's gone your door's gone completely I can hear*. Shimizu, very calm on the surface and screaming underneath. Aoi, praying, in a language Rei did not know she spoke. Majima, thinking nothing at all, only one word, over and over, like a heartbeat. *Mika. Mika. Mika.*

And Rei, for the first time in his life, thought back.

*I'm all right,* he thought, at Kato. Not loudly. Clearly. Placed. The way you place a cup in front of somebody. *I'm all right. Don't let go of me.*

Kato's head snapped round. His face went white, and then something else, something Rei had never seen on it, something like a man hearing music in an empty house.

"I heard that," he said. "Rei. *I heard that.* You said it into me. You've never..."

*Telepathy.* The hand that touches minds.

The third thing was heat.

It came up out of the ground under his feet, out of the old iron of the rails, into his hands. The iron filings in his jacket pockets. He put his hand in and closed it round them and they went hot, red-hot, the way iron goes in a forge, and then, as he thought it, cold, so cold they burned white with frost, the way a candle in a girl's hand had gone blue and then white in a training hall in Nezu.

*Psychokinesis.* The bluntest hand. Heat and cold and lightning.

The fourth thing was his own body. He felt it from the inside. His heart going too fast. His lungs. The blood in his ears. A hairline crack in the small bone of his left wrist under the latch, from a fall in the spring that he had never noticed. And Majima, beside him, Majima's right hand, the one that had held a ceiling, still bruised to the bone from the Hotel Rosa, still aching every time he made a fist. Rei put his hand on Majima's arm without thinking, and felt the bruise, and felt it go, the way a scrape on Itsuki's knuckle had gone once under an old woman's hands.

Majima looked down at his own hand. He flexed it. He looked at Rei.

*Psychometabolism.* The hand that reads the body. And mends it.

The fifth thing was weight.

Every object in the yard. Every freight car. Every rail. Every rusted wheel. He could feel what each of them weighed, and where it rested, and how it would move if he leaned on it. He looked at a freight car standing on its side twenty metres away, full of rust and weeds, and thought *up*, and it lifted. Not much. A hand's breadth. It hung there, groaning, rust raining off it like red snow, and came down again with a crash that shook the whole yard.

Majima made a sound. It might have been a laugh.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Seven? That was a seven."

*Telekinesis.* The hand that moves things.

And the sixth thing.

The sixth thing was distance.

Twenty metres away, a girl in a pink hoodie was walking toward the river. Twenty metres. Thirty, now. Forty. Through thousands of people. Through the weeds and the rails and the long shadows. Too far. Much too far to run.

Rei did not run.

He took one step.

And he was there.

Not across the yard. Not through the crowd. *There*. Standing in the weeds in front of her, between her and the river, with the long orange light behind him, as if he had always been standing there and the twenty metres had simply been folded up and put away, the way you fold a map.

*Teleportation.* The hand that opens doors. The Kuroda path. The rarest of the six.

The girl stopped. Her dark eyes looked at him without seeing him.

Rei looked at her river. Everything came in, and so he could see it, the way Mio had taught him. Under the black eyes, under the tea and the flower and the long walk, very small, very far down, like a stone at the bottom of a deep pool.

*Longing.* A girl who wanted somebody, anybody, to come and find her.

He did not touch her yet. "Mika. Somebody came."

[break What Mori said]

He did not remember the next part clearly.

He remembered putting his hand on her forehead. He remembered the black going out of her eyes like ink running out of a glass of water, and the white chrysanthemum falling out of her hands into the weeds, and her knees going. He remembered Majima catching her. Majima had crossed forty metres of crowded yard on foot in the time it had taken Rei to take one step, and he caught her before she hit the ground, and held her against his chest with the dog pressed against his legs and his face doing something that Rei never told anyone about afterwards.

He remembered looking up.

The whole yard had stopped.

Not the hollow ones. They were still walking, still holding out their flowers, still moving toward the grey beach. But the people at the gate had stopped. Shimizu with her hand on Kuma's collar. Kato with his mouth open. Aoi with a seal paper half out of her bag. Moriya with his pen in his hand and his notebook open and nothing written in it. Sena, very still, very pale, his measured face for once entirely without measure. Okada, whose three notebooks had slipped out of their rubber band and lay open at her feet in the weeds, and who did not pick them up, and was looking at Rei as if she had been watching for him for five years and had only just now found him.

And Mori.

Mori was standing in the gateway with his arms at his sides and the air round him no longer shimmering. It had gone completely still. As if all the heat in him had stopped to look.

He did not raise his voice. "Six."

His voice was very quiet. It carried right across the yard.

"All six. Every hand." He took a step through the gate. Another. "I told you. In March. At the round table. To hold a seat you have to be Guardian. All the way up. Every path open." He stopped. "I told you that you had a long way to go."

Rei stood in the weeds with the girl's chrysanthemum at his feet and his door off its hinges and everything, everything, coming in.

"I was wrong," said Mori. "You didn't have a long way to go." He looked at Rei with his coal-coloured eyes. "You had a door to take off."

Behind Rei, at the river, the woman in the soft white dress set down her teapot.

She was not smiling any more.

Nobody in the yard knew the whole of it. Not Mori, who had seen the six hands open. Not Okada, who had watched for five years. Not Kato, who had heard Rei speak inside his own head for the first time. They knew what they had seen. They did not know what had been underneath it all along, pressed flat beneath the door for eleven years, making a noise like a radio between two stations whenever anybody tried to read it.

Only one person in the city had ever known that.

And he was coming up the road from the south, in Gin's body, with Gin's jacket over his arm, at a walk, toward the cordon, where a policewoman with her cap pushed back looked at him, and went very pale, and lifted the tape.
