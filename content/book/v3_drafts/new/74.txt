#title Guardian

At midnight the sending fires went out.

Not all at once. One after another, all across the city, on every doorstep in Kita and Arakawa and Toshima and beyond, the little piles of hemp stalk in their clay dishes burned down to red and then to grey, and the families who had sat beside them went inside and shut their doors, and the dead who had followed the smoke went home.

Most of them.

From the road above the freight yard, Rei could feel it happening, like a tide going out across the whole city. And he could feel, at the same time, a great many small lights not going home. Turning, instead, one after another, toward the yard. Toward the brightness by the river.

Beside him at the gate, Majima had his hands open at his sides and his weight forward. Kinako lay across the gateway with her chin on her paws.

"It's open," said Kato behind them. His eyes were shut. His knight was in his fist. "Not all the way. But it's started. Like a tap running in another room."

[break The yard]

The yard had changed.

At ten there had been four hundred of them. Now there were thousands. They covered the yard from the fence to the river. They sat on the old tracks and stood among the weeds and crowded the open doors of every freight car, shoulder to shoulder, silent, with their faces turned toward the water and a white chrysanthemum in every hand. More arriving every minute, through gaps in the fence, along the old lines, out of the dark streets behind, walking slowly, without speaking, like a funeral procession.

And among them, through them, the white lights. Ten thousand. Twenty. Drifting toward the river like snow falling sideways. Every one with a small face in it, patient and puzzled and hopeful, going home by the wrong road.

At the river's edge, where the old rails ran down into the water, the water was not water any more.

It was grey.

A grey beach, sloping down. A grey sea beyond it, flat and still, going away into a fog that had no far side. And on the beach, at the very end of the old rails, a long low white room with no walls, and cushions, and a low table, and a small neat woman in a grey cardigan kneeling at it, pouring tea.

She did not look up.

[break The meeting hour]

The last of the fires went out across the river in Saitama, a small red point on a far balcony, and was gone.

And the hollow ones stood up.

All of them. All at once. Like a flock of birds going up off a field. They stood up from the rails and the weeds and the doors of the freight cars, and they turned toward the grey beach, and they began to walk. Slowly. Without speaking. Holding their flowers out in front of them like candles. And the white lights went with them, drifting down toward the water, ten thousand small faces turning toward the woman at the table, who looked up at last, and smiled, and held out her arms.

*Come,* she said. Not loud. Everywhere. In the air, in the weeds, in the ears of every living and dead thing in the yard. *Come and rest. You don't have to go all that way. It's so far. And so cold. Come and sit down. I've made tea.*

Beside Rei, Majima took one step forward through the gate, and stopped.

His hand went flat over his jacket pocket. "Mika."

The girl in the pink hoodie was walking past them. Twenty metres away. Barefoot through the weeds, with her flower held in both hands and her face calm and blank and her eyes dark, toward the river.

Rei felt the latch on his wrist.

It was not cold. That was the first thing. All year it had been cold. Cold on the walkway at a quarter to four with Gin's key in his fist. Cold in the Hotel Rosa. Cold on Tanizaki's step. Burning cold in the white room in Asagaya.

It was not cold now. It was warm. Warm like the empty chair when he put his hand on it. Warm like a door that somebody on the other side has been leaning on for a very long time, waiting for you to open it.

*You keep a door in your chest,* Sōun had said, in a training hall in Nezu, in September. *And you believe it was given to you so that you could sleep.*

*It was not.*

Rei lifted the latch.

[break All the way]

He had lifted it before. A finger's width, to read a form. Three quarters, in Asagaya. Half, in the Hotel Rosa, to see the black thing behind the waiting woman. All the way, in the round room this afternoon, with eleven hands on his back.

He had never opened the door.

He opened it now. Not the latch. The door. The one under the latch. The one Gin had taught him to close at the kitchen table when he was eight, with a hand on his back, saying *like this, Rei, like a door, close it, close it, good*. The one he had kept shut, mostly, for twelve years, so that the things outside the kitchen window could not get in.

He opened it all the way. He took it off its hinges. He put it down.

And everything came in.

[break Everything]

The first thing he felt was the yard.

All of it. At once. The structure. The load. Where it was strong and where it would break. He felt the thousands of hollow people as a single great weight, leaning toward the river like water leaning on a dam. He felt the far shore as a crack, a long grey crack running along the line where the rails went into the water. He felt exactly where the crack was thinnest, and where it was widest, and where, if enough of something went through it the right way at the right moment, it would close.

He had always had that. Since he was small. Since the faces on the stairs. It had only ever been a finger's width before. Now it was the whole yard, and the river, and the grey beach, and the fog beyond it, and something moving in the fog, a long way off, a great many somethings, coming closer, waiting for the doorway to clear.

The second thing he felt was the people beside him.

Not their bodies. Their minds. He had felt them before, a little, always, like voices through a wall. Now there was no wall. Kato, terrified, holding his knight so hard the edges cut his palm, thinking *Rei Rei Rei what are you doing your door's gone your door's gone completely I can hear*. Shimizu, very calm on the surface and screaming underneath. Usami, back by the van, with Ren wrapped round him like a coat, both of them shaking. Majima, thinking nothing at all, only one word, over and over, like a heartbeat. *Mika. Mika. Mika.*

And Rei, for the first time in his life, thought back.

*I'm all right,* he thought, at Kato. Not loudly. Clearly. Placed, like a cup set down in front of somebody. *I'm all right. Don't let go of me.*

Kato's head snapped round. His face went white, and then something else, something Rei had never seen on it, like a man hearing music in an empty house.

"I heard that," he said. "Rei. *I heard that.* You said it into me. You've never..."

He had never. He had heard Kōhei in his own mouth on a crossing in Higashi-Jūjō, and a frightened girl through a little green screen, and the man who had been a bus driver smiling at him through a window in the east corridor. He had always been the one listening. He had not known the door went both ways.

*A door is always open both ways,* the orange screen had said.

Twenty metres away, a girl in a pink hoodie was walking toward the river. Thirty, now. Forty. Through thousands of people. Too far to run.

Rei did not run.

He looked at her, across the yard, and looked at her river, the way Mio had taught him in Kyoto. Under the black eyes, under the tea and the flower and the long walk, very small, very far down, like a stone at the bottom of a deep pool. A girl of sixteen who wanted somebody, anybody, to come and find her.

And he spoke into it.

*Mika,* he thought. Not to the thing on top of her. To her. Down at the bottom of the pool. *Mika. Somebody came. He's at the gate. He's been at the gate all night.*

The girl stopped walking.

[break What Mori said]

He did not remember the next part clearly.

He remembered her turning round. He remembered the black going out of her eyes like ink running out of a glass of water, and the white chrysanthemum falling out of her hands into the weeds, and her knees going. He remembered Majima catching her. Majima had crossed forty metres of crowded yard on foot in the time it took her to turn round, and he caught her before she hit the ground, and held her against his chest with the dog pressed against his legs and his face doing something that Rei never told anyone about afterwards.

He remembered looking up.

The whole yard had stopped.

Not the hollow ones. They were still walking, still holding out their flowers, still moving toward the grey beach. But the people at the gate had stopped. Shimizu with her hand on the gatepost. Kato with his mouth open. Usami by the van with both hands over his mouth. Moriya on his crutch with his pen in his hand and his notebook open and nothing written in it. Sena, very still, very pale, his measured face for once entirely without measure. Okada, whose three notebooks had slipped out of their rubber band and lay open at her feet in the weeds, and who did not pick them up, and was looking at Rei as if she had been watching for him for five years and had only just now found him.

And Mori.

Mori was standing in the gateway with his arms at his sides and the air round him no longer shimmering. It had gone completely still. As if all the heat in him had stopped to look.

He did not raise his voice. "Guardian."

His voice was very quiet. It carried right across the yard.

"I told you. In the spring. At the round table. You don't get there by having more. You get there by going all the way down the path you've got, until there's no door left at the bottom." He took a step through the gate. Another. "I told you that you had a long way to go."

Rei stood in the gateway with his door off its hinges and everything, everything, coming in.

"I was wrong," said Mori. "You didn't have a long way to go." He looked at Rei with his coal-coloured eyes. "You had a door to take off. And you found out it opens outwards too."

Behind Rei, at the river, the woman in the soft white dress set down her teapot.

She was not smiling any more.

Nobody in the yard knew the whole of it. Not Mori. Not Okada, who had watched for five years. Not Kato, who had heard Rei speak inside his own head for the first time. They knew what they had seen. They did not know what had been underneath it all along, pressed flat beneath the door for twelve years, making a noise like a radio between two stations whenever anybody tried to read it.

Only one person in the city had ever known that.

And he was already in the yard. He had come in from the river end while everyone was looking at the gate, in Gin's body, in Gin's jacket with the sleeves pushed up, at a walk, between the hollow people, who did not see him. He was going toward the grey beach. He did not look back.
