#title The Temple Again

He meant to step back to the yard.

He stepped, and the world folded, and he came out somewhere else. On gravel. In the dark. Under a pine tree. With the smell of incense and old wood and something burnt, and a great dark shape rising up in front of him against the orange glow of the city.

A bell tower.

Wooden. Old. With a tiled roof and a long thick rope hanging down, and inside it, swinging very slightly although there was no wind at all, a bell. Green bronze. Big enough for a man to stand inside. Rei knew it. He had seen it come through the side wall of a temple hall in February like a fist through a paper bag. He had seen it swing across the end of the hall in a great low arc and catch Aoi's leg.

Jōkō-ji. Takinogawa. Halfway between his building and the river.

He had not meant to come here. He did not know why he had. He stood on the gravel with his nose bleeding and his ears ringing and his door off, and something had brought him here, the way something had brought him to the walkway. Something had asked.

"You came," said a voice behind him. "I wondered if someone would."

[break Egami]

Reverend Egami was sitting on the steps of the main hall in his dark robes, with his shaved head and his long gentle lined face, holding a can of beer in both hands.

He did not get up. He looked at Rei in the light from the hall. At the blood. At whatever was in Rei's eyes.

"Uedera-san," he said. "From the Division. You were the one who held your door shut." He looked at the can. "It's open now. All the way. I can see it from here."

Rei did not try to deny it. "Yes."

Egami tilted his head, the way a priest tilts his head at a confession. "Is that safe?"

"No." Rei wiped his face. "Reverend. Why am I here?"

Egami looked up at the bell tower.

"Because of that," he said. "It's been ringing on its own again. Since sunset. Very softly. Not like before. Not like when the thing was in me. Just a little. As if it were trying to remember how." He turned the can in his hands. "I haven't rung it since February. I couldn't. I'd come out every evening at dusk with the mallet and stand there, and I couldn't lift it. I kept thinking of the young woman's leg." He swallowed. "And of Shōta."

Rei came and sat down on the step below him.

"Tonight," said Egami, "at about seven, I felt something go past. North. A great many somethings. Coming home. And then turning. As if somebody had lit a lamp in the wrong window." He looked at Rei. "And then, a little later, turning back. Coming home after all. Through a door." A faint smile. "That was you."

Rei looked down at the gravel. "That was my friend. He painted it."

"Ah." Egami nodded slowly. "And the bell began to ring. On its own. The way it used to at the meeting hour, before. Only gently. As if it were asking." He held the can very still. "I thought, perhaps it's asking me."

[break The hour]

"It's the meeting hour," said Rei. "Still. It should have ended. The sun's gone. But she's holding it open. That's how the far shore stays open. As long as it's the meeting hour, the doors stay thin." He looked at the bell. "Somebody has to end it."

Egami was very quiet.

"There's a bell at dusk," he said at last. "In every temple. Every evening, for a thousand years. One stroke. The evening bell. It's not for prayer. People think it's for prayer. It isn't." He set the beer down on the step beside him, very carefully, the way Tanizaki used to set down a cup. "It's to say *the day is over*. To the living, so they go home. To the dead, so they go back. To whatever is standing at the edge of the evening, waiting for the light to go strange." He looked at the bell. "It's to end the meeting hour."

Rei turned his head toward the tower. "Then ring it."

Egami looked at his hands.

"I can't," he said. "I've tried. Every evening since February. I stand there and I think about the young woman, and the bell coming through the wall, and the thing that lived in me. And about Shōta. Seventeen. Hearing voices. Coming to me because I was the priest and I was supposed to know. And me thinking I was enough." His hands closed. "I let the thing in because I could not forgive myself. I let it go in February because a boy with his door shut told me to say Shōta's name. But I have never been able to lift that mallet again."

Rei looked at him for a long time.

He could see the priest's river. Everything was coming in, and so he could see it. Grief, old and deep and slow-moving, like a river in winter under ice. And at the bottom of it, very small, a seventeen-year-old boy sitting on a bench by the Shakujii river in October with his hands over his ears.

And something else.

Not in the priest. Behind him. In the dark. Up the steps of the main hall, by the door, where the candlelight did not quite reach.

Rei had all six hands and he did not know how to use most of them. But he knew how to read a room. He had always known how to read a room.

Someone was standing in the doorway of the main hall. Thin. Seventeen. In a school uniform. With his hands at his sides, not over his ears. Looking down at the priest on the steps with an expression of enormous, patient, puzzled kindness, the way you look at someone who has been waiting for you at a bus stop in the rain for a very long time without realising that you already came.

He had come home through Itsuki's door. Rei could feel it on him. The way you can feel rain on someone's coat. He had come home for Obon to the place he had last been looked after, and he had been standing in the doorway behind the priest since seven o'clock, waiting for him to turn round.

"Reverend," said Rei, very quietly. "Don't look round yet. Just listen."

Egami went still.

"He came home," said Rei. "Tonight. He's here. He's been standing behind you for an hour."

The priest did not move. Rei watched his hands on his knees. Watched them start to shake.

"He's not angry," said Rei. "He's not sad. He's not hearing the voices any more. He's just..." He looked at the boy in the doorway. At the boy's face. "He's just waiting for you to ring the bell. So he can go back. So it's properly over. So the day's done." He swallowed. "He came home so you'd know he was all right. And now he wants to go. And he can't, while the meeting hour's still open. Nobody can."

Egami closed his eyes.

He sat on the step with his eyes closed for a long time. The bell in the tower moved, very slightly, in no wind at all. Somewhere north, by the river, a woman in a soft white dress held the hour open with both hands.

Then Egami stood up.

He did not look round. He walked down the steps and across the gravel to the bell tower, slowly, an old man in dark robes with his shoulders very straight. He climbed the three wooden steps into the tower. He took hold of the rope that hung down from the long wooden striking beam.

His hands were shaking so badly that the rope shook.

Rei stood at the bottom of the tower steps.

He looked up at the old man in the tower. "I'm here. I've got the door. You ring."

He put his hand on the old wood of the tower post. Not to push. To lend. The way he had lent his Duty to a tired man on a walkway. He let it go out of him, through the open frame where his door had been, into the wood, into the beam, into the priest's shaking hands on the rope. Not strength. Not exactly. Only the feeling of someone standing behind you who will not let you fall.

Egami pulled back the beam.

And let it go.

[break The evening bell]

The sound went out across Takinogawa like a wave across a pond.

Low. Huge. Deep enough to feel in the teeth and the knees and the bottom of the stomach. It went out over the roofs and the narrow streets and the old houses, over the railway and the river, north, toward the old freight yard, rolling and rolling, not fading, the way a bell's note does not fade so much as spread, wider and wider, until it is everywhere and nowhere and you cannot tell any more if you are hearing it or remembering it.

*The day is over.*

Rei felt it reach the yard.

Four kilometres away, among the thousands of hollow ones walking toward the grey beach, he felt it arrive. Not as sound. As a hand on the shoulder. As someone saying your name across a crowded station. As the moment at the end of the day when the light goes and you realise you have been out too long and it is time to go home.

He felt them stop.

Not all of them. Not even most. But some. A woman with a handkerchief. A university student whose girlfriend's hand had gone slack in his. A young man in white who had knelt and set down a cup of tea in front of Rei once in Asagaya. They stopped walking. They stood in the weeds with their chrysanthemums in their hands and their heads lifted, listening, the way people stop in the street when the evening bell goes, and look up, and remember something.

And on the grey beach, the woman at the low white table flinched.

Rei felt the hour slip. Not close. Slip. The way a door you are holding open slips an inch when somebody bumps into you. The far shore went a little less grey. The fog on the grey sea thinned, very slightly, and through it, for a second, there was the real river, black and ordinary, with the lights of the city on it.

Then she caught it, and held it, and the grey came back.

But it had slipped.

[break Shōta]

When the sound had gone so far that it was only a feeling in the ground, Egami let go of the rope.

He stood in the bell tower with his hands hanging at his sides. He was crying. He did not wipe his face.

He did not turn round. "Is he still there?"

Rei looked at the doorway of the main hall.

The boy in the school uniform was still standing there. But he was standing differently now. Lighter. As if something he had been carrying had been taken off his back. He was looking at the bell tower. At the priest. He was smiling.

He lifted one hand. Not a wave. Just the fingers. The way you lift your fingers to someone across a room to say *I'm going now, don't get up*.

Then he turned round and walked into the dark of the main hall, past the gold Buddha in the candlelight, and did not come out again.

Rei let out a breath he had been holding. "He's gone. He said goodbye. He was smiling."

Egami stood in the bell tower for a long time.

Then he came down the three wooden steps and across the gravel and sat down on the steps of the main hall again, where he had been sitting before, and picked up his can of beer.

He looked at it.

He opened his mouth, and drank. The whole can. All of it. Straight down. And set the empty can on the step beside him.

"I've never finished one before," he said. "In twenty years." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "It's terrible beer. I should have bought better."

Rei laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt.

"Go," said Egami. "Go back. Whatever you're doing. I'll ring it again in an hour. And every hour after that until the sun comes up. Every temple in Kita should be ringing tonight." He reached into his robes and took out a mobile phone, an old one, a folding one. "I know eleven priests in this ward. Most of them owe me money." He opened the phone. "Go on."

Rei stood up.

He looked at the doorway of the main hall one more time. Empty. Candlelit. The gold Buddha sitting calmly in the dark.

Then he took one step, and the gravel and the pine and the bell tower folded away, and behind him, as he went, he heard the old priest's voice on the phone, cracked and hoarse and absolutely certain.

"Kōzen. It's Egami. Yes. Tonight. The evening bell. I know what time it is. Ring it anyway. Ring it every hour. Ring it till dawn." A pause. "Because the day is over, Kōzen. Somebody has to say so."
