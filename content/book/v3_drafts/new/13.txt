#title Small Machines

*The Healer.*

The Healer had never worn a body before. It had watched the living from the other side of the veil for longer than the city had had its name, as you might watch fish through the side of a tank. It had not known how many small things a body had to do. And it had not known that the world on this side had filled up, while it watched, with small machines.

At the end of August it stood in front of a ticket machine in Tabata station at eleven at night, in the borrowed body of Uedera Gin, and did not know what to press.

The machine was a wall of buttons and lights. Numbers. Station names. A little screen that changed every few seconds, showing a smiling cartoon train, then a list of prices, then a warning about pickpockets. People came up to the machines on either side, pressed three buttons without looking, took a small paper ticket and walked away. It took them four seconds.

The Healer had been standing there for nine minutes. A salaryman had come and gone at its elbow four times. One of them had been the same salaryman twice.

It had the body's memories. It knew, in the way you know a word in a language you speak badly, that you did not need the machine at all. That there was a small plastic card in the coat pocket that you touched to the gate. Gin had done it ten thousand times without thinking. But knowing a thing from inside someone else's memory is like knowing a city from a map. When the Healer reached for the knowledge, it found it, and then it found that the hand did not know how fast to move, or how hard to press, or what the beep meant. Every small machine asked it a question in a language it almost spoke.

In the end it walked to the gate and touched the card to the grey square, as the body remembered. The gate gave a sharp angry double beep and its little doors snapped shut against its legs. A red light flashed. The man behind it sighed the long, theatrical sigh of a Tokyo commuter who has been delayed by one and a half seconds and will be telling his wife about it.

The Healer looked at the closed gate for a long moment. It did not understand what it had done wrong. It did not understand why a small grey box had the right to refuse it.

A young station attendant in a blue cap came over and looked at the card and said something about *charge* and *balance* and pointed at the machine again. The Healer understood perhaps half. It nodded gravely and thanked him, very formally, with a deep bow from the waist, the way it had seen the living thank magistrates, long ago, through the glass. The attendant, who was twenty-two and had never been bowed to like that in his life, went pink to the ears and bowed back, and then did not know how to stop, and in the end walked away backwards.

[break Money]

Money was worse.

It understood debts. It had always understood debts. It was made of them. A debt was a weight between two people, held until it was paid. A bag of rice owed for a field ploughed. A life owed for a life saved. Heavy, real things.

But the money of the living was not heavy. It was paper. Small, light, coloured paper with old men's faces printed on it, and round bits of metal with holes in the middle. The Healer stood at the counter of a konbini with a bottle of tea that cost a hundred and thirty yen and a handful of coins in its palm, and could not make them add up.

It counted them. A large silver one. Several small brown ones. A gold one with a hole. Some tiny white ones, light as leaves. It laid them out on the counter in a row and moved them about, slowly, carefully, like stones in a game it did not know the rules of. The girl behind the counter waited. Behind the Healer, a queue began to form.

In the end it took a paper note out of the body's wallet. A large one. Ten thousand yen. It laid it on the counter.

The girl looked at the note. Then at the bottle of tea. Then at the Healer. She opened the till and counted out a great deal of change, papers and coins, nine thousand eight hundred and seventy yen, and put it in its hand, and said *thank you very much*, and stepped back.

The Healer stood holding a fistful of paper and metal.

*A promise,* it thought slowly. *All of this is only a promise.* A piece of paper that said *this is worth a thousand bowls of rice*. And everyone in the city simply agreed to believe it. Every day. Millions of people, handing each other promises printed on paper, and trusting that the next person would honour them.

It had thought the living were careless with their debts. It began to suspect they were something stranger. That they were so used to trusting each other that they had forgotten they were doing it.

The girl behind the counter was looking at it with an expression it could not read. It was the same expression people had made at it all week. A small pulling back. As if they had seen something in its face that they did not have a word for. It did not know that it was the eyes. That its pupils were always wide and black, even under the bright shop lights. That it looked at a girl at a till the way no tired man at eleven at night ever looks at anything. With all of its attention. Feeling everything in the room. Already wanting.

"Are you alright, sir?" she said.

"Yes," said the Healer, in Gin's voice. "Thank you. I am very well."

It smiled at her to reassure her. It turned up only the corners of the mouth, and did not touch the eyes, because it did not know that the eyes were supposed to come too. The girl stepped back from the counter until she touched the cigarette shelf.

[break Sorry]

Outside, on a crowded crossing near the station, a woman walking fast with her head down bumped into its shoulder.

"Ah, sorry, sorry," she said, bowing quickly, and was gone into the crowd.

The Healer stood still on the crossing while the lights changed and people flowed round it.

That, it understood at once.

She had touched it, and so she owed it something, a very small thing, and she had paid it immediately, with a word, before walking on. *Sorry.* A debt made and settled in one breath. It was exactly right. It was the most correct thing it had seen since it came into this body. The Healer felt something close to pleasure. Here at least was a rule it knew. Here at least the living behaved as they should.

It watched the crowd for a while, after that, from the corner of the crossing. It saw a man step on a child's foot and bow. It saw a cyclist brush a bag and call *excuse me* over his shoulder. It saw two old women at a bus stop bow to each other over and over, each apologising for being in the other's way. A whole city settling small debts all day long. It found this very satisfying. It thought perhaps it had misjudged the living after all.

[break The phone]

Inside the body, very deep down, the man was asleep.

The Healer could feel him there, like the weight of a sleeping cat on your legs in the night. Uedera Gin. It held him down gently, like a hand on the shoulder of a fevered patient. It was not cruel to him. It did not believe in cruelty. It kept the body warm and fed and rested. He had carried too much for too long. It was only right that someone else should carry it now.

It had not taken him by accident. A thing like the Healer cannot wear just anybody. It needs a body that already carries what it carries, so that the two fit together without a seam, and nothing is lost in the crossing. It had looked for a long time. Most people who carry duty carry it badly, out of fear, or for money, or because someone is watching. This one carried it as other men carried their own hands. Six years of other people's dead, and a younger brother, and an old man's teapot, and never once a complaint. And he was strong, stronger than he let anyone write down. And nobody had put anything round him to keep things out. And the veil where he walked had been worn thin by all that carrying, like a step worn thin by feet.

It had only had to wait for a night when he was tired. In August, in Obon week, there are a great many of those.

But twice this week he had woken.

Once in the kitchen, over the tea, when the phone buzzed. Once in the night, for one breath, when it walked past a train going by with a sky-blue stripe. Both times it had felt him surge up from the bottom of the body like a swimmer kicking toward the light. Both times it had been the younger one that woke him.

And once his finger had moved.

The Healer took the phone out of the coat pocket. It held it in both hands, flat, like a small heavy tablet of stone. The screen was black. It did not know how to make it light. It pressed the glass with its thumb. Nothing. It pressed harder. Nothing. It turned the phone over and looked at the back, and turned it over again. In the end its thumb found a small button on the side, and the screen lit up and asked for a number.

It knew the number. It was in the body's memory, a string of six digits, the younger one's birthday. Gin had changed it to that six years ago, on the first night of his new job, sitting on the edge of the bath at two in the morning, for a reason the Healer could find in the memory but could not understand. It pressed them, one at a time, very slowly, with one finger, like a man writing in a language he has only read. It got the fourth one wrong. The phone shook itself, angrily, a small cold refusal. It tried again.

When the screen opened, the message was there.

*I helped someone today. In Jūjō. With salt and anpan. I think I did it right.*

The Healer read it.

It had a debt with no one called Rei. There was nothing between them, owed or paid. It had no reason to read the message. It did not understand why it read it a second time. Or a third.

Somewhere up the river, something cold and hungry had begun to move through the city, sending its rats along the embankment to sit outside lonely doors and look. The Healer could feel it from here, a sick grey pull at the edge of everything, like a bad tooth under the tongue. It knew what that was. It was not its business. Not yet.

*First,* it told itself, *what is owed.*

It did not reply to the message. It did not know how. It turned the phone face down, carefully, and put it back in the pocket.

[break The umbrella]

It began to rain on the walk back.

The Healer had never been rained on. It stood still on the pavement under a dark sky and let it happen, interested, while the water ran down the body's face and into the collar and soaked the grey jacket through. It was cold. It was a great many small cold things, all at once, each of them a little different. It thought it could have stood there all night, learning them.

Around it the street bloomed. Umbrellas came up all at once, as if somebody had given an order. Clear plastic ones, black ones, a red one with white dots, a child's yellow one with a frog's face on top. Hundreds of them, in a few seconds, tilting and bumping and passing over each other's heads like the scales of something enormous and polite. The Healer watched them with great attention. It did not have one. It had not known that you were supposed to.

A man stopped beside it. An ordinary man in a white shirt, with a briefcase, about fifty, with the face of somebody who had worked too late and was going to miss the last train if he stood still. He looked at the Healer, soaked, standing in the rain with its arms by its sides and its face turned up. He did not say anything. He held out a clear plastic umbrella, the cheap kind they sell at every konbini for five hundred yen, and when the Healer did not take it, he pushed the handle into its hand and closed its fingers round it.

Then he put his briefcase over his head and ran for the station.

The Healer stood there with the umbrella in its hand.

It did not know his name. It did not know where he lived. It would never see him again. He had given it something, and walked away, and there was no way in the world to pay him back. A debt with no one at the other end of it. It felt it settle in the body's chest like a stone dropped into a well.

It did not like this at all.

It walked the rest of the way under the umbrella, holding it very straight, as if it were carrying something in a procession. When it got back to the small yellow kitchen it shut the umbrella and stood it in the sink, and looked at it, and the stone in its chest did not go anywhere.

For the first time since August, the Healer let the man come up.

Not all the way. Only enough. It was like letting a little water into a lock.

*Uedera Gin,* it said.

From the bottom of the body, slow and furious and thick with sleep, something answered. *What.*

*A man gave me this,* said the Healer. *I do not know his name. I will not see him again. How is it paid?*

There was a silence inside the body for so long that the Healer thought he had gone back down.

Then Gin laughed. It was not much of a laugh. It came up from very far away, through a great deal of dark water, and it hurt him to do it. But it was a laugh.

*It isn't,* he said. *You don't pay it back. You pass it on. Next time it rains you give it to some idiot standing in the street with his face up.*

The Healer considered this.

*That is not how debts work,* it said.

*It is here,* said Gin. And then, because he was a man who had never once in his life wasted a chance, even half drowned, even at the bottom of his own body, *Where's my brother.*

*Rest,* said the Healer, and pressed him gently down again, like a lid onto a pot.

But afterwards it stood in the dark kitchen for a long time, looking at the umbrella in the sink. And when it went out again the next night, it took the umbrella with it, though there was not a cloud in the sky.
