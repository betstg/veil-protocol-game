#title Small Machines

*The Healer.*

The Healer had not worn a body since it had one of its own, a very long time ago, before this city had trains or lights or its present name. Since then it had been on the other side of the veil. It had forgotten how many small things a body had to do. And it had not known that the world on this side had filled up, while it watched, with small machines.

In its first days it stood in front of a ticket machine in Tabata station at eleven at night, in the borrowed body of Uedera Gin, and did not know what to press.

The machine was a wall of buttons and lights. Numbers. Station names. A little screen that changed every few seconds, showing a smiling cartoon train, then a list of prices, then a warning about pickpockets. People came up to the machines on either side, pressed three buttons without looking, took a small paper ticket and walked away. It took them four seconds.

The Healer had been standing there for nine minutes. A salaryman had come and gone at its elbow four times. One of them had been the same salaryman twice.

It had the body's memories. It knew, in the way you know a word in a language you speak badly, that you did not need the machine at all. That there was a small plastic card in the coat pocket that you touched to the gate. Gin had done it ten thousand times without thinking. But knowing a thing from inside someone else's memory is like knowing a city from a map. When the Healer reached for the knowledge, it found it, and then it found that the hand did not know how fast to move, or how hard to press, or what the beep meant. Every small machine asked it a question in a language it almost spoke.

In the end it walked to the gate and touched the card to the grey square, as the body remembered. The gate gave a sharp angry double beep and its little doors snapped shut against its legs. A red light flashed. The man behind it sighed the long, theatrical sigh of a Tokyo commuter who has been delayed by one and a half seconds and will be telling his wife about it.

The Healer looked at the closed gate for a long moment. It did not understand what it had done wrong. It did not understand why a small grey box had the right to refuse it.

A young station attendant in a blue cap came over and looked at the card and said something about *charge* and *balance* and pointed at the machine again. The Healer understood perhaps half. It nodded gravely and thanked him, very formally, with a deep bow from the waist, as a man thanked a magistrate, in its day. The attendant, who was twenty-two and had never been bowed to like that in his life, went pink to the ears and bowed back, and then did not know how to stop, and in the end walked away backwards.

[break The body]

The body started to complain on the third day.

It began as a small hollow ache under the ribs. Then the ache grew teeth. Then, standing on the platform at Tabata, the body made a noise, a long low grinding noise from the middle of it, loud enough that a schoolgirl on the bench looked up from her phone.

The Healer stood very still and waited for it to happen again. It happened again.

Something was wrong with the body. It did not know what. It had kept the body upright and walking and breathing. It had not thought of anything else. And now the body was making noises, and its hands had begun to shake a little, and the edges of things had gone soft and grey.

It went down the platform steps and stood in a corner by the toilets, where nobody was, and for the first time since it had taken him, it let the man come up.

Not all the way. Only enough. Like letting a little water into a lock.

*Uedera Gin,* it said.

From the bottom of the body, slow and furious and thick with sleep, something answered. *What.*

*Something is wrong with the body. There is a pain here.* It put the body's hand on the body's stomach. *And it makes a noise.*

There was a silence inside, long enough that the Healer thought he had gone back down. Then something came up from the dark that was almost a laugh.

*You're hungry.*

*Hungry.* The Healer turned the word over. *I eat souls.*

*Then go and eat one,* said Gin. *And watch my body fall over. It doesn't run on souls. It runs on rice. When did you last feed it?*

The Healer looked through the body's memory for the answer, and found tea, and tea, and tea, and nothing else.

*How is it done,* it said.

*You buy food. Or you pick it off a tree, if you can find a tree in Tabata.* The almost-laugh again. *You buy it. With money.*

*There is no money in the coat.*

*No. I never carry much.* A pause, and the Healer felt him thinking, quick and sharp even from the bottom of the dark. *There's a card in the wallet. The blue one. You put it in the machine in the wall by the station exit, and you press four numbers, and the machine gives you money.*

*Which four numbers.*

The silence this time was longer. The Healer felt him decide. It felt the weight of it, a man handing a stranger the key to his house because the alternative was watching the house burn down.

Gin told it the four numbers.

[break The machine in the wall]

The machine in the wall had a screen and a slot and a keypad with a little hood over it. The Healer put the card in the slot the wrong way round, and the machine spat it out. It put it in the right way round, and the machine asked it a question in polite green letters.

*Withdrawal,* said Gin, inside. *That one. Top left.*

It pressed the four numbers, one at a time, slowly. The machine thought about it and asked how much.

*Ten thousand. Press one, then zero four times. Then the button that says yen.*

The machine whirred. A small door opened and there was a paper note in it, with an old man's face printed on it. The Healer took it out and looked at it for a long time.

*This is food?*

*That's the thing you swap for food.* Gin sounded very tired. *Don't ask me why. Nobody knows why. Put the card away. Put the money away. Go up the street and turn left. There's a ramen shop with a red curtain. Old man called Hosoda. I've eaten there a hundred times.*

[break Hosoda]

The ramen shop was one narrow room with a counter and eight stools and steam on the windows. At the door there was another machine. This one had rows of buttons with small pictures on them and writing under each picture, and a slot for the money.

The Healer stood in front of it.

It could read. It had always been able to read. But the writing on the buttons was not quite the writing it knew. Some of the characters were simpler than they should be, cut down, as if somebody had been in a hurry. Some were not characters at all, but the round sound-letters, spelling out words it had never heard. *Tonkotsu.* *Chāshū.* *Ajitama.* It knew every sound and not one meaning.

*What is this,* it said. *What are these things. In my day food had names.*

*It still has names. They've just changed.* Gin was more awake now. The Healer could feel him leaning forward, as much as a man can lean at the bottom of a well. *Top row is the soup. Shōyu is soy sauce, clear and brown. Miso is fermented bean, it's thicker. Tonkotsu is pork bone, it's white and heavy and it'll put you to sleep. Chāshū is sliced pork. Ajitama is an egg soaked in soy, soft in the middle.*

*Which is right.*

*There's no right. What do you want?*

The Healer considered this. It had not been asked what it wanted for a very long time. It did not know.

*You choose,* it said.

*Shōyu,* said Gin at once. *With an egg. And tell him less oil, he always overdoes it.*

It put the note in the machine and pressed the button for shōyu, and the button for the egg, and the machine gave it two small paper tickets and a great deal of change. It went in and sat at the counter and put the tickets down. The old man behind the counter, Hosoda, looked up at it from the steam.

He looked for one second too long. Something in his face went still, the way a dog goes still when somebody walks past the gate who smells wrong. Then he took the tickets.

"Less oil," said the Healer, in Gin's voice.

"I know," said Hosoda. "I know how you have it." He turned away to the pots. He did not look at it again.

The bowl came. Brown clear soup with a shine on it, and noodles folded under, and a slice of pork, and half an egg with a yolk the colour of a sunset, and a sheet of dark seaweed standing up at the back. Steam came off it and went into the body's face, and the body's stomach made its noise again, very loudly, and Hosoda's shoulders moved as if he might have laughed if he had not been so uneasy.

The Healer picked up the chopsticks. The hand knew how. It let the hand do it.

It ate.

It had forgotten. In all the long time behind the veil it had forgotten that a mouth can do this. Salt. Heat. Something deep and dark and savoury at the bottom of the soup, like the smell of the sea turned into a taste. The noodles slippery and firm. The egg soft in the middle, exactly as he had said. Each thing was a small surprise, and then the next one came before the last one had finished.

It ate the whole bowl. It drank the soup from the bowl with both hands. When it put the bowl down the body's hands had stopped shaking and the edges of the room had come back sharp.

*Well?* said Gin, from the bottom of the dark.

The Healer looked at the empty bowl.

*Is it good?* it asked. *Is this what good is?*

There was a silence.

*Yeah,* said Gin. And there was something in his voice that the Healer could not name, small and sore. *Yeah. That's good. That's what good is.*

The Healer sat at the counter with its hands round the warm empty bowl.

*Where's my brother,* said Gin.

*I do not know,* said the Healer. *He is not my concern.* And it pressed him down again. But not as hard as before. It had found out that it would need him. For the machine in the wall, and the names of food, and whatever other small things were waiting out there in this changed world to ask it questions it could not answer.

It did not like that at all.

[break The coat]

Inside the body, very deep down, the man went back to sleep.

The Healer could feel him there, like a coat it had not taken off. It did not think about him much. He was what came with the body. It kept him down because he was in the way.

It had not taken him by accident. A thing like the Healer cannot wear just anybody. It needs a body that already carries what it carries, the same thing, so that the two fit together without a seam and nothing is lost in the crossing. Wear the wrong body and you come out of it weaker than you went in. It had looked for a long time. This one fitted. He was strong, stronger than he let anyone write down. Nobody had put anything round him to keep things out. And the veil where he walked had worn thin around him, from six years of walking into other people's worst rooms.

It had only had to wait for a night when he was tired. In August, in Obon week, there are a great many of those.

In the coat pocket there was a phone. The Healer took it out and held it in both hands, flat, like a small heavy tablet of stone. The screen was black. It pressed the glass. Nothing. It found a small button on the side and held it, and the screen showed a picture of an empty battery for one second and went black again.

It did not know what that meant, and it did not ask. It put the phone back in the pocket and walked out into the night with a full stomach, which it was beginning to understand was a good thing to have.
