#title Haruki

On the page in Gin's book for Tsukishiro Haruki there was no address. There were only six words. *Jazz bar. Thursday to Saturday evenings.* Under them Gin had drawn a tiny coffee cup, and next to the cup a musical note, and next to the note a small arrow pointing at the name of a station. Nezu.

Nezu is an old neighbourhood in Bunkyō, the ward next to Ueno. It is one of the few parts of Tokyo that did not burn in the war, so it still has narrow lanes of wooden houses with potted plants crowding every doorstep, and old shops that sell rice crackers and paper and brooms, and a big shrine with a long tunnel of red gates. On Friday evening Rei came up out of the subway into warm air that smelled of grilled fish and mosquito coils. The lanes were full of families walking slowly in the dusk, and old men in white undershirts sitting outside their front doors on folding chairs, fanning themselves, and a boy somewhere behind an open window practising the trumpet very, very badly.

Itsuki's family lived in Nezu. Rei had been past the high wall of the Kuroda house a hundred times on the way to somewhere else, and never once been inside. He walked the other way.

He walked the lanes for forty minutes before he heard the piano.

It was coming up out of a basement under a shop that sold wooden combs. A narrow staircase went down from the street. At the bottom was a heavy wooden door with a small brass plate on it. No name. Just a crescent moon, engraved. Rei put his hand on the door, and remembered Tanizaki's open door and the third cup he had not been given, and stood there for a moment and breathed. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six.

Then he pushed it open.

The bar was tiny. Eight stools along a wooden counter, three little tables, and a piano in the corner with a man of about seventy playing it with his eyes shut. The walls were covered with old record sleeves, faded jazz musicians in black and white, holding trumpets and cigarettes. The air was cool and dark and smelled of whisky and old wood and coffee. Very good coffee. Rei had never smelled coffee like it in his life.

Behind the counter a young man was making it.

He was about thirty. He had dark hair that fell untidily across his forehead and a lean, handsome, sleepy face, the face of somebody who has just woken up from a nap and is pleased about it. He wore a thin dark knitted jumper with the sleeves pushed up to the elbows, even in August. He was pouring hot water from a kettle with a long thin swan's-neck spout over a paper filter, in slow, careful circles, with complete attention, as if nothing in the world mattered except the next circle.

He did not look up. "Sit anywhere," he said. "One minute. You can't hurry this. I've tried. It knows."

Rei sat at the counter. The man finished pouring. He stood and watched the coffee drip through the paper for what felt like a very long time, with his head a little on one side, listening to it. Then he poured it into a white cup and carried it down to an old woman at the far end of the counter, who smiled up at him the way grandmothers smile at their favourite grandson. Then he came back and turned to Rei.

And stopped.

The sleepy face went very still. He looked at Rei for a long moment. At his face. At his eyes. And then, exactly the way Okada had, down at Rei's right hand, which had gone into his trouser pocket again without asking and closed round the key.

"Oh," said the man quietly. "Oh, no."

Rei had rehearsed this on the train. He had rehearsed it twice. He took a breath, and did not say any of what he had rehearsed.

"Good evening," he said instead. "Sorry to come in like this. How are you?"

The man blinked at him.

For a second he looked almost startled, the way you look when somebody says a line from a song you have not heard in years. Then something in his face gave way, very slightly, and he put both hands flat on the counter and leaned on them.

"Honestly?" he said. "Terrible. I've been terrible for nine days. Thank you for asking." He looked at Rei's face, all of it, slowly, as if he were reading it. "You're Rei. You've got his jaw. He always said you didn't, and he was wrong. I told him so." He took a breath. "He hasn't come in. He comes every Thursday. Every single Thursday for three years. He sits on that stool, he drinks two cups, he complains about the Division, he goes home. Last Thursday he didn't come. Last night he didn't come. I've called him I don't know how many times." His eyes went down to Rei's pocket. "That's his key in there, isn't it."

Rei did not trust his voice. He nodded.

The man stood very still behind his counter. Then he turned away, took a clean white cup down from a shelf, and began to make another coffee. He did it exactly as slowly as before. The kettle. The circles. The waiting. His hands were perfectly steady. His face, in the mirror behind the bottles, was not.

"I'm going to make you a coffee," he said, with his back to Rei. "And while I make it, I'm going to be very upset in private. Then I'll turn round and we'll talk properly. Is that alright with you?"

"Yes," said Rei.

The old man at the piano played something slow and sad and full of rain. The old woman at the end of the counter sipped her coffee and pretended, very kindly, not to be listening. Haruki made the coffee. When he turned round and put the cup down in front of Rei, his eyes were red at the edges, but he was smiling again. A small crooked smile, held up with both hands.

"Drink it while it's hot," he said. "It's the only thing I'm actually good at."

Rei drank it. It was the best coffee he had ever tasted. Strong and clean, with a faint taste of dark chocolate, and something underneath like cherries. For a few seconds he forgot to be afraid.

[break Best friend]

"How did you know him?" Rei asked.

"2012." Haruki leaned on the counter on his forearms. "A case in Kita. A house by the river where a whole family kept getting sick and nobody could work out why. I was there for my reasons, he was there for the Division's. Four days in that house together. On the third night we were sitting in the kitchen at three in the morning, both scared out of our minds, both pretending we weren't, and he turned to me and said, very politely, *Do you have any food.* And I had a box of chocolate biscuits in my coat pocket. And we ate the whole box, sitting on the floor, not talking." He smiled at the memory. "After that we were friends. Best friends, I think. He'd never have said that. He'd have said *colleague*. Or *a contact*. But he came here every Thursday for three years. You don't do that for a contact."

His hand went to his chest, to the inside pocket of the jumper, and rested there for a second, as if checking that something was still there. Then it came away.

"He talked about you," said Haruki. "All the time. Rei's exam. Rei's terrible cooking. Rei fell asleep on the sofa with his shoes on again. Rei notices everything and doesn't know he does. He was so proud of you it was embarrassing. I used to tell him to stop. He never stopped."

"He never told me about you."

"No." Haruki's smile went thin. "He never told anybody about anybody. He kept every part of his life in a separate box with a separate lock. I think he thought it kept us safe. All of us. You most of all."

"Did it?"

Haruki did not answer that. He looked at the piano player instead, for a while.

"My family is the Tsukishiro," he said at last. "Have you heard of us?"

"One of the Tower Families."

"The oldest one. We've been doing this since before Kyoto was the capital. Big old house in Bunkyō with a lot of rooms and a lot of rules and a lot of things in the basement that nobody talks about at dinner. I have one wing of it to myself, which sounds very grand and mostly means I have my own personal bad heating." He shrugged. "I don't work for the family. I don't work for the Division. I don't really work for the Grid either. I work in between. I find things out. People pay me for that, sometimes. Mostly I make coffee." He tapped the wooden counter with one knuckle. "This bar belongs to an old friend. He lets me stand behind it three nights a week. It's the only place in Tokyo where nobody asks me which side I'm on."

"What's the Grid?"

Haruki raised his eyebrows. "Wow. He really didn't tell you anything." He thought about how to say it. "The Grid is everybody who can do what you can do and isn't on the Division's list. People who never registered. People who registered and then walked out. People who don't trust the government to write down what they are." He looked at Rei over the rim of his own cup. "People like you, right now, sitting there."

Rei told him everything.

The key on the walkway at 3:47. Gin's book. The young man in the lobby who would not touch the key. Kumagai and Ono. The man on the platform at Ōji with his face being worn from the inside like a glove. Okada and her card. Tanizaki and his two cups of tea. The old woman in the arcade at Jūjō, checking her empty wrist. He had not said any of it out loud to anybody except Itsuki. Once he started, he could not stop, and Haruki did not try to stop him. He listened without interrupting once. He made two more coffees while Rei talked, one for Rei and one for himself, and he never drank his.

When Rei got to Tanizaki, Haruki laughed out loud for the first time. A real laugh, surprised out of him.

"Two cups," he said. "He gave you two cups and sent you home."

"He told me to come back when I'd finished being in a hurry."

"He said exactly that to Gin when Gin was nine." Haruki was still smiling. "And to me when I was twenty-five. It took me three visits to get the third cup. It took your brother, I think, about two years."

"I haven't got two years."

"No." The smile went away. "No, you haven't." He picked up his own cup, looked down at the cold coffee in it, and put it back. "Okay. Here's what I can do, and here's what I can't. I can't find Gin tonight. I've been trying for nine days. Whatever has him is quiet, and it's careful, and it isn't leaving any marks I know how to read. I can't make the Division tell you the truth. And I can't teach you what Tanizaki teaches. Nobody can. That's his."

"Then what can you do?"

"I can walk with you." Haruki said it simply, the way you say what time it is. "Wherever you go next, I can come too. I know the Grid. I know the families. I know which doors in this city are safe to knock on and which ones you only knock on once in your life. I know how to keep you alive while you learn." He glanced down at Rei's pocket. "And I knew your brother. Better than almost anyone. That has to be worth something, when you're looking for him."

Rei looked at him. At the sleepy face that was not sleepy at all now. At the red edges of his eyes, and the small crooked smile that was trying very hard to stay where it was.

"Why?" he said. "You don't even know me."

"I know you a little," said Haruki. "He talked about you every Thursday for three years." He shrugged one shoulder. "And I still owe him a box of chocolate biscuits."

[break The konbini]

It was after one in the morning when they came up the stairs into the street. Nezu was asleep. The lanes were dark except for a vending machine humming red and white on the corner, and at the end of the street the lights of a konbini, white and flat and very bright.

"Come on," said Haruki. "Lesson one."

He walked Rei into the konbini. The door chimed. A sleepy young clerk said *Irasshaimase* without lifting his eyes from his phone. After the dark bar the light was so bright that Rei squinted. The air conditioning was freezing. A pop song played very quietly from somewhere up in the ceiling, a girl singing about summer.

Haruki bought two hot cans of corn soup from the warming cabinet and a bag of fried chicken from the glass case by the till. He handed Rei a can and a piece of chicken and stood him at the counter by the window, facing the empty street, and made him eat.

"Here?" said Rei.

"Here. Exactly here." Haruki pulled the ring on his can. "Your brother wrote about this in the book. Have you got to it yet? He called it the Konbini Protocol. He was ridiculously proud of the name."

Rei shook his head.

"The veil is thin where big things happen to people," said Haruki. "Deaths. Fights. Love. Grief. Old houses, hospitals, stations, shrines. Places soaked in feeling. And what does a konbini have?" He waved his chicken at the shelves of instant noodles and toothpaste and magazines and umbrellas. "Nothing. Nothing ever happens in a konbini. That's the whole point of a konbini. Bright lights. Cold air. A clerk who doesn't care if you live or die. Some guy behind you trying to choose between two rice balls. It's the most ordinary place in the world. And ordinary is the strongest medicine there is, for people like us."

Rei drank his soup. It was too sweet and too hot and it burned his tongue, again. Behind him a man in a construction worker's uniform was standing in front of the rice balls, reading every single label, very slowly, as if it were the biggest decision of his life.

And Rei felt it. Slowly, the way warmth comes back into cold hands. The scraped-out, hollow feeling he had carried around since the arcade in Jūjō began to fill up. The pressure behind his eyes, which had been sitting there faintly for a whole week without his even noticing, went quiet. The floor felt like a floor. The world felt like a place where nothing would ever be left on a walkway at 3:47.

"Oh," he said.

"Yeah." Haruki gave him the crooked smile. "When you help a spirit, you spend yourself. That's what you felt in Jūjō. The legs, the hands, the tunnel in your eyes. That comes back with sleep and food. But there's something else that goes down, slower, deeper, every time you open that door in your chest. The thing that keeps you being you. Gin called it stability. When it gets low, things can get in." He knocked his can gently against Rei's. "This fills it back up. A hot can of soup at one in the morning under terrible lights. Your brother came into a konbini every night of his life after a bad case. I used to find him in the one by Ōji station, eating oden at the window, staring at the magazine rack like it was a fire in a fireplace."

They stood at the window and ate fried chicken and watched the empty street. The man behind them finally chose a salmon rice ball. Then he put it back and chose a tuna one. Then he stood there looking at the salmon one with a face full of regret.

Rei laughed. It just came out.

"Same time next Friday?" said Haruki.

"Yes," said Rei. And then, because it was true, "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the corn soup." Haruki crushed his empty can in one hand and dropped it in the bin. "And go back to Tanizaki. Keep going back. Sit there and drink his tea and don't ask him for anything. It'll drive you completely crazy. Do it anyway."

The last train had gone hours ago. Rei walked to Ueno and took a taxi home, and watched the meter climb past three thousand yen with a sick feeling in his stomach, because he did not know any more how long his money had to last. He slept for nine hours without dreaming. When he woke up it was raining for the first time in two weeks, and there was one message on his phone, from a number he did not know.

*Tuesday. Café Asahi. Ni-chōme. After seven. Ask for the usual. H.*
