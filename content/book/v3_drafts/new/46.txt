#title Two Cups

*Gin.*

This time he woke slowly, like a man coming up from the bottom of a deep pool, and the voice was waiting for him.

*You're awake,* it said. Not *you should not be awake*. Just *you're awake*. As if it had been expecting him.

He was sitting at the table in the small yellow kitchen with the frosted window. The dry-cleaning calendar on the nail said April now. Somebody had turned the pages. It was night, and it was raining, and the kettle was on the gas ring, starting to hum.

His hands were on the table. He looked at them. He tried to move a finger.

It moved.

Not much. Not easily. Like moving through cold honey. But it moved, and the voice did not stop it.

*I am letting you,* said the voice. *A little. For a while.* A pause. *I wish to talk.*

[break The talk]

*Talk,* said Gin. His thought came out rough and slow, like a voice after a long illness. *You want to talk. After the road.*

*Yes. After the road.* The voice was quiet. *I have been thinking about the road. About what you said. You said I do not understand anything.* Another pause. *You were right. I wish to understand.*

The kettle began to whistle. Gin's hands got up, by themselves, and went to the stove. He let them. He was too tired to fight over a kettle. They turned off the gas. They lifted the kettle. They poured the water into the small clay teapot, slowly, the right way, the way Tanizaki taught him.

They took down one cup from the shelf.

*Two,* said Gin.

The hands stopped.

*Two cups,* said Gin. *Every night. For nineteen years. Even when he wasn't there. You've been pouring one.* He was so tired. *If you want to understand something, start with that.*

The hands stood still at the shelf for a long time.

Then, slowly, the right hand reached up and took down a second cup. It put it on the table beside the first. It poured tea into both. Two cups, steaming, side by side, on the small table in the yellow kitchen in the rain.

The body sat down in front of them.

*Why?* said the voice. *Why two? When there is no one to drink the second?*

*Because one day there will be,* said Gin. *That's what it means. It's not for him. It's for the day he comes home.*

The voice was silent.

*You understand debts,* said Gin. *Everything's a debt to you. What's owed. What's paid.* He looked at the two cups through his own eyes. *The second cup isn't a debt. Nobody owes it. Nobody pays it. It's just a promise you make to yourself that you won't give up.*

*A promise,* said the voice. Very carefully. As if the word were hot. *A promise to whom?*

*To nobody. To the morning.*

*That is not possible. A promise must be to someone. A promise is a chain between two.*

*Not for us,* said Gin. *We make them all the time. To nobody. To ourselves. To people who'll never know. And we break half of them. And we make them again the next day.* He felt, very far away, a kind of laugh, his own. *You'd hate it. It's very untidy.*

[break What it wanted]

They sat with the two cups for a long time. The rain went on.

*Tell me what you want,* said Gin at last. *You've been in me for eight months. You've kept me warm and fed and you've stood on a kerb and watched a boy bleed to death. You keep saying *what is owed*. Owed by who? To who?*

The voice was silent for so long that Gin thought it would not answer.

Then it said, *There is an old thing in the city.*

Gin waited.

*Very old. Older than me, almost. It feeds on despair. It sits in a white room, inside a kind woman, and pours tea for frightened people, and drinks them.* The voice was very calm and very cold. *Four hundred years ago, it and I made an arrangement. We were young then. Hungry. We were both of the other side, and we both wanted the same thing. The living world. Its warmth. Its weight. We agreed to help each other reach it.* A pause. *It broke the arrangement.*

*I thought demons never break their word.*

*We do not.* The voice was flat. *It found a way to keep every word of it, and still betray me. That is what the clever ones do. They keep the letter. They poison the meaning.* Another pause. *It left me on the far side, alone, for four hundred years. Holding what was owed. And it went on, into the living world, and ate, and grew.*

Gin felt something in the voice that he had never felt in it before. Not anger. Something colder and older and much more patient than anger.

*And now it wants a chair,* said the voice. *The chair of Despair. It is eating the man who holds it, very slowly, from far away. When the man dies, it will take his chair. And then it will open the door between the two sides, and pour the far side into the near.* The voice was quiet. *It wants the living world. I do not. Not any more. I have had four hundred years to think about what I want.*

*What do you want?*

*The far side,* said the voice simply. *To rule it. With those like me. Quietly. In order. Each in its place. As it should be.* A pause. *And I want the old thing dead. That is what is owed. That is the debt. Four hundred years of it.*

*And you need my body for that.*

*Yes.* The voice did not apologise. *I cannot reach it from the far side. It is too well hidden in the woman. I need hands on this side. Strong hands. Hands that know how to fight things like it.* A pause. *Yours were the best I could find. You were close to the shape. Duty. You were looking for the dark chair. You opened the door yourself.*

Gin looked at his hands on the table, either side of the two cups.

*You could have asked,* he said.

*Asked?*

*Asked. Instead of taking. You could have come to my door and knocked and said, there's an old thing eating people, I need your help to kill it.* Gin's thought was very tired. *I'd have said yes. That's my whole job. That's all I've done for six years. Kill old things that eat people.*

The voice was silent for a very long time.

*It did not occur to me,* it said at last. Slowly. Like a man discovering a door in a wall he has walked past every day for years. *It did not occur to me to ask.*

[break The deal]

*Here's what I want,* said Gin. *Since we're talking.*

*Tell me.*

*No more roads. No more standing on a kerb. If someone's dying in front of me, and I can help, you let me help.* He felt his own will, thin and stubborn, pressing against the warm heavy weight that held him. *And you don't touch my brother. Ever. Whatever happens with your old thing. You don't touch Rei.*

*You want a promise.*

*I want a promise.*

The voice did not answer for a long time. Gin felt it turning the word over. *Promise.* Feeling the weight of it. The chain.

*I will not promise,* it said at last.

Gin's heart sank.

*I cannot,* said the voice. *Not because I do not wish to. Because I do not know what will happen. If I promise not to touch your brother, and the day comes when touching him is the only way to keep him alive, I will have to stand on the kerb and watch him die, because I promised. To the letter.* It was very quiet. *That is what a promise means, for us. I will not make one I might have to keep like that.*

Gin was silent.

*But I will tell you this,* said the voice. *Not as a promise. As a thing that is true now, tonight. I have watched your brother for eight months. In a station. Outside a building in a cheap suit. On the telephone, in messages I read three times.* A long pause. *I do not wish him harmed. I do not understand why. But I do not.*

*That's not a promise.*

*No.* The voice almost sounded amused. *It is very untidy. You said you would hate it.*

Gin felt something then that he had not felt in eight months, inside his own body. Something small and warm and painful, deep in his chest. It took him a long moment to recognise it.

Hope.

*Drink your tea,* said the voice. *Both of them. It is getting cold.*

His hands lifted the first cup. He drank. Then the second. It was the same tea. It tasted different.

The dark came up slowly after that, like warm water, the way it always did. But this time, before it closed over him, he felt the voice do something it had never done.

It washed both cups. And dried them. And put them back on the shelf, side by side.

Two.
