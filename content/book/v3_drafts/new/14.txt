#title Kato

The email came three days after Akabane.

*Family restaurant by Itabashi station. The one with the green sign. Thursday, 11pm. Drink bar's on me. Okada says hi. By which I mean Okada said nothing at all, but she told me where you'd been on Sunday night, which for Okada is basically a hug. K.*

Rei read it twice at the kitchen table. Then he turned to the back of Gin's book, to the pages under the heading *The Ones Worth Knowing*, and read the entry again, though he knew it by now.

*Smarter than he presents, which he knows, which makes the performance slightly exhausting. Knight piece, jacket pocket. Will get you what you need through exactly the channels he pretends he doesn't use. Useful. Not warm. Has said approximately four genuine things to me in two years and they were all correct.*

*I'll keep my eyes open,* Kato had written, four minutes after Rei's email, on the first day. *If I find anything, I'll tell you.* There had been nothing since. Not one line.

Rei looked at the hook by the door, where nothing hung, and said out loud to the empty flat, "Useful, not warm," and the empty flat, as usual, had no opinion.

[break The green sign]

Itabashi is the ward west of Kita. It is mostly flats and small factories and long straight roads that go on for a long time without anything happening on them. The family restaurant sat on a corner by the station under a big green sign, its tall windows full of yellow light. Family restaurants in Japan stay open all night. They have enormous menus with a photograph of everything, from spaghetti with cod roe to pork rice bowls to chocolate parfaits taller than a child, and a machine called the drink bar where you pay once and refill your glass as many times as your bladder will allow. At eleven at night they fill up with students cramming, taxi drivers eating with their elbows out, and people who have nowhere they want to go home to.

[need ch14_famires | A Japanese family restaurant at 11pm, big windows, booths, students studying, a trim young man in a neat dark jacket in a corner booth turning a small ivory chess knight in his fingers, two glasses of melon soda from the drink bar, Rei sliding in opposite]

He saw Kato Ryusei at once.

He was alone in a corner booth with his back to the wall, where he could see the whole room and the door. About twenty-four. Trim, neat, very still, in a dark jacket with the sleeves pushed to the elbow and a plain white T-shirt under it. Short tidy hair. Two tall glasses of fluorescent green melon soda sweating on the table in front of him. And in his right hand, turning slowly over and over between his fingers, a small ivory chess piece. A knight, the horse's head worn smooth at the ears.

He did not look up when Rei came in. While Rei was still five metres away, threading between tables, he said, "Yes. I'm the one in the book."

Rei stopped dead.

"Sit down," said Kato. "You're blocking the waitress. She's about to ask if you'd like to see the dessert menu. You wouldn't. You'd like the cheese hamburg steak with rice, but you're going to order fries, because you're doing sums about rent in your head." He pushed one of the green glasses across the table with one finger. "I got you a melon soda. Everybody likes melon soda. It's the only thing the human race has ever agreed on."

Rei sat. He sat very straight, the way he used to sit in front of Kumagai, hands on his knees under the table.

The waitress arrived at his elbow. "Good evening, would you like to see our dessert..."

"No. Thank you." Rei cleared his throat. "Just fries, please."

The waitress went. Kato turned the knight between his fingers and did not smile. Quite.

"Excuse me," said Rei, carefully. "How did you know that?"

"Excuse me," Kato repeated, in Rei's exact voice, and then looked a bit sorry he had. "You were thinking it. Loudly. You think very loudly, you know. Like someone talking on the phone on a train." He stood the knight up on the table between the two glasses. "That's the first thing to know about me, so let's get it over with. I'm a telepath. On paper, not a very good one. I hear the surface of people. What you're feeling. What you're about to say. What you want, and whether you're lying about it. Not your secrets. Not your memories. Just the top layer. Like the foam on a beer." He shrugged one shoulder. "It doesn't switch off. It's never switched off. I've been listening to everybody's foam since I was eleven years old."

He said it lightly, like the setup of a joke. His eyes did not look like it was a joke.

"So. Sorry," he went on. "In advance. I'm going to hear things you don't say. Everybody hates it. Most people go very polite and very careful and start thinking about the weather on purpose. Some people go angry. A guy at the academy threw a chair at me." He picked up his melon soda. "So, you know. Feel free to think about the weather. It's been very humid."

Rei looked at him. At the neat jacket. At the knight standing on the table like a sentry. At the way he sat with his back to the wall and one eye always on the room, like someone who had learned to expect a chair.

*If Rei ever meets him, Rei should not be polite.*

Rei thought, very clearly, the way you shout across a car park, *Then just read it. I don't give a shit. It saves time.*

Kato's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

He looked at Rei. And for the first time his face did something it had not planned. His eyebrows went up. His mouth came open a little. For one second he looked a lot younger than twenty-four. Caught.

"Huh," he said.

Rei laughed.

He did not mean to. It came out of him like a cough, the first real laugh since the twelfth of August, because the look on Kato's face was so completely ridiculous. Kato stared at him laughing, with the melon soda still hanging in the air, and visibly did not know what to do with his own face, and that made Rei laugh more, until he had to put his forehead down on the table next to the knight.

"Nobody's ever," Kato started, and stopped. He put the glass down very carefully. He picked up the knight and turned it, faster now. "Okay. That's new. That is new information. I'm going to need a minute with that."

"Take a minute," said Rei into the table.

"I'm taking it. I'm taking a minute." Turn, turn, turn. "Okay. Minute taken." He cleared his throat. "Your brother did that. You know that? First day they put me with him. He was my partner for two years, and he asked for me, and nobody asks for me. First day, in the canteen. I said sorry, the usual speech, the foam, the beer. And he thought, *I'd rather you heard it than guessed it.* Very loud. Very clear. Like a man reading out a sign. Then he asked me to pass the soy sauce like nothing had happened." He looked at the knight. "I've been thinking about that for two years."

Rei lifted his head.

"Though he didn't say *shit*," Kato added. "Your brother has very clean thoughts. It's actually quite annoying."

"He's not that clean."

"No," said Kato, with a small sideways look. "He's not. But he's tidy about where he keeps the dirty ones."

[break Nothing]

The fries came. Rei ate them. Kato stole three without asking, chewed, then said "sorry" and pushed the plate back across with the same one finger he had used for the melon soda.

"Right. Business." He sat back. "I said I'd keep my eyes open. I want you to hear this from me, in person, so you don't think I didn't try." He turned the knight over once. "I've found nothing."

Rei waited.

"Nothing," said Kato. "His desk is still there, by the window, four along from mine. His mug's still there. His cactus is still there, and it's alive, because I water it, because nobody else will. His status on the board says *on assignment*. I've asked where. I've asked three people, nicely, and stood next to them while they answered." He tapped the side of his head, once. "Not one of them was lying. Not one of them was even thinking about him. They were thinking about lunch."

"So nobody knows."

"Or nobody on my floor knows. Which isn't the same thing." Kato put the knight down. "I've worked in that building four years, Rei. Usually when I go looking I find something. Somebody's guilty, somebody's bored, somebody's sleeping with somebody. There's always foam. This time there's nothing. That's never happened to me before." He looked at the knight, not at Rei. "I don't like it. That's all I've got. I don't like it."

Rei sat with that. It was not much. It was more honest than anything the man at the front desk in Marunouchi had given him.

"And the river?"

"Okada says you found a hunger thing looking out of rats. She says you threw iron at it, which is either the bravest or the stupidest thing a civilian has done in Kita this year." Kato shrugged one shoulder. "Nobody's flagged anything on the river. Nobody upstairs is thinking about rats. Three old people died in the heat. That's all it is, on paper." He met Rei's eyes. "Paper is a big part of what we do."

"It's going to do it again."

"Probably."

"Then somebody has to..."

"I know what you're going to say," said Kato. "You don't have to finish it. That's one of the nice things about me."

[break The real answer]

Rei looked at the knight for a while. Then at Kato.

"Why are you helping me?" he said. "Really."

Kato looked back at him for a long moment. Rei had the feeling of being heard. Not his words. Underneath them. Like standing in the sun with your eyes closed, feeling it on your face, knowing exactly where it is without having to look.

"Do you want the funny answer or the real one?"

"The real one."

"Nobody ever wants the real one." Kato almost smiled. "Okay. The real one. I've spent thirteen years making jokes so people forget I can hear them. It works, mostly. They laugh. They relax. They forget. And then they remember. And they look at me the way you look at a camera in a changing room." He turned the knight once more, and then put it away in his jacket pocket, and kept his hand on it there. "And you just sat down and thought *read it, I don't give a shit*. Like it was nothing. Like I was a person and not a camera." He shrugged. "Gin did that too." He was quiet for a moment. "You should know something, so you hear it from me first. Nobody in that building trusts me. Not really. A telepath who makes jokes. They think I'm always listening, and they're right, and they think I'm always selling, and they're wrong. Your brother trusted me. Mostly." He looked at the window. "I figure that's worth being near. For now."

Rei did not know what to say. So he thought it instead, as clearly as he could manage. *Thank you.*

Kato heard it. Rei watched him hear it. Something went at the corners of his eyes, and he turned his head fast to the window, to the black road outside and the reflection of the whole bright restaurant floating on it.

"Don't do that," he said to the window. "Don't think nice things at me. I don't know what to do with my face."

"Sorry."

"You're not sorry. I can hear you not being sorry." Kato stood and yanked his jacket straight with both hands, a little too hard. "I'll keep watching. It might take a long time. Don't ring me at the office. Don't text me anything you wouldn't want read out in court. If you need me, send me a photo of a cat."

"A cat?"

"Any cat. Everybody sends cats. Nobody reads cats." He dropped a thousand-yen note on the table for the drink bar and the fries. "And Rei. The thing on the river. Nobody in that building is going to lift a finger. It's going to do it again."

"I know."

"I know you know. I can hear you knowing." The joke had gone out of his face completely. "I can also hear you thinking you're going to go back there and stop it yourself. With a jar of iron and your friend who paints."

Rei said nothing.

"Yeah," said Kato. "Thought so. Just checking." He hesitated, half turned to go. "Send me a cat when you do. Please."

It was the *please* that did it. Rei looked up. Kato was already walking away between the tables, hands in his pockets, out under the green sign into the night, and he did not look back.

Rei sat alone in the booth for a long time with two glasses of melon soda and the warm place on the table where the knight had stood. Then he took out his phone and looked for a photograph of a cat. He did not have one. He had never in his life taken a photograph of a cat.

He went outside and walked three streets until he found a stray tabby washing its back leg on the roof of a parked car with total disregard for the world, and he took its photograph, and saved it, and went home on the last train with the cat in his pocket.
