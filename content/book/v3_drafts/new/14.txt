#title Kato

The email came on a Wednesday in the middle of September, at ten past six in the morning.

*Family restaurant by Itabashi station. Green sign. Tomorrow, 11pm. I'll be in the corner. K.*

That was all. No greeting. No *how are you*. Rei read it at the kitchen table with his first coffee, and then turned to the back of Gin's book, to the pages under the heading *The Ones Worth Knowing*, and read the entry again, though he knew it by now.

*Smarter than he presents, which he knows, which makes the performance slightly exhausting. Knight piece, jacket pocket. Will get you what you need through exactly the channels he pretends he doesn't use. Useful. Not warm. Has said approximately four genuine things to me in two years and they were all correct.*

*I'll keep my eyes open,* Kato had written, four minutes after Rei's first email, on the first day. *If I find anything, I'll tell you.* There had been nothing since. Not one line in a month.

Rei looked at the hook by the door, where nothing hung.

"Useful, not warm," he said to the empty flat. The flat did not have an opinion.

[break The green sign]

Itabashi is the ward west of Kita. It is mostly flats and small factories and long straight roads where nothing much happens. The family restaurant sat on a corner by the station, under a big green sign with a cartoon chef on it giving a thumbs up. Its tall windows were full of yellow light. Inside it was warm and smelled of fried things and coffee that had been on the hot plate since dinner. The seats were red vinyl, the tables were fake wood, and every table had the same laminated menu, thick as a magazine, with a photograph of every dish from spaghetti with cod roe to a chocolate parfait taller than a child. There was a drink bar by the toilets where you paid once and filled your glass as many times as you wanted.

At eleven at night it belonged to people with nowhere better to be. Two university students at a window table with their textbooks open and their eyes closed. A taxi driver eating curry with his elbows out and his cap on the seat beside him. An old woman alone with a pot of tea and a crossword, filling it in with a pen, which Rei thought was very brave. A young waitress with her hair in a net, moving between the tables in white trainers that squeaked.

[need ch14_famires | A Japanese family restaurant at 11pm, red vinyl booths and big yellow-lit windows, students asleep over textbooks, a lean young man in a dark overcoat and tie sitting very upright in a corner booth with an ivory chess knight standing on the table, Rei in a hoodie sliding in opposite]

Kato Ryusei was in the corner booth with his back to the wall, where he could see the whole room and the door.

He did not look like anyone else in there. He was tall and lean and sat very upright, like somebody waiting to be called into an interview. He wore a dark grey overcoat that he had not taken off, and under it a charcoal suit and a dark tie, properly knotted, at eleven at night in a family restaurant. His black hair was short and neat, combed so precisely that it looked as if it had been done with a ruler. His face was narrow and pale and gave nothing away. His eyes were very dark, almost black, and they were already on Rei before Rei was through the door.

In front of him on the table there was a cup of black coffee from the drink bar, untouched, and a paper napkin folded into a perfect square. And standing beside the napkin, on its round base, a small chess piece in old yellowed ivory. A knight. The horse's head was worn smooth at the ears from years of being turned in somebody's fingers.

Rei slid into the booth opposite. Up close Kato looked about twenty-four. There was a small cut on the side of one finger, the kind you get from handling something with an edge.

"Good evening," Rei said. "Thank you for..."

"You'd like the hamburg steak," said Kato. His voice was quiet and level. "You're going to order fries, because you've been doing sums about rent since the station. Order the hamburg steak. I'm paying."

Rei stopped with his mouth open.

The waitress squeaked up to the table. "Good evening, are you ready to..."

"Hamburg steak with rice," said Kato, without looking at the menu. "And the drink bar. He'll get it himself."

The waitress went. Kato moved the knight a centimetre to the left, so that it stood exactly in line with the edge of the napkin.

"I'm a telepath," he said. "I expect Gin's book told you. I hear the surface of people. What you're feeling. What you're about to say. What you want, and whether you're lying about it. I can't turn it off." He said it as someone else might say *I'm left-handed*. "Most people don't like it. They go careful. They start thinking about the weather on purpose. That's fine. I'm used to it."

He picked up his coffee, looked at it, and put it down again without drinking.

"So you know," he said. "That's all."

Rei looked at him. At the knotted tie. At the knight lined up with the napkin. At the way he sat with his back to the wall and his eyes on the door, like a man who expected people to get up and leave.

*If he can hear it anyway,* Rei thought, very clearly, *then he can just read it. I don't care. It saves time.*

Kato went still.

It was a very small thing. His face did not move much. But a single strand of his perfect hair slipped loose and fell across his forehead, and he did not notice it. He looked at Rei for a long second, with the coffee cup halfway to the table, and for that second he did not look twenty-four. He looked surprised, which Rei suspected did not happen to him often.

Rei laughed. He did not mean to. It came out of him like a cough, the first proper laugh in weeks, because of the hair, and because Kato so plainly had no idea what to do with his face.

"Something funny," said Kato.

"Your hair."

Kato put a hand up, found the strand, and pushed it back. It fell down again. He left it there.

"Your brother did that," he said after a while. "The first day they put me with him. Same thing. Same words, nearly. Then he asked me to pass the soy sauce." He picked up the knight and turned it over once in his fingers. "He was my partner for two years. He asked for me. Nobody asks for me."

[break Nothing]

The hamburg steak came on a hot iron plate that spat and hissed, with a fried egg on top and a little mound of corn and a separate bowl of rice. Rei got himself a cola from the drink bar and came back and ate. He was hungrier than he had known. Kato watched him eat with mild interest, as if he were watching somebody else solve a chess problem quite badly.

"Business," said Kato, when the plate was half empty. "I said I'd keep my eyes open. I have. I want you to hear this from me, in person, so you don't think I didn't look." He put the knight down. "I've found nothing."

Rei put his fork down.

"I work in the Arsenal," said Kato. "Sub-level. Weapons, salt, iron, everything people take out into the field and bring back broken. Everybody in that building comes down to my counter sooner or later. They stand there while I check their kit, and they think." He tapped the side of his head once. "For four weeks I've stood at that counter and listened to every field officer in the Division think. Not one of them is thinking about your brother."

"Nobody?"

"Nobody. His desk upstairs is still there. His mug's still there. There's a cactus on his windowsill that nobody else would water, so I go up and water it on Mondays." Kato said this without any expression at all. "His name on the board says *on assignment*. Nobody's lying when they read it. Nobody's thinking about it either. They're thinking about lunch, and overtime, and who's sleeping with who in Special Cases."

"So nobody knows."

"Or nobody who comes to my counter knows. That isn't the same thing." He straightened the napkin, which was already straight. "Usually when I go looking, I find something. Somebody's guilty, somebody's bored, somebody's hiding a bottle in their locker. There's always something. This time there's nothing at all." He looked at the knight, not at Rei. "I don't like it. That's all I've got."

It was not much. It was more honest than anything the man at the front desk in Marunouchi had given him.

"Thank you," Rei said. "For looking."

"Don't thank me for finding nothing." But Kato's hand had gone to the knight again, and he was turning it, slowly, and the strand of hair was still on his forehead.

[break The real answer]

They sat for a while without talking. The students at the window had both fallen properly asleep, one with his cheek on an open page. The old woman finished her crossword, capped her pen, and looked very pleased with herself. Outside a lorry went past and the windows hummed.

"Why are you doing this?" Rei said. "You said you'd watch. You didn't have to meet me."

Kato considered the question. Rei had the feeling of being listened to underneath his words, like the sun on your face when your eyes are shut.

"In that building," Kato said, "people don't trust telepaths. They think we're always listening. They're right. They think we're always selling what we hear. They're wrong, but there's no way to prove it, so I stopped trying." He said it flatly, as if reading out a weather report. "Your brother didn't care. He'd stand at my counter and think whatever he liked, and some of it was very rude about the coffee machine." He turned the knight. "I wanted to see if it ran in the family."

"Does it?"

"You've been thinking for the last ten minutes that my tie is ridiculous," said Kato. "And that you'd like another cola but you don't want to look greedy. So yes. Apparently it does."

Rei got up and got another cola.

When he came back, Kato was standing, buttoning his overcoat to the neck. He put a thousand-yen note under the edge of the hot plate, squared it with the edge of the table, and looked at it to make sure it was straight.

"Don't ring me at the office. Don't text me anything you wouldn't want read out in a meeting. If you need me, send me a photograph of a cat."

"A cat?"

"Everybody sends cats. Nobody reads cats." He picked up the knight and put it away in the inside pocket of his jacket, and kept his hand on it there for a moment. "I'll keep looking. It might take a long time."

He went out between the tables, past the sleeping students and the old woman with her finished crossword, out under the green sign into the dark, and did not look back.

Rei sat alone in the booth with his second cola. Then he took out his phone and looked for a photograph of a cat. He did not have one. He had never in his life taken a photograph of a cat.

On the way to the station there was a stray tabby on the roof of a parked car, washing its back leg with total disregard for the world. He took its photograph, and saved it, and went home on the last train with the cat in his pocket.
