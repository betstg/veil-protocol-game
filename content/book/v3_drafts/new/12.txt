#title Rats

The file came across Rei's desk at the station on a Tuesday in the middle of September, in a pile of things nobody wanted.

Detective Kumagai dropped the pile on the corner of the desk without looking at it or at Rei, the way you drop a coat on a chair. "File those, monk. Nothing in there. Heat deaths." He was already walking away, peeling the wrapper off a rice ball with his thumb. "Summer. Old people. Every year."

It was a report from the week before. A woman of seventy-eight found dead in her flat in Akabane, near the Arakawa. Lived alone. A neighbour noticed the smell. The doctor said heart failure, made worse by the heat. Case closed. The kind of report that came in every week in August and September, and that nobody read twice, because there was nothing in it to read.

But stapled to the back was a second page. A note from the uniformed officer who had found her, in careful round handwriting that went uphill across the lines. The neighbours, it said, had complained about rats for a week before she died. Not rats running about. Rats *sitting*. A dozen of them, every evening, on the outside stairs and the walkway in front of her door, up on their back legs, very still, all facing the door. When the neighbours chased them they went, all together, and came back the next evening and sat in the same places facing the same way.

At the bottom, in pen, the officer had added, *Neighbours report many rats along embankment road this month. Behaving oddly. Health department informed.*

Rei read the second page three times.

Then he got up, and said to nobody that he was going to the filing room, and went to the filing room, and pulled every death report from the river wards for the last five weeks. He found two more. A man of eighty-one in Kawaguchi, just across the river. A woman of sixty-six in Shimura. Both alone. Both heart failure. In both, somebody had mentioned rats. Sitting still. Looking at the door.

He photographed all three with his phone, put the files back exactly where they belonged, squared the edges, and went back to his desk and made Kumagai a cup of tea with two sugars, the way he liked it. Nobody saw. Nobody would have cared if they had.

That night at the kitchen table he found the page in Gin's book. He had read it weeks before and not understood it. He understood it now.

[doc Gin's book. Borrowed senses]
A Class B or higher entity can divide off a sliver of itself into ordinary neighbourhood animals and ride their senses. Rats, most often, because nobody looks at rats. One entity, a hundred small eyes and ears spread across a ward. The tell is never the rats. It is the pattern. Rats that move with too much purpose. Rats that hold still together. Rats that all turn toward the same thing at the same time. Twelve rats looking at one corner means something is wearing them to look. Salt does not stop borrowed senses. The rats are not the thing. They are only its eyes. Do not waste iron on rats. Save it for whatever they are looking at.
[/doc]

*They are only its eyes.*

Something was walking the river with a hundred small eyes, looking at doors. Looking for the people behind them who were alone.

Rei looked up at the brass hook by the front door. Then at the sofa where he slept. Then at the second cup he had put out without thinking, on the other side of the table, cold.

He texted Itsuki. *are you busy tomorrow night. bring shoes you can run in.*

The answer came back in eleven seconds. *i was born busy. what are we running from*

*rats*

A long pause. Then. *i hate you. what time*

[break The embankment]

[need ch12_arakawa_night | The Arakawa river embankment at night in Kita, a long raised grass bank with a straight footpath on top, the dark wide river, a distant lit bridge, a line of live rats sitting perfectly still on their back legs along the path all facing the same way inland, two young men with torches stopped in front of them]

The Arakawa is the big river along the northern edge of Kita. It is wide and grey and slow, and on both sides there are high grass banks that stop the city drowning when the typhoons come. Along the top of the bank on the Tokyo side runs a long straight path. In the daytime it belongs to joggers and cyclists and old men with fishing rods who have not caught anything since the Olympics, and families flying kites shaped like squid. At night it belongs to nobody. The lights of the bridges hang far off on either side. The black water slides past below without a sound, as if it has somewhere better to be.

Rei and Itsuki walked the bank at eleven at night with two torches and a backpack full of salt and iron.

"So we're looking for rats," said Itsuki.

"Yes."

"On a riverbank."

"Yes."

"At night."

"Yes."

"In the dark, by the water, where rats live, which is their *house*." Itsuki swung his torch in a lazy circle over the grass. "This is the worst night out you've ever taken me on, and you once took me to a hundred-yen sushi place that gave me food poisoning on my birthday."

"That was the sushi's fault."

"You *chose* the sushi."

"You ate eleven plates."

"Because I was *celebrating*." Itsuki's torch found a beer can, a shoe, a heron standing on one leg in the shallows that looked at them with deep contempt. "Gin used to say rats were the second cleverest animal in Tokyo. After crows. Before people."

"He told me that too."

"He told everyone that. He thought it was a great line." Itsuki was quiet for a few steps. "It is a good line."

They found the first one twenty minutes upriver from Akabane.

It was sitting in the middle of the path. A big brown sewer rat, fat and glossy, up on its back legs, front paws held together against its chest like a little old man about to make a speech. It did not run when the torch hit it. It did not move at all. It faced away from the river, inland, toward the houses, perfectly still. A small brown statue somebody had put there as a joke.

A metre further on there was another. Sitting the same way. Facing the same way. And a metre after that, another.

Rei swung his torch along the path. In the yellow circle of light the rats sat one after another, evenly spaced as fence posts, all up on their back legs, all facing inland, all perfectly, completely still. A line of small watchers leading off the top of the bank and down a flight of concrete steps into the streets.

"Okay," said Itsuki, very quietly. "Okay. I don't like that."

Rei breathed. Four in. Four held. Six out. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width, slowly, the way Sōun had taught him, the way he had practised every morning for two weeks on the old soft boards.

And the rats were not many rats.

That was the strange part. With the door open he did not feel thirty small animals. He felt one thing. One presence, stretched thin along the path like a thread through beads, looking out through thirty pairs of small black eyes at once. It was grey-yellow and sick and it pulled at something under his ribs. It felt like hunger. Not for food. A wider hunger. A wanting with no bottom to it. Wanting someone to call. Wanting to be touched on the arm. Wanting the phone to ring, just once, just once, and it to be for you.

"It's not in them," Rei said. "It's looking through them. They're its eyes." He followed the line down the steps with the torch. "It's watching for somebody. Somebody alone."

The nearest rat turned its head and looked at him.

Then the next. Then the next. All down the line, one after another, thirty small heads turning on thirty small necks, slowly, smoothly, together, until every rat on the path was looking straight at Rei with its tiny wet black eyes.

"*Oh,*" said Itsuki. "Oh, I *really* don't like that. Rei. Rei, they're all..."

The rats held still for one long second. Looking.

Then all of them dropped onto four feet in the same instant and ran. Not away. Down the steps. Fast. One brown stream pouring off the bank toward the houses.

"It's seen us," said Rei. "It's going to hurry."

He ran.

[break Tachibana]

The rats went down the steps, across a road, along the side of a shuttered petrol station and into a street of old two-storey wooden apartment buildings with iron stairs up the outside and laundry hanging off the balconies like flags of surrender. In front of the third building the stream went up the outside stairs and stopped. They sat down again. On every step. Along the walkway at the top. Up on their back legs. Still. All facing the second door.

The light inside was on. There was a name card in the slot by the door. *Tachibana.*

The door was not quite shut.

Rei felt it before he touched the handle. Cold. The same wet cold as the platform at Ōji, but thinner and closer and hungrier, like the air in front of an open freezer in a dark kitchen at night. The pressure behind his eyes hit like a hand. He did the breathing. He opened the door in his chest a crack and held it there with his whole body, the way you hold a front door against a typhoon wind.

Behind him on the iron stairs, Itsuki was breathing very fast through his nose.

"You can wait here," Rei said, without turning round.

"Fuck off," said Itsuki, also very quietly, and came up the last step.

Rei pushed the door open.

The flat was one room and a tiny kitchen, very clean and very full. Every shelf was covered with small things. China cats. Postcards from Hokkaido and Okinawa and somewhere in Hawaii. Plastic flowers in a jam jar. Photographs. A young man in a graduation gown. The same young man at a wedding. A small child on a beach squinting into the sun with a spade. The television was on with the sound off, a quiz show, a man in a bow tie laughing at something. On the low table were six empty takeout ramen bowls from a shop called Hachiya, stacked neatly, each with its paper receipt tucked underneath, the way you keep things you want to remember.

Along the top of the wardrobe, along the windowsill, along the back of the little sofa, rats sat in a row. Still. Watching.

A woman sat on the floor in the middle of the room with her back to them.

She was about sixty. She wore a pink cotton nightdress with small blue flowers on it. Her grey hair was rolled in pink plastic curlers. She sat with her legs folded under her, very straight, facing the wall. And she was eating.

Rei saw her right hand rise to her mouth. Lower. Rise again. There was a sound in the room, small and wet and constant, under the silence of the television. Chewing.

"Mrs. Tachibana?" Rei said.

She stopped.

Her head turned. Not her shoulders. Not her body. Only her head, slowly, round on her neck, much further than a neck is built to go, until her face was looking at them over her own shoulder blade and her body still faced the wall. Something in her neck made a small dry grinding noise, like a wooden pestle turning in a mortar.

Her face was smeared with something grey and brown. Her mouth was full. Her cheeks were stuffed so full they bulged out like a squirrel's. She was still chewing, slowly, jaw going round and round, round and round. Behind Rei, Itsuki made a sound and clapped a hand over his own mouth.

She had been eating everything in the room. Paper. A postcard was in her hand, half of it, wet and torn at the corner where she had bitten it. Cotton wool. Dry rice from a split bag, scattered all over the mat. The petals off the plastic flowers. A receipt from Hachiya, chewed into a grey pulp. There was blood on her lips and her chin where something sharp had gone in, and it had run down into the neck of her nightdress, and the little blue flowers there had gone dark.

Her eyes were black.

The pupils had spread until her eyes were two holes, and the holes took in the whole room at once. Rei felt them take him in. His face, his hands, the backpack, the iron, Itsuki behind him. All of it, in one second, with a wanting so strong he could feel it on his skin like heat off a stove. Not an old woman's look. Not a human look. Something that saw everything and wanted all of it and had already started deciding what it would have first.

"Hungry," she said.

The voice was hers. That was the worst of it. It was not deep, not strange, not anything from a film. It was a small, polite, ordinary voice. The voice of a woman who said good morning to her neighbours and thank you to the man who read the gas meter. But it came out of her full mouth around the food still in it, so it was thick and wet, and it did not stop. It went on and on, very fast, all on one note, a radio left on between stations.

"Hungry hungry so hungry nobody calls nobody comes my son is in Osaka he says next month next month next month the ramen boy comes on Thursdays he says thank you auntie he says it every single time so hungry so hungry so..."

[break Iron]

Rei tore open the salt and threw a line of it across the mat between her and the door. It did nothing he could see. On the wardrobe and the sill and the sofa, the rats did not move. They did not care about salt. They were only eyes. They watched him do it, every one of them, heads turning together to follow his hands.

So he stopped thinking. He tore the lid off a jar of iron filings and threw them at her.

Gin's book said iron bites anything that is wearing a body that is not its own. It said it burns. The filings went across the room in a black spray and hit Mrs. Tachibana on the side of her face and her shoulder.

She screamed.

Not with her own voice. Something screamed out of her mouth, high and thin and furious, a kettle and a cat at once, and half-chewed rice sprayed out of her with it. Her head snapped back round the right way with a crack Rei felt in his own teeth. Her whole body jerked as if she had grabbed a live wire.

And every rat in the room screamed with her.

Thirty small throats on the wardrobe and the sill and the sofa, all opening in the same instant, all shrieking the same thin shriek. The scream of the thing that was looking out of them. Then they ran. All of them at once, off the furniture, across the floor, over Rei's feet, over Itsuki's legs where he had fallen back into the kitchen doorway, a brown wave of hot small bodies going for the door and the window and out and gone. The thing was pulling its eyes back. It was running.

"Rei!" Itsuki was on his back in the kitchen doorway with his arms over his face. "Rei, they're on me, they're on *me*..."

"They're only eyes," said Rei. "They can't hurt you. Iron. Throw it at her."

He tossed the second jar without looking. Itsuki caught it one-handed, flat on his back, which surprised them both very much. He tore the lid off with his teeth, spat it across the room, and threw.

The second spray hit Mrs. Tachibana full in the chest.

The thing came out of her.

Rei saw it, with the door open. A grey-yellow shape, thin and stretched, like smoke with too many elbows, dragging itself out of her open mouth and her nose and her ears. It hung over her for one second. It looked at Rei. He felt it look, all that bottomless wanting, turned on him like a torch. Then it went out of the window after its rats, into the dark, toward the river.

The room was silent except for the man in the bow tie, still laughing without any sound.

Mrs. Tachibana was lying on her side on the mat.

[break The last thing]

Rei knelt beside her.

Her eyes were brown now. The black had shrunk back to two small ordinary dots. Ordinary brown eyes, wet and terrified. Her mouth was still full. She was trying to breathe and she could not. Her chest jerked. Her hands moved weakly at her throat, the fingers opening and closing.

"Itsuki. Ambulance. Now."

Itsuki was already dialling. His hands were shaking so badly that he dropped the phone, picked it up, dropped it again, and said "fuck, fuck, *fuck*" and got it on the third try.

Rei rolled her onto her side and hooked his fingers into her mouth. Paper. Rice. Plastic. Cotton wool. Some of it came out in a grey wet mess over his hand. Most of it was too far down. She was looking at him the whole time. Her hand found his wrist and held on, very hard for a woman so small.

He did not know what else to do. So he opened the door in his chest all the way, and he looked at her.

He saw her. Not her past, like the mug in Lilac. Her *now*. A woman alone in a room full of small things. A son in Osaka who rang on her birthday and at New Year and said next month, next month. A husband nine years dead. Nobody who touched her, ever, except the doctor once a year with cold hands, and the boy from Hachiya who brought the ramen on Thursdays and said *thank you, auntie*, and she ordered ramen every Thursday even when she was not hungry, even when she could not afford it, just to hear him say it.

That was what the thing had found in her, with its rats sitting outside her door every evening, watching. Not hunger for food. Hunger for one warm word. It had found that and climbed inside it and eaten her out from the middle, using her own wanting as the spoon.

Her grip on his wrist went loose.

"I'm here," Rei said. He did not know if she could hear him. "I'm here. Somebody's here, auntie. Somebody came."

Her eyes looked at him. Then past him, at nothing. Then at nothing at all.

The ambulance took eleven minutes. By then she had been dead for eight.

[break The back of the card]

They sat on the bottom step of the outside stairs while the ambulance men went up. Rei's hands were covered in things he did not want to look at. Itsuki sat next to him with his arms locked round his knees, shaking from his shoulders down, the unlit cigarette from behind his ear crushed to pieces in his fist. Neither of them said anything for a long time. Above them someone's wind chime was ringing, over and over, though there was no wind.

Then Rei wiped his right hand on his jeans, took out his wallet, and found the white card with the owl on it. He turned it over to the side with the small neat writing.

*Call the back. Never the front.*

He called the back.

Two rings. "Okada."

"It's Rei. Uedera Rei." His voice came out wrong and he stopped and made it come out right. "I'm in Akabane. There's a woman. She's dead. There were rats. It was in her, and I got it out, and she... I couldn't..." He shut his eyes. "There are two others. I've got the files. It's going along the river. It'll do it again."

Silence on the line. Then, faint, the sound of keys being picked up off a table, and a door.

"Address," said Okada. "Don't touch anything else. Don't talk to anybody in a nice suit. I'm coming."

She came in twenty minutes, in a taxi, in the same plain suit with the shiny elbows. She looked at the stairs. She looked at the iron filings glittering on the walkway and the salt on the floor inside the door and at Rei's hands. She did not ask a single question. She took a packet of wet wipes out of her bag and put it in Rei's lap. Then she took out her phone and made a call, and Rei heard her say, in a flat bored voice, a voice for people who are filling in the same form for the hundredth time, "Okada, Investigation. Natural death, Akabane, single occupant, elderly. I'm on scene. No veil signature. No need for Public Confidence. I'll file in the morning."

She hung up. Rei stared at her.

"You lied," he said.

"Yes." She sat down on the step on his other side, between him and the street, and took out her three notebooks with the rubber band round them and opened the middle one on her knee. "If I report what really happened here, men in blue suits will be on this staircase in twenty minutes. They'll ask who threw the iron. Then they'll want to know who you are." She clicked her pen. "Then they'll know. Your brother didn't want them to know. I'm not giving it to them for free on a staircase in Akabane." She looked at him over the notebook. "Now. Show me the files. All three. Then tell me about the rats, from the beginning, slowly, and don't leave anything out because you think it's stupid."

Itsuki laughed. It came out of him suddenly, high and shaky, half of it a sob.

"Sorry," he said, wiping his face on his sleeve. "Sorry. It's just. I've been scared out of my mind for an hour. And she's the scariest thing I've seen all night."

Okada did not look up from her notebook.

"Thank you," she said.
