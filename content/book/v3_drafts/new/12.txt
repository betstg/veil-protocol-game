#title The Tired Man

The entry was near the back of Gin's book, on a page Rei had turned past a dozen times without stopping.

He found it at three in the morning on the second Saturday in September, because that was when he found most things. He had woken at three, as he always did now, and lain there for a while with the key cold against his chest, and then given up and put the kettle on. He made one cup. He looked at it. He took down a second cup and put it on the other side of the table, and sat down between the two, and opened the book at random.

[doc Gin's book. Kamiya]
Kamiya, the old market arcade. Upstairs flat over the fish shop, number 3. A salaryman, Hirose. Mid forties, lives alone. Tired in a way sleep doesn't touch. Losing weight. Going grey too fast. Doctors find nothing.

There's something down in the arcade that's very sad, and it's coming up through his floor. Not a demon. Nothing that wants him. Just something heavy that doesn't know it's leaking.

He hasn't asked anyone for help. He doesn't know there's anything to ask about.

Haven't had time. Come back in September. Don't leave it past the autumn.
[/doc]

It was dated the end of July. Two weeks before the twelfth of August.

Rei read it again. He thought of a man he had never met, in a flat he had never seen, getting thinner and greyer every week, going to work and coming home and sleeping and waking up tired, while the person who had meant to help him had vanished off a walkway in Kita.

*Come back in September.*

It was September.

He drank his tea. Then, because it was getting cold and nobody else was going to, he drank the second cup too.

[break Kamiya]

Itsuki came with him on Sunday afternoon, in his paint-stained hoodie, with a can of coffee in each pocket and a clipboard under his arm that he had taken from the art supplies cupboard at Geidai.

"What's the clipboard for?"

"Nobody questions a clipboard," said Itsuki. "You walk into any building in Tokyo with a clipboard and a tired face, and people hold the door open for you. I've been in the back room of the National Museum with a clipboard." He clipped a sheet of paper to it and wrote across the top in neat capitals, *KITA WARD HOUSING. DAMP AND MOULD SURVEY.* "Also a lanyard. Hold on."

He pulled a lanyard out of his pocket. It had a plastic card on it with his own photograph and the words *VISITOR. GEIDAI LIBRARY.*

"Nobody reads the lanyard," he said, and hung it round his neck. "They only see that there is one."

The old Kamiya market was ten minutes' walk from the station, on a street of low houses with potted plants crowded on every doorstep. It had been a proper market once. Rei could see that from the entrance. A covered arcade, long and straight, running between two rows of narrow shops, with a high curved roof of yellow plastic panels on an iron frame. A painted sign over the entrance arch said *KAMIYA GINZA* in red letters with the paint coming off in flakes. Under the sign hung a row of plastic flowers, pink and orange, faded almost white by forty summers of sun.

Inside, nearly every shop was shut. Grey metal shutters, one after another, down to the ground, some of them dented, some with notices taped to them that the rain had got to. *Closed. Thank you for many years.* *For rent.* *Closed.* A shutter at the far end had been painted, a long time ago, with a picture of a smiling cartoon fish in a chef's hat.

Two shops were still open. A small tofu shop near the entrance, where an old woman in a white apron and rubber boots sat on a stool beside her tanks of cold water, listening to a radio. And halfway down, a tobacco kiosk with nobody in it at all, just a bell on the counter and a sign that said *Ring*.

The light through the yellow roof made everything the colour of weak tea. It was very quiet. Their footsteps came back to them off the shutters. A pigeon walked across the tiles in front of them without hurrying, and went under a gap in a shutter, and was gone.

[need ch12_kamiya_arcade | An old covered market arcade in Kita on a Sunday afternoon, a curved yellow plastic roof, rows of closed grey metal shutters, faded plastic flowers hanging at the entrance under a flaking red sign, one tofu shop still open with an old woman on a stool, two young men walking in, one with a clipboard]

"This is the saddest place I've ever been," said Itsuki, very quietly. "And I've been to my family's New Year party."

Rei did not answer. He had stopped walking.

He breathed. Four in. Four held. Six out. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width, slowly, as Sōun had taught him on the soft old boards every morning for two weeks.

The arcade changed.

Not the shutters, or the light, or the pigeon. The air. The air in the arcade was heavy, the way the air in a room is heavy after somebody has cried in it for a long time. It lay along the tiles like water lying in a ditch, and it was cold, and it was grey, and it was sad. Not angry. Not hungry. Just sad, in a slow, tired, old way, as if the sadness had been there so long it had forgotten what it was about.

And it was thickest at the far end. By the shutter with the cartoon fish on it.

"There," Rei said.

[break The fish shop]

The shutter was padlocked, and the padlock was rusted shut. Above it, a wooden sign, painted, said *UOKICHI. FRESH FISH. SINCE 1958.* The paint had worn off the bottom half of the letters. To the right of the shutter there was a narrow door, and behind the door a steep staircase going up to the flats above the shops. Number 3 had a name card in a plastic slot. *Hirose.*

Rei put his hand flat on the cold metal of the fish shop shutter.

He saw it at once. It was not like the mug in Lilac. There was no single moment, no shout, no word that had never been said. It was a thousand mornings, all laid one on top of the other, so many that they had worn smooth together.

Six o'clock. Every morning. An old man in rubber boots and a long rubber apron, with a white towel tied round his head, bending to unlock this padlock, and pushing up this shutter with a long hooked pole, and the shutter going up with a rattle that echoed down the whole arcade. Laying out ice on the slanted counter. Laying out fish on the ice. Mackerel. Squid. A few sea bream, red and silver. Then a broom, and sweeping the tiles in front of the shop. Then standing behind the counter with his hands folded on his apron. Waiting.

In the early mornings, the arcade was full. Housewives with baskets. Restaurant men with lists. Children on their way to school, stopping to look at the octopus. He knew them all by name.

Then, slowly, over many years, fewer. The supermarket opened by the station. The shutters began to come down, one by one, along the arcade. The housewives got old. Their children moved away. The restaurant men stopped coming.

He opened every morning anyway. Six o'clock. The padlock, the pole, the rattle, the ice, the fish, the broom. Standing behind his counter with his hands folded, in an arcade with nobody in it. Throwing the fish away at night. Opening again in the morning.

Because if he did not open, then it was over. And he could not let it be over.

Rei took his hand off the shutter. His fingers were so cold that they hurt.

"Rei?" Itsuki had his hand on Rei's shoulder. Rei had not felt it arrive. "You're grey. You've gone actually grey."

"He's still opening," Rei said. "Every morning. He doesn't know he's dead. Or he knows, and he doesn't care. He's still opening the shop."

Itsuki looked at the cartoon fish on the shutter. The smiling fish in its chef's hat. He looked at it for a long time.

"Come on," he said at last. "Let's go and see the man upstairs. Then we'll get you something hot."

[break Number 3]

Mr. Hirose opened the door on the third knock.

He was a man in his middle forties who looked sixty. He was very thin. His white office shirt hung off his shoulders as if it belonged to a bigger man, and his grey trousers were held up by a belt pulled to the last hole. His hair had gone grey all over, not in streaks but evenly, like ash. His face was polite and pale and very tired, with deep shadows under the eyes that looked as if they had been there for years. He was holding a cup of instant noodles with the lid peeled halfway back and chopsticks stuck in it.

"Yes?"

"Good afternoon," said Itsuki, before Rei could say anything. He held up the clipboard and smiled a small, bored, professional smile that Rei had never once seen on his face. "Kita Ward Housing. We're doing a damp and mould survey of the older buildings in the area. It'll only take five minutes. We're very sorry to disturb you on a Sunday."

Mr. Hirose looked at the clipboard. He looked at the lanyard. He did not read it.

"Damp," he said. "Yes. I suppose there might be damp." He stepped back from the door. "Please. Come in. Sorry about the mess."

There was no mess. The flat was one room and a small kitchen, very tidy and very bare. A futon folded against the wall. A low table with a laptop on it and a pile of work files. A television that was not on. A shelf of business books. Along the windowsill there was a row of potted plants, the only things in the room that anybody had chosen because they liked them.

On the left side of the sill, by the door, the plants were green and healthy. A small cactus. A pot of mint. A spider plant with babies hanging off it on long stems.

On the right side of the sill, the plants were dead. All of them. A fern gone brown and crisp. Something that had once had flowers, now just grey stalks. A little bonsai pine, its needles dropped in a ring round its pot.

The dead ones were in the corner. And the corner was cold.

Rei could feel it from the door. It was not a draught. It came up through the floor, through the boards, out of the fish shop below, grey and heavy and slow.

"Is that corner always cold?" he asked.

Mr. Hirose looked at him, surprised. "Yes. How did you know? I've had the landlord look at it twice. He says the building's old." He put his noodles down on the table. He did not seem to have eaten any of them. "I put the plants there because it gets the morning sun. But they don't like it. I keep buying new ones." He gave a small embarrassed laugh. "My mother says I'm a plant murderer."

Itsuki wrote something on the clipboard. Rei looked over. It said *damp* and then a very good drawing of the dead bonsai.

"How have you been sleeping, Mr. Hirose?" Rei said. "Sorry. It's on the form. Damp can affect sleep."

"Oh. Fine. Well." Mr. Hirose rubbed his eyes. "I sleep a lot. Too much, my doctor says. I go to bed at nine and wake up at seven and I'm still tired. They've done blood tests. Everything's normal." He looked at the cold corner. "I have a strange dream. Every night, the same one. I'm under water. Deep. It's dark and it's very quiet, and I'm just lying there on the bottom, and I could swim up, but I don't." He smiled, a little puzzled. "And I don't mind. That's the strange part. I don't mind at all."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Thank you," said Itsuki, in his bored professional voice, which had gone slightly unsteady. "That's very helpful. We'll put it in the report."

[break The night shift]

Rei went back on his own that night.

He did not tell Itsuki. Itsuki would have come, and Rei did not want him to see if it went wrong. He told himself that was the reason.

The arcade at midnight was very dark. The yellow roof was black. One streetlight at the entrance threw a long pale stripe down the tiles, and the rest was shadow and shutters and the smell of old fish and rust. The tofu shop was closed. The tobacco kiosk was closed. Somewhere a tap was dripping.

Rei stood in front of the fish shop shutter with a torch and a bag of salt he did not think he would need, and opened the door in his chest. A crack. Then a little more.

*Hello,* he thought, at the shutter. He did not know how else to begin. *I know you're there. I know you're still opening. I've come to help.*

Nothing happened.

He opened the door wider. The grey came in at once. Cold and slow and enormous, like standing in the sea when a big wave goes past your waist. It was not aimed at him. It did not want him. It did not even notice him. It was just there, all of it, forty years of mornings, and he had opened a door into it, and it flowed in to fill the space, the way water does.

He tried to close the door. The latch would not catch.

The torch was on the ground. He did not remember dropping it. His knees were on the tiles. He was very tired, suddenly, more tired than he had ever been, and it seemed like a good idea to lie down for a minute, here, on the cool tiles, in the dark. Just for a minute. He could always get up. He did not mind. He did not mind at all.

*Not your feeling,* said a voice in his head. It sounded like Sōun. It was his own. *Not yours. Close it.*

He found the latch. It took everything he had. He pushed the door shut a finger's width at a time, the way he had been taught, and every finger's width was like pushing a car uphill. When it shut, he was lying on his side on the tiles with his cheek in something sticky, breathing like he had run up all the stairs at Ōji.

He lay there for a long time. Then he got up, and picked up the torch, and walked out of the arcade, and went into the first konbini he could find. He stood at the window counter with a can of corn soup in both hands and a rice ball he could not eat, under the bright flat lights, until the floor felt like a floor again.

At two in the morning he took out his phone and wrote to Haruki.

*I need help with something. I tried on my own. I couldn't.*

The answer came in a minute, though it was Sunday and the bar was shut and Haruki had no reason to be awake.

*Good. That's the first sensible thing you've done. Friday. Bring the painter.*
