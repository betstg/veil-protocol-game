#title Grid Money

On Tuesday the twenty-fifth of August, Gin's salary would go into Gin's account, the same as every month. Rei had no way to see it. Gin had never given him a way to see it, or touch it.

At one o'clock Rei walked to the bank branch by Ōji station and stood in a queue behind a grandmother who was paying her gas bill entirely in coins, counting them out onto the counter one at a time with enormous dignity. When he got to the window, a young woman in a neat uniform typed for a long time.

"I'm very sorry," she said. "Only the account holder can make withdrawals or transfers."

"He's my brother. He's missing. The rent comes out of that account. Can you at least tell me if his salary came in?"

"I'm afraid I can't tell you anything about the account." She looked as if she understood. It did not change anything. "Without the account holder, we would need written authorisation from him. Or a court order." She slid a printed sheet across the counter, a list of documents in very small type. "I'm sorry, sir."

He walked out into the rain with the sheet of paper in his hand and stood under the awning of a shoe shop and lit a cigarette, and did the sums again on the back of it, with a biro, pressing so hard that he tore the paper.

Whatever was in Gin's account, it might as well have been on the moon.

The rent for September was eighty-four thousand yen, due on Thursday. Gin had always paid it by hand on the twenty-sixth, the day after his salary came in, every month, like a clock.

Rei had thirty-one thousand yen in his own account. His internship would pay him eighteen thousand at the end of the month, for travel and lunches.

It was not even close.

[break Ni-chōme]

Ni-chōme is a few narrow streets east of Shinjuku station. In the daytime it is quiet. Small offices, shuttered bars, a café or two. At night it fills up. For fifty years it has been where gay men and women in Tokyo go to find a bar where nobody looks at them twice. There are hundreds of bars packed into those few streets, most of them so small that eight people make a crowd.

Rei came up out of the subway at seven in the evening. The rain had stopped. The streets were wet and shining and full of red and pink and green reflections, like somebody had spilled a box of paints. Two men in office suits went past holding hands and laughing at something. A very tall woman in a sequined dress stood smoking in a doorway, talking into her phone in a voice like gravel in a bucket. Somewhere upstairs somebody was singing karaoke, an old sad love song, very loudly and very, very well.

Café Asahi was on a corner, on the ground floor of a narrow building with a staircase climbing up its side. It was not a bar. It was an old coffee shop, the kind called a kissaten, with red leather seats gone shiny with age, a glass case full of plastic models of the food, net curtains in the window, and a counter of dark polished wood. It smelled of toast and coffee and forty years of cigarettes. Most of the seats were empty.

Rei had never been inside. He had heard the name for three years. *I'm going to Asahi, don't wait up.* Tuesday nights, every Tuesday, and Gin coming home at ten smelling of coffee and somebody else's cigarettes and in a better mood than when he left.

[img char_matsuda-keiko.png | Matsuda Keiko]

A woman in her forties stood behind the counter in a white apron. She was compact and neat, with short hair gone grey early and cut close at the neck, steady hands, and a quiet, square face that did nothing it did not need to. She looked up when the door opened. Something in her face lifted, very slightly, and then went back down when she saw who it was.

He sat at the counter. He remembered Tanizaki's door, and Haruki's face when he had asked.

"Good evening," he said. "Sorry to bother you. How are you?"

She looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable.

"Tired," she said. "My feet hurt. The man who delivers the milk was late again, and he will be late again tomorrow, and I will say nothing again tomorrow." She picked up a glass and began to dry it. "Thank you for asking. Most people don't."

"I was told to ask for the usual."

She stopped drying the glass.

Then she turned and made a coffee, dark, in a thick white cup, without asking how he took it. She put it on the counter with a small plate. On the plate was a slice of thick white toast with butter and a little dish of sweet red bean paste. She did not put it in front of Rei. She put it in front of the empty stool beside him. Then she looked at it there, and moved it across.

"That's his," she said. Her voice was low and slow, like somebody who had decided to spend every word carefully. "Every Tuesday for three years. He never ordered. I never asked. I had it ready before he came through the door, and he drank it and ate the toast and wrote in his notebook and went home." She folded the cloth in half. "Last Tuesday he didn't come. I made it anyway. It went cold. I drank it at closing, which was stupid, because I don't drink coffee after six." She looked at Rei. "Is he sick?"

"I'm his brother."

"I know. You sit like him. Like you're ready to leave." She waited. "Is he sick?"

Rei had said it to Kumagai, and to Ono, and to the man at the CVD front desk, and to Itsuki, and to a Director on the fourth floor. It had not got any easier.

"He's missing," he said. "Since the twelfth. He went out in the night and he didn't come back."

Matsuda Keiko put the cloth down on the counter. She did not say she was sorry. She did not say anything for long enough that the karaoke upstairs finished its song and began another.

"The police?" she said.

"They wrote it down."

She nodded, as if that was about what she had expected from the police.

"Then I'll tell you something," she said, "and you can decide whether I'm a silly woman." She looked past him at the net curtains. "Last Tuesday. About eight o'clock. I was watching the door, because I am a silly woman. And he went past."

Rei was on his feet before he knew it. The stool rocked behind him.

"He went past on the other side of the street," said Keiko, "under the sign for the noodle place. Same grey jacket. He didn't look at the window. He didn't look at anything. He went toward the station." She shook her head once. "That was a week ago. Sit down. You won't catch him."

Rei sat down. His heart was going so hard that he could feel it in his fingers on the counter.

"Was he all right?"

She thought about it. He could see her choosing the words and putting back the ones she did not want.

"He was looking at everything," she said at last. "The noodle sign. The puddles. A girl's umbrella. A cat on a wall. He stopped in the middle of the pavement to look at a vending machine." She turned the glass in her hands. "Your brother walks down this street like an officer. Straight, quick, and he looks at one thing, which is my door. For three years, my door. This man looked at everything on the street except my door." She picked up the glass again and did not dry it. "I told myself it was somebody else. A man in the same jacket. Tokyo is full of grey jackets." She put the glass down. "I haven't been sleeping very well."

Rei looked out through the curtains at the wet street and the sign for the noodle place. He let the door in his chest open a crack, carefully, and felt nothing out there but rain and neon and a thousand people on their way to have a good time. A week was a long time. The street had forgotten.

*I believe your brother is alive,* Adachi had said, in his cold office.

Alive. Walking. Walking past the one place in Tokyo where somebody had his coffee waiting, and not coming in. Rei could not decide whether that was better than nothing, or worse.

"Thank you," he said. "For telling me."

"Don't thank me. I didn't do anything. I stood here and watched him go." Keiko reached under the counter. "That's not all. A man came on Sunday. Tall. A crooked smile. A dried flower in his jacket pocket, which I have never seen on a man before and hope never to see again. He said a young man would come in on Tuesday and ask for the usual, and I was to give him this."

It was a plain white envelope. Inside was a half sheet of notepaper, folded twice, in small, neat, slanting handwriting.

*His salary will keep going into his account, and you can't touch it. Nobody can, without him. Rent is due Thursday.*

*Lilac. Two streets over, second floor. Mama Sachi. She has a cup that won't stay thrown away. She'll pay cash. Don't tell her what you are. She won't care, but other people might.*

*Kit first. Then Lilac. Eat something. H.*

At the bottom, underlined, a number. *40,000.*

Rei read it, and thought of the bank, and the young woman in the neat uniform, and the sheet of paper with its list of documents in very small type. Haruki had known on Sunday. He had known that Gin paid the rent by hand on the twenty-sixth, every month, and that nobody else in the world could do it for him.

"Is it a job?" said Keiko.

"Something like that."

She did not ask what kind. "Your brother never told me what he did either," she said. "I decided he was something in insurance. He had the face for bad news." She reached under the counter again. "And this one's from him."

It was another envelope, older, soft at the corners. On the front, in a handwriting Rei knew better than his own, it said *For Rei. If he ever comes in.*

"He left it with me two years ago," said Keiko. "I asked him why on earth you would ever come in here. He didn't answer. I didn't open it."

Rei opened it. It was a short list, in pencil.

*Basic kit. Coarse salt, one kilo. Iron filings, cast iron only, NOT steel, one jar. White chalk, the soft kind. Red cotton thread. A small torch. Something warm to eat.*

Keiko could read it upside down. Rei saw her read it. Salt, iron, chalk, thread. He saw her decide not to ask about that either.

"Is it a recipe?" was all she said.

"Sort of."

He folded the list very carefully, along the old folds, and put it in his wallet next to Okada's card.

He ate the toast. It was the best toast he had ever had, thick and soft in the middle with crunchy edges, the butter melting down into the red bean paste. He had not realised until the second bite that he had eaten nothing since breakfast.

When he went to pay, she shook her head.

"He always paid a week ahead," she said. "Last Tuesday's was paid, and I drank it. So I owed him one." She took the empty cup. "Come next Tuesday."

"Why?"

"Because I'll make it anyway," said Matsuda Keiko, and went to the far end of the counter, and stood with her back to him, drying a glass that was already dry.

[break The kit]

He bought the kit on the way to the bar. The salt and the chalk and the torch came from an enormous discount store that sold everything from toilet paper to party costumes, with music blaring and shelves stacked up to the ceiling and a recorded voice singing the shop's name over and over until he wanted to scream. The thread came from a tiny sewing shop run by an old man who was closing up for the night and sold it to him through the half-lowered shutter, bent over, without a word. The iron filings were harder. He found them in the end in a hardware shop under the railway arches, in a small plastic jar meant for school science experiments, for four hundred yen. Gin's book said why it had to be cast iron. Steel is made in a way that burns the old thing out of the metal. Cast iron still has it in. Rei did not understand what *it* was. He bought two jars, to be safe.

His backpack was heavy now. It rattled a little when he walked. It was the first time since Gin left that he had felt like he was carrying something useful, instead of just carrying something.

[break Lilac]

Lilac was on the second floor of a narrow building, up a staircase so steep it was almost a ladder. It was one room. A counter, seven stools, a karaoke machine in the corner with its screen showing a beach at sunset. The walls were painted dark purple. Behind the counter stood a big woman of about sixty in a silk blouse with enormous gold earrings and a great deal of make-up. She looked at Rei, and at his rattling backpack, and sighed from the bottom of her soul.

"Haruki-chan sent me a child," she said.

"I'm twenty."

"That's what I said." She lit a cigarette with a gold lighter and blew the smoke at the ceiling. "Sit down. You want a drink? No? Good. You can't afford one." She tapped ash into a heavy glass ashtray. "Every night, two in the morning, my customers start fighting. Every single night. Good people. My regulars. Some of them twenty years. Two in the morning comes and they start shouting at each other about nothing. Last week one of them threw a glass at the wall. Last night two of them sat there crying and couldn't tell me why." She pointed at Rei with the cigarette. "And there's a cup that won't go away."

"A cup?"

She jabbed the cigarette over her shoulder. On the top shelf behind her, among the bottles, there was a plain white coffee mug with a small chip out of the rim. It looked like nothing at all.

"It was Ken-chan's," she said. "He worked this counter with me eleven years. He died last spring. His heart. In the street right outside, on his way in to work." Her voice did not change. Her hand did, a little, round the cigarette. "I threw that cup away in April. Put it in the rubbish myself, with my own hands. Next night it's back on the shelf. I threw it away again. Back again. In June I smashed it with a hammer, I swear to you on my mother. Next night, back on the shelf. Chip in the same place."

Rei looked at the mug. He let the door in his chest open a crack.

The mug changed.

It did not move. It did not glow. But it went heavy, the way a room goes heavy after people have been shouting in it. When he looked at it he felt something that did not belong to him. Shame. Hot, sick, stomach-turning shame. And under the shame, something smaller and sharper, like a stone in a shoe. A word that had never been said out loud.

He opened the door a little wider, and reached up, and took the mug down off the shelf.

[break What the cup knew]

Gin's book called it an object read. You hold a thing, and if enough feeling has soaked into it, it shows you what it remembers. Gin had written that most people who can do it think they are imagining things. Under that, underlined twice, *You are not imagining things.*

Rei held the mug in both hands, and the bar went away.

He was standing behind this same counter. It was night. It was the same purple room. But it was two years ago, and he was not himself. He was taller and heavier and older. His knees hurt. His hands were drying this same mug with a tea towel. And Mama Sachi was on the other side of the counter, younger, with smaller earrings, shouting. Shouting about money. About the till. Somebody had taken money out of the till. And he, the man he was being, Ken-chan, was shouting back that it wasn't him, how could she even think it was him, after all these years, after everything.

Except that it was him.

Rei felt it, in the hot sick place under his ribs. Ken-chan had taken the money. He had needed it for something. His mother. A hospital. Rei could not see the details, only the shame. And he had lied about it, and Sachi had believed him, in the end, and they had made up, and he had gone on working at her counter for another whole year. And every night, at two in the morning, when the bar was loud and warm and full of people he loved, he had looked at her across the counter and opened his mouth to tell her. And every night he had shut it again.

And then one spring evening his heart stopped in the street outside, on his way in to work. On his way, at last, to telling her.

Rei came back. He was standing in the purple room with the mug in his hands and his face was wet. His legs were shaking. The tunnel had started at the edges of his eyes again.

Mama Sachi was staring at him. Her cigarette had burned right down to her fingers and she had not noticed.

"What," she said. "What did you see."

He told her. He told her slowly. He did not try to make it gentle, because there was no gentle way to say it, and pretending there was would have been worse. Gin would not have tried either. When he finished she did not say anything for a long time. She put the cigarette out. She took the mug out of his hands. She held it in both hands, carefully, as if it might break.

"Eighty thousand yen," she said at last. "Two years ago. I knew. Of course I knew. I always knew." She laughed, and it came out wrong, half of it a sob. "I didn't care, you stupid man. I didn't *care*. I would have given it to you."

She put the mug down on the counter. Not up on the shelf. On the counter, in front of the empty stool where her customers sat.

"I forgive you, Ken-chan," she said to it. "You idiot. You complete idiot. I forgive you."

Rei felt it go. The heaviness went out of the room like air out of a balloon. The mug was just a mug. A white cup with a chip in it.

Mama Sachi wiped her face with the back of her hand and smeared her make-up halfway to her ear and did not care at all. She opened the till and counted out forty thousand yen in notes and pushed them across the counter. Then she counted out another ten thousand and slapped them down on top.

"That's for the crying," she said. "Nobody cries for my customers but me." She flapped her hand at him. "Go home, child. Eat something. Tell Haruki-chan you did good."

[break Thursday]

On Thursday Rei paid the rent.

Not all of it. He took fifty thousand yen in cash and thirty thousand from his own account to the property company's office in Akabane, in an envelope, and stood at the counter and asked very politely whether he could bring the last four thousand the following week. A tired man in a short-sleeved shirt looked at him, and at the envelope, and at the rain on Rei's shoulders, and wrote something on a form, and said yes. Just this once.

From Akabane he went to the police station, and up the stairs to the second floor, and put a letter on Kumagai's desk.

Kumagai read it with his glasses pushed up on his forehead. He read it twice, which for Kumagai was a lot.

"Eighteen months," he said. "You've got a good eye, Uedera. Everybody says so. Even the ones who don't say thank you for the tea."

"I need the days," said Rei.

Kumagai put the letter down and squared it with the edge of the desk. "This is about your brother."

"Yes."

"He hasn't turned up."

"No."

Kumagai looked out of the window for a while, at the rain on the car park. He did not say he had been wrong. He was not a man who said that kind of thing. But when he looked back, the patient voice was gone.

"If a name crosses this desk," he said, "or a description. Anything. I'll call you."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Go and find him." Kumagai pulled his glasses back down and picked up the next file. "And stop making the tea so strong. Nobody ever told you that either."

Rei walked out of the station with eleven hundred yen in his wallet and a backpack full of salt and iron, and no job. He felt terrified. He felt light. He felt, for the first time, a little bit like his brother.

That night at the kitchen table he made a list on the back of the rent receipt. Everything he had that might be useful. The key. Gin's book. Okada's card. The kit. A Director who believed Gin was alive. Kato, who was watching. Itsuki. Haruki. A café in Ni-chōme where a woman would make Gin's coffee anyway. A bakery in Jūjō. An old man with a garden full of weeds who would not give him a third cup of tea.

And one more thing, which he wrote and then sat looking at for a long time. *A man in a grey jacket, looking at everything, going past a window.*

It was not much. It was more than he had had a week ago.

At the bottom he added one more line.

*Go back to Tanizaki on Saturday. Don't ask him for anything.*
