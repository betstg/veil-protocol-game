#title Eviction

The letter came on the fourth of July, in a long white envelope with a window, and it was addressed to Gin.

Rei found it in the post box at the bottom of the stairs, between a pizza menu and a flyer for a gym, and knew what it was before he opened it, the way you know what a phone call is before you pick it up. The name of the management company was printed in the top corner in small blue letters. *Kita Residential Services.* He had seen that name twice a year his whole life, on the renewal papers Gin signed at the kitchen table with his reading face on, saying *every two years they rob us, every two years we let them*.

He opened it on the stairs.

It was very polite. Japanese letters from companies always are. It thanked Uedera Gin-sama for his continued residence. It reminded him that his tenancy agreement would expire on the thirty-first of July. It noted, with regret, that the company had been unable to reach him by telephone since the previous autumn, and that rent for the past ten months had been paid in cash at the office counter by a person other than the contract holder. It explained, with deep apology, that under the terms of the agreement, the renewal could only be signed by the contract holder in person. Failing which, the company would be obliged to ask the occupants to vacate the property by the thirty-first of August.

It hoped for his understanding.

Rei sat down on the stairs.

Gorō dropped off the railing above him and landed on the step beside his foot, and tilted his head, and looked at the letter with the black eye.

"They want Gin," said Rei, and folded the letter in half, and in half again, the way Tanizaki folded his newspaper. "Get in line."

[break The counter]

The office of Kita Residential Services was above a dry cleaner's near Ōji station, up a narrow staircase that smelled of steam and chemicals. Rei went on his lunch break in his suit. He had thought the suit would help. It did not help.

The woman behind the counter was about fifty, with reading glasses on a chain and a cardigan the colour of weak tea, and she was kind to him, which was worse. She listened to the whole thing. She looked at his staff card from the Division, which said he was a Ministry employee and said nothing else, and she nodded, and she typed something into her computer, and she sighed.

"I'm sorry, Uedera-san," she said, and took her glasses off and let them hang. "I can see you've been paying. Every month. On time. That's not the problem." She tapped the screen with one finger. "The problem is your brother's name is on the contract and your brother isn't here. The owner's very strict about it. If the contract holder can't be reached for a renewal, the property goes back to the owner. That's the rule."

Rei kept his hands flat on the counter so they would not do anything. "Can I sign a new one? In my name?"

"You can apply." She looked at him over the counter, at the suit, at his face, and he watched her do the arithmetic. "A new contract is a new contract. Deposit, two months. Key money, one month. Agency fee, one month. Guarantor company, half a month. First month's rent in advance." She wrote the numbers on a pad as she said them, in neat small figures, and turned the pad round so he could read it. "And you'd need two years of proof of income. Or a guarantor with a steady job who'll sign for you."

He looked at the number at the bottom of the pad.

Four hundred and sixty-two thousand yen.

He had thirty-one thousand in the bank. He had been paying the rent and the electricity and the gas and Gorō's eggs and the train out of a trainee's wage that the Division paid on the twenty-fifth, and there had never been anything left over on the twenty-fourth.

Rei folded the corner of the pad down with his thumb so he would not have to look at it. "By when?"

The woman put her glasses back on, which seemed to be what she did when she did not want to look at somebody. "The thirty-first of July for the application. If you're approved." She paused. "I'll hold the file as long as I can. I'm sorry. I really am. I remember your brother. He used to bring us those little cakes from the place in Jūjō every New Year. Nobody else does that."

[break What it was]

He did not tell Itsuki. He did not tell Kato. He sat at the kitchen table that night with the letter and the pad and a calculator, and Gorō on the back of Gin's chair, and he looked at the walls.

He could see the wards now. That was the thing. Ever since Uruma had taught him to read, he could not stop seeing them. The long grey line round the kitchen window, layer on layer, nineteen years of *not here, not this one, not him*. The one under the hook by the door. *Come home.* The one inside the lid of the rice cooker. *Eat.*

They were written on the walls. On the concrete. On the boards under the tatami. You could not take them with you. You could not peel them off and fold them into a box and carry them to some room in Itabashi with a window onto a ventilation shaft.

If they lost the flat, they lost all of it. Every word Gin had ever written in the night.

And if Gin came home, he would come home to the walkway and the hook by the door and a stranger's name on the post box.

Rei put his forehead down on the kitchen table.

"Okay," he said into the wood. "Okay. Okay."

Gorō walked down the back of the chair and along the table and pecked him once, quite hard, on the top of the head.

[break Keiko]

Café Asahi had not changed. It never would. The red leather seats were still shiny, the plastic food in the window was still slightly dusty, and Matsuda Keiko was still behind the counter in her white apron, drying a glass that was already dry.

She looked at him as he came in, and he felt her read him, all the way down, the way she had the first time.

She set down the glass she was drying. "How much?"

Rei sat on a stool at the counter. "Four hundred and sixty thousand. By the end of the month."

Keiko put the glass down and picked up another one. "That's not a gig. That's a career."

Rei looked at the plastic food in the window. "I know."

"The small stuff won't get you there. Lost cats and crying mugs and grandmothers in the cupboard. Ten thousand here, thirty there." She held the glass up to the light, looked through it at him. "You'd need the other list."

He waited. She dried the glass. It was a long time.

"There's a list," she said finally. "The Grid keeps it. Jobs the Division won't touch because they're too small to be worth a field team, or too messy, or because somebody important owns the building and doesn't want men in grey suits on the stairs. The pay is good because the jobs are bad." She put the glass down. "People get hurt on that list. Your brother never took anything off it. He said he had a reason not to get hurt."

Rei put both hands round the empty cup she had not filled. "He's not here."

Keiko looked at him for a long moment over the top of the counter. Then she reached under it and took out an old cardboard index box, the kind libraries used to have, and flicked through the cards with one thumb, and pulled one out, and put it on the counter in front of him face down.

"Not alone," she said, and kept two fingers on the back of the card. "You don't go on this one alone. There's a man who'll be there. He's been doing these for twenty years and he's still alive, which tells you most of what you need to know. You do what he says. You don't argue." She lifted her fingers. "And you don't tell Haruki I gave you this. He'll stop coming in for coffee."

Rei turned the card over.

*Uguisudani. Hotel Rosa, Room 304. Demolition stalled 3 wks. 4 workers injured (falls). Owner pays 300,000 on clearance. Majima attending.*

[break Majima]

The Hotel Rosa was a love hotel in the narrow streets behind Uguisudani station, where the love hotels stood shoulder to shoulder with their coloured signs and their curtained car park entrances and their little boards out front with prices for a REST and a STAY. It had been closed for a year. The sign over the door had lost most of its letters, so it said HO EL OS. Scaffolding had gone up round the whole building, green netting hung from top to bottom, and a demolition company's board had been fixed to the fence with a date on it that was three weeks gone.

There was a man sitting on an upturned crate by the gate, and a dog lying at his feet.

He was about forty, tall and dense, built the way a tool is built, for use, with short black hair going grey at the temples and a face like a closed door. He wore dark clothes, worn soft, nothing that would catch on anything. He sat with his weight a little forward, as if he might have to stand at any moment and did not mind. He had a plastic convenience-store bag beside him with two cans of coffee in it.

The dog was old. A big brindled mongrel with a grey muzzle and a torn ear and a long pale scar across its shoulder where the fur had never grown back. It lifted its head when Rei turned into the street, looked at him for a long, flat, considering moment, and put its head down again on its paws.

The man watched Rei come up the street without moving anything except his eyes. They were dark brown and they did not stop working, not for a second, the whole time.

He took his hand off the dog's back. "Keiko's kid."

Rei stopped at the fence. "Uedera Rei."

"I know who you are." The man looked him up and down, the suit, the bag, the latch on the wrist. Something moved in his face and was put away again. "You look like him. Round the mouth. He was a pain in the arse too." He reached into the bag without looking, took out one of the cans of coffee and held it out. "Majima. Drink that. It's going to be a long night."

Rei took the can. It was hot. "You knew my brother?"

"Everyone on the list knew your brother. He was the one who wouldn't come on the list." Majima reached down without looking and rubbed the dog's torn ear once with his thumb. "Room three-oh-four. Four men fell off the scaffolding outside that window in three weeks. One's still in hospital with his back broken. The owner wants it down by August so he can put up flats. The Division looked at it and said it was a Class D residual, not worth a team, and went home." He stood up. He was taller than he had looked sitting down, and the dog stood up with him. "It's not a Class D."

Rei looked up where he was looking. "How do you know?"

Majima looked at the building. At the third floor. At the one window with no netting over it, a square of black in all that green.

"Because I've been doing this for twenty years," he said. "And I don't take cases that interest me anymore. And this one interests me." He looked at Rei, and the dark eyes sharpened, and he asked one question. "When you walk past a building like that, what do you hear?"

Rei looked up at the black window. "Somebody saying *come in*."

Majima held his eyes for a moment longer. Then he picked up the bag. "Come on."

[break Room 304]

Inside, the Hotel Rosa smelled of damp carpet and old perfume and something underneath that Rei did not want to name. The lift was dead. They went up the stairs with torches. On the second-floor landing there was a vending machine that had once sold condoms and canned tea, gutted, its glass front smashed.

On the third floor, the corridor was cold.

Not cool. Cold. July in Tokyo, thirty-one degrees at nine at night, and their breath came out white. The carpet was red, with a pattern of gold hearts. The doors were pink. Halfway down, one of them stood open.

Rei felt it before he saw it. The latch on his wrist went cold as metal in winter. He put his free hand on it.

"Don't open it," said Majima, without turning round. "Not yet. Tell me what you feel with it shut."

Rei stood still. He let the cold come up through his feet, the way Grandmother Mitsu had taught him, the way Mio had taught him to read rivers. Something in the room ahead. Something that had been in there for a long time, and had been one thing once, and had gone over into something else.

"Somebody died in there," he said slowly. "A long time ago. A woman. And she waited. And somebody fed her." He frowned. "Something fed her. Something came along and found her waiting and leaned on her, the way a Hunger leans on a gambler. And now she's not her anymore. It's not her. It's the thing. Wearing her waiting like a coat."

Majima turned his head and looked at him for a long moment in the torchlight.

"Huh," he said, and nothing else, and turned back to the door.

He put his hand inside his jacket, over his heart, and held it there for a second, the way a man checks his wallet is still in his pocket before he gets on a crowded train. Then he put down the bag and rolled his shoulders, and Rei felt the air in the corridor change. A pressure, heavy and dull, like the moment before a thunderstorm. The torch in Rei's hand dipped by itself, as if someone had pushed on it gently from above.

"Telekinesis," said Majima, seeing his face. He flexed the fingers of his right hand. "Seven. Mostly I hit things, and the hit lands on the inside. Tonight I hold things." He picked up his torch again. "You get it out of her. I'll stop the building falling on us while you do it. If I say run, you run. You don't look back for me. Clear?"

Rei swallowed. "Clear."

Majima turned the torch so it lit Rei's face. "Say it like you mean it."

Rei met his eyes. "Clear."

[break What waited]

The room was small and pink and furnished for one purpose. A round bed with no mattress left on it. A mirror on the ceiling, cracked. A little bathroom with a glass wall. A window onto the scaffolding, black, with no netting, the city lights beyond it smeared and orange.

There was a woman sitting on the edge of the bare bed frame.

She was young, or had been. Twenty-five, perhaps. In a white blouse and a dark skirt, the kind of thing office girls wore in the eighties, with big shoulders and a thin belt. Her hair was permed. She was looking at the door with enormous hope on her face, the hope of someone who has been told *wait here, I'll be right back*. And she had been waiting since 1987, and her face had not changed in all that time, and that was the most horrible thing Rei had ever seen.

Behind her, wrapped round her, there was something else.

Rei could only see it with the latch half up. A shape like wet black smoke, or like hair, long and dark and endless, coiled round her shoulders and her waist and her throat. It had no face. It had a mouth, somewhere. He could feel the mouth. It was feeding on her waiting the way a leech feeds, slowly, gently, so the host does not notice. And every time a man came near the window, out on the scaffolding, it reached out through her hope with one long black tendril and pulled, just a little. *Come in. Come in. Are you him. Are you the one.*

And the men lost their footing.

"I see it," said Rei. His voice was very quiet. "It's a Longing. Small. But it's been eating for thirty years. It's fat."

"Fat's bad," said Majima from the door, behind him, very calm. "Fat means it'll fight."

The woman's head turned toward them. Slowly. All the hope in her face turned toward Rei, bright and terrible. *Are you him.*

And the black thing behind her opened.

[break Down]

Afterwards, Rei could never remember the order of it properly.

He remembered the ceiling cracking. The mirror up there splitting in a long jagged line and then coming down all at once in a shower of glass, and stopping, a foot above his head, hanging in the air, a thousand pieces of pink-lit glass turning slowly like snow in a globe. Majima in the doorway with his arm up and his teeth bared and a vein standing out on his forehead like a cord.

He remembered the black tendrils coming at him, not fast, slow and soft and certain, the way water comes up a beach. One went round his ankle. One went round his waist. One went into his chest, right through his shirt, cold, and he felt it touch the hollow place in him where Gin should be and taste it.

*Oh,* it said. *Oh. You are waiting too.*

He remembered the window. The black square of it, behind the woman. The scaffolding. The street, three floors down, with the coloured signs. The tendril pulling him toward it, gently, gently, and the part of him that was so tired thinking *yes*.

"*Uedera,*" said Majima, very far away. "*Kid.* Don't you fucking dare."

And he remembered the latch.

He did not lift it. He shut it. All the way. Hard. The way Gin had taught him at the kitchen table when he was eight, the door in his chest slammed so hard the hinges rang. And then, in the dark behind his own closed door, he reached for the iron filings in his jacket pocket, a whole handful, cold and gritty, and threw them, not at the black thing, but at the woman.

At her hands. Folded in her lap. Waiting.

The iron went through the black like shot through paper. It landed on her hands. And for one second, she looked down at them, surprised, the way you look down when someone touches your hand in a crowd.

Rei let his hand drop to his side. "He's not coming."

He said it to her. Not to the thing. To her. In his own voice. Kindly. The way he had said it to the old woman outside the watch shop.

"He's not coming back. He didn't come back in 1987, and he won't now. I'm sorry. I'm really sorry. But you don't have to wait anymore. You can stop."

The woman looked at him for a long time.

And the hope went out of her face. Not all at once. Slowly. Like a light on a dimmer switch. And with it, all the black thing's food. All of it. Thirty years of waiting, gone in a second.

The black thing screamed.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, inside the skull, behind the eyes. Rei's nose started bleeding. The tendrils round him went thin and grey and papery, like burnt hair, and fell apart, and the shape behind the woman shrank and shrank, and came apart in pieces like ash going up a chimney, and was gone.

The woman was still sitting on the bed.

She looked tired. Ordinary. A young woman in an old blouse who has been stood up and is about to go home.

She stood. She smoothed her skirt. She looked round the room, at the pink walls and the cracked mirror and the glass hanging in the air, as if she did not know how she had come to be there.

She looked at Rei, and bowed, very slightly. Then she walked to the door, past Majima, out into the cold red corridor, and was gone.

The glass came down.

Majima let it fall where they were not. It went everywhere, across the bed and the floor and the window ledge, a long bright glittering crash, and then silence.

[break After]

They sat on the stairs. Majima had his head against the wall and his eyes closed. His nose was bleeding too, from one side, and he did not seem to have noticed. His right hand was shaking. He held it with his left.

"Iron at the host," he said, without opening his eyes. "Not at the demon. Where'd you learn that?"

Rei wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Nowhere. It just seemed like she was the one who needed to stop. Not it."

Majima opened his eyes and looked at him.

"Your brother did that," he said. "Once. I saw him do it once. Years ago. The only job I ever did with him. Exactly that." The dog had come up the stairs at some point and was lying across Majima's feet, and he put his shaking hand on its back and left it there. "I asked him where he learned it. He said the same thing. Word for word."

They went down. Outside, the street was hot and loud and bright, and someone was laughing in the car park of the hotel next door. Majima took a thick envelope out of his inside pocket, counted it without looking, split it, and handed Rei half.

Rei stared at it. "That's a hundred and fifty."

Majima licked his thumb and closed the other envelope. "That's half."

Rei held it out, back toward him. "You did most of it."

"I held the ceiling." Majima did not look at him. He was looking up at the HO EL OS sign. "Holding a ceiling's easy. You talked her out." He folded the empty envelope and put it away. "Half. I don't like being owed."

Rei put the envelope in his jacket. It was heavy. It was a hundred and fifty thousand yen. A third of the way.

He looked at the dog, not at the man. "Keiko says you go round Taitō every week."

"Thursdays." Majima did not look at him. "Ueno to Asakusa. Checking things. Old men in parks. Spots that go bad." He clicked his tongue, and the old dog got up stiffly. "Mostly boring."

Rei pushed his hands into his pockets. "Can I come?"

Majima turned his head, slowly, and looked at him with his closed-door face for a long time.

"It doesn't pay," he said.

Rei shrugged, and the envelope shifted against his ribs. "I know."

"It's boring." Majima bent and clipped a lead to the dog's collar.

"I don't mind." Rei crouched and held his hand out, and the old dog sniffed it once, thoroughly, and licked his knuckles, and turned away.

Majima watched that. He did not say anything about it. He put the empty coffee cans in the convenience-store bag and picked the bag up.

"Seven o'clock," he said. "Ueno Park. By the statue of the man with the dog. Don't be late." He walked away up the street with his shoulders hunched, and then stopped, and turned half round. "And buy some better shoes. You can't run in those."

[break The window]

On the way home, he got off the train at Ueno and walked down the side street behind the station where the pawn shop was. It was closed. The shutters were down. In the little lit window beside them, among the rings and the handbags and the camera lenses, on squares of faded blue velvet, there were three silver watches with black straps. None of them was Gin's. Gin's was in the safe at the back, in a paper envelope with a number on it, because Rei had never once missed a payment.

He had paid the interest on it every month since September. Three thousand yen, on the first, at the counter, to an old man who never asked him why. The ticket was in the back of Gin's book.

He stood in front of the window for a long time anyway, looking at the watches that were not Gin's, with a hundred and fifty thousand yen in his jacket.

Then he went home, and put the envelope in the rice cooker, under the lid that said *Eat*, and went to bed.
