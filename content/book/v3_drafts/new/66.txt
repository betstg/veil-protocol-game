#title Immunity

On the morning of the sixteenth, Moriya was waiting for him at the top of the stairs to Containment, leaning on one crutch, with a cardboard folder under his other arm.

"You came in," he said.

"Shimizu said everyone in for Obon." Rei had slept four hours, on Haruki's sofa in the back room of the bar, with Gorō on the arm of it and Kato asleep on the floor with his coat over him. He felt washed and wrung out and hung up to dry. The door in his chest still swung. But slowly now. Like a door in a breeze, not a gale. "And Okada said if I didn't, she'd come and get me."

"She would." Moriya looked at him, slowly, all the way through, as he looked at everyone. "All right. Come and see something. Then I'm going to ask you to come upstairs with me. You can say no." He turned on the crutch. "I'd rather you didn't."

[break The three]

The three officers from Public Confidence had been in Containment since the night of the twelfth. Okada had brought them in herself, in the back of a field car, two men and a woman, sitting very upright on the back seat with their faces empty, and nobody upstairs had come to ask for them. That was the first thing Moriya told him, on the way down the long polished corridor, thump, swing, thump, swing.

"Four days," he said. "Not one phone call. Not one form. Their own office hasn't asked where they are." He stopped outside a door on the right, four down from Room Nine. "When your own people don't ask where you are, it's because they already know."

Through the little square window, the woman who had sat down in the road and cried was sitting on the edge of the white bed. She had been given grey Containment pyjamas. Her hair was tidy. Her hands were in her lap. She was looking at the wall.

"Watch," said Moriya.

He unlocked the door and went in on his crutch, and the woman did not look at him. He put the crutch against the wall and stood beside her on his good leg and laid two fingers on the side of her neck, where the big vein is, as he had laid them on Tendo's in Sena's office. He closed his eyes. His face went grey with concentration.

"Open your door," he said, without opening his. "A finger. Look where I'm looking."

Rei lifted the latch.

Through Moriya's fingers, through the body, he felt what Moriya felt. A woman. Tired. Forty-five, perhaps. Not hollow, not like the man in the east corridor. Still there. But behind her breastbone, threaded through her like a stitch through cloth, there was something thin and grey and taut, running out of her and away. West. Across the city. Like the thread that ran out of Watanabe's chest toward the white house.

"She's been drinking the tea," said Rei slowly.

"For a year, I think. Maybe more." Moriya took his fingers away and opened his eyes. "Not enough to empty her. Just enough to tie her on. All three of them. Same thread. Same direction." He picked up his crutch. "These are Public Confidence officers. Field-cleared. They go into every incident in this city before the ambulances do. And every one of them has a string on them going back to Asagaya."

The woman on the bed turned her head and looked at Rei. Her eyes were small and brown and tired and quite ordinary.

"I'm sorry," she said, in a small voice. "About the old man. I didn't want to. I don't know why I did." She looked at her hands. "I don't know why I do things any more."

[break The paper]

Sena was waiting for them in the corridor when they came out. He was standing quite still beside the door, with a thin folder under his arm, in his grey suit, with his hair exactly where it should be. He bowed to Moriya. A small exact correct bow.

"I heard you'd come down," he said. "I thought you'd want this before you went up."

He took a long strip of white paper out of the folder, a little crumpled, with one word written on it over and over in black ink. *Closed.* He held it up to the light from the ceiling. Inside the paper, very faint, there was a pattern. A small round crest, like a flower seen from above.

"Okada brought me this from the gatepost," said Sena. "This isn't Division paper. Division seal stock is made in Saitama and it's white all the way through. This is mulberry, hand-laid, from a mill outside Kyoto that has sold to exactly nine buyers for two hundred years. All of them old houses." He lowered the strip. "My family is one of them. I recognised the crest because I've been writing on paper like this since I was four."

"Is it yours?" said Moriya.

"No. Different crest. I know whose it is. It's in my report." Sena put the strip back in the folder and squared it with one fingertip. "A Tower family bought this paper, and Public Confidence used it on a civilian's gate, and it doesn't appear anywhere in Public Confidence's procurement records. Somebody is supplying that office off the books. I've filed that this morning." He looked at Rei. "And Katagiri's memo is on file. Reason code forty-one. The order to stop looking for your brother came from the same office. I've filed that too."

He took a second form out of the folder. Grey. With the Internal Affairs crest at the top. It was filled in, all of it, in small precise black ink, except the signature.

"This designates you as a material witness in an open Internal Affairs matter," he said. "The death of a civilian during an unauthorised operation in Kita ward on the twelfth of August. While it's open, no office of the Division may question you, transfer you, reassess you or contain you without the written approval of Internal Affairs." He uncapped a pen and signed it, a short, plain, quick signature. "It was written in 1952 by somebody who had seen what happens to witnesses. Nobody's used it in eleven years." He handed it to Moriya, not to Rei. "Take that upstairs with you. She'll read it. She reads everything."

Rei looked at him. "Why?"

Sena turned his head and looked at him. For a second something moved in his face. Something careful.

"I'm an Auditor," he said. "I write down what's true. It doesn't matter whose house it embarrasses." He paused. "And my family voted to leave your brother's file closed, last November, in Ōtemachi. I've been thinking about that." Another pause. "I'd like it on the record that I've stopped thinking about it and started doing something."

He bowed, the same small exact bow, and walked away down the corridor.

[break The glass office]

Public Confidence was on the top floor, behind glass doors with nothing written on them. Hagiwara Mizuki's office was at the end of a long quiet carpet, all glass on two sides, and when they came in she was standing at the window with her back to the door and the whole of Tokyo spread out below her in the morning heat, white and shimmering.

She turned round.

She was a woman of about fifty, in a grey suit cut so exactly that it looked as if it had been poured on, with short grey hair and no jewellery except a thin watch. Rei had seen her once before, at the families' meeting in Ōtemachi in November, standing in front of a screen, saying his brother's name in a voice that did not change at all.

She looked at Rei. Then she looked at Moriya, one step behind him, in his slept-in suit, on his crutch, with a cardboard folder under his arm.

For one second, Rei thought he saw something in her face. Like a woman who has opened her front door expecting the post and found a policeman.

Then she smiled. The smile arrived exactly on time, exactly the right size, and went no further than her mouth.

"Moriya-san," she said. "Uedera-san. I didn't expect you. Sit down, please."

Moriya did not sit. He held out the grey form. "Uedera-san is a witness in an open Internal Affairs matter. He doesn't speak to other offices without a Field officer present. That's me."

Hagiwara did not take the form. She looked at it in his hand for a long moment, reading it from where she stood. Then she sat down behind a long white desk with nothing on it except a fountain pen, and folded her hands, and looked at Rei.

"I'm sorry about Tanizaki Seiichi," she said. Her voice was low and calm and perfectly pleasant. "It was not intended. My officers were sent to secure a residence used by an unregistered entity. Not to engage civilians. Something interfered."

"Your officers," said Moriya, "are tied to the same thing that interfered. All three of them. I put my hand on them this morning." He laid the folder on the white desk and opened it. "You can send a doctor down to check. I'd like you to. I'd like it on the record that you did."

Hagiwara looked at the folder. She did not touch it.

"I'm not your enemy, Uedera-san," she said, still looking at Rei. "I know you think I am. I know what they say about this office downstairs. But I'm the only person in this building who is trying to solve the actual problem." She picked up the fountain pen and turned it in her fingers, not uncapping it. "Every year more things come through. Every year more people like you are born who can see them, and hear them, and let them in. And every year the Division gives a few more of you a lanyard and a crow and sends you out to stand in the gaps with a bucket of salt." She set the pen down. "That's not a solution. That's a bucket."

"And yours is closing it," said Rei. His voice came out very quiet.

The smile did not move. But the hand on the desk went still.

"Closing everything," said Rei. "With strips of paper. Doors and walls. You don't care which." He looked at her. "You sealed an old man's house. Forty-one years of him writing *not here* on every board, so people could come and drink tea and be safe, and you put *closed* on top of it, and it broke. And something walked in through the place where it broke."

Hagiwara looked at him for a long time.

"Your brother asked questions too," she said at last. Very softly. "Last summer. About a circle in Asagaya. About who was paying for whose paper. I reported him. The Director put my report in a drawer." Her eyes went to Moriya, and back. "Two weeks later, something took him off a doorstep. If he had been contained then, under my authority, he would be safe now. In a white room, asleep, with a guard on the door." She leaned forward. "I could still bring him in, Uedera-san. Safely. All I need is for you to tell me where he goes."

Rei thought of a long white room in Asagaya, and a woman in a grey cardigan saying *we'll hold it still for you*.

He thought of the strips of paper on Tanizaki's gate.

*They're the same,* he thought. Not the same reasons. The same offer. *Put him in a white room. Let me hold him still.*

He kept his hands flat on his knees. "No."

[break What she did not know]

Moriya turned a page in the folder.

"There's a deputy in this office," he said. "Nishimura. He drew the containment order for the twelfth. He chose the three officers. He picked up the paper from the Kyoto mill himself, in person, in June, on a day he was signed off sick." He laid a sheet on the white desk, a printout of a commuter pass record with one station circled in red pencil, again and again, down the whole page. "And for fourteen months, every second Sunday, his pass has gone through the gates at Asagaya at a quarter to four and come back out at six. Katagiri pulled it last night. She says she'd have found it a year ago if anybody had asked her to look."

Hagiwara did not look at the sheet.

Rei watched her not look at it. And he felt something. Faint. So faint. Like a sound in another room. Coming off her, the way things came off people now that the door in his chest would not quite close.

It was not guilt. It was not fear. It was something much older and much smaller, pushed down so hard and for so long that it had nearly gone. A child, a long time ago, in a room at night, seeing something at the end of the bed, and lying very still, and telling herself it was nothing. It was a draught. It was the curtain. It was nothing.

He did not mean to say it.

"You didn't know," he said quietly. "About Nishimura. You didn't know what he was doing with your orders."

Hagiwara's shoulders went very still.

"You only wanted it shut," said Rei. "You've always only wanted it shut. You feel them too. At the edges of rooms. And you call it a draught."

She did not answer. She sat at the long white desk with her hands folded beside the fountain pen, and the fingers of her right hand closed slowly into a fist, and opened again.

Then she reached out and drew the printout toward her, and looked at the circled line for a long time.

"Moriya-san," she said. Her voice was perfectly even. "Nishimura is in the building. He's on this floor. Third door on the left." She picked up the telephone on the desk and pressed one button. "This is Hagiwara. Close the glass doors on the top floor. Nobody leaves until Internal Affairs arrives." She put the phone down. She looked at Moriya. "I'd be grateful if you'd ask Fujiwara-Kage-san to come up himself."

"He's already on his way," said Moriya.

Hagiwara nodded once.

"I'll want it on the record," she said, "that I opened my own office to Internal Affairs before I was asked." She turned her chair a little toward the window, toward the white shimmering city. "And I'll want it on the record that I still believe I'm right. Somebody has to shut the door."

"It'll be on the record," said Moriya. "Both things. They're both accurate."

[break The lift]

In the lift, going down, very fast, so fast Rei's ears popped, Moriya stood with the folder under his arm and his eyes on the numbers.

"That's part of it," he said. "Not all of it. Nishimura didn't buy paper from a Tower family on a deputy's salary. Somebody in one of those houses is drinking the same tea." He did not look at Rei. "Sena will find out who. It'll take him weeks. He'll enjoy every minute."

"We haven't got weeks."

"No." Moriya watched the numbers go down. "We've got tonight." The lift slowed. "What you said up there. About the draught. I'm going to leave that out of the report. Some things are true and nobody else's business." A pause. "But be careful. People who've spent their whole lives telling themselves it's a draught don't like being told it's a ghost. Sometimes they close the window harder. Sometimes they open it."

The doors opened on the Operations floor.

Kato was standing right outside the lift, white-faced, with his phone in his hand.

"Chen Lian rang," he said. "From the Archive. It's Watanabe." He swallowed. "She says come now. She says he's asking for you."
