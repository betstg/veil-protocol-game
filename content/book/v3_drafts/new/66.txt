#title Immunity

Moriya's office had burned down in May, and Internal Affairs had been given the old map room on the fifth floor while the builders argued about the wiring. It still had the map cabinets. Long, low, grey, with wide flat drawers, and on top of them, now, files. Stacks of them. And a laptop, and a kettle, and a pot noodle with a pair of chopsticks standing up in it, and a child's drawing in crayon, pinned to the wall above the desk with a drawing pin. A house with a red roof. A man with brown hair and a woman in a triangle dress and two small people between them. A very large sun.

Moriya sat under the drawing with his pale grey eyes on the door.

"Sit down, Uedera," he said. "Close the door. Thank you for coming back to work."

Rei sat. It was Tuesday, the second of August. He had slept for most of Monday, at Itsuki's, on a mattress on the studio floor among the paintings, with Gorō on the windowsill and the smell of turpentine and Itsuki snoring. He felt like something that had been washed and wrung out and hung up to dry. The door in his chest still swung. But slowly now. Like a door in a breeze, not a gale.

"I'm going to show you some things," said Moriya. "Then I'm going to ask you to do something. You can say no." He did not smile. He almost never smiled at work. "I'd rather you didn't."

He opened a cardboard folder and took out three things and laid them on the desk in a row, very neatly, the way he did everything.

A Division lanyard. Blue and grey, the Marunouchi colours, with a photo card on it of a man Rei had last seen standing in the middle of Tanizaki's lane, doing nothing, with his face empty.

A long strip of white paper, a little crumpled, with one word written on it over and over in black ink. *Closed.*

And a single sheet of thick cream paper in a clear plastic sleeve. A form. Printed, mostly, in neat black type. With a date and an address filled in by hand in a beautiful even script, and a signature at the bottom in the same hand, long and slow and elegant.

"Okada brought me the lanyard," said Moriya. "The night it happened. From the lane. With a name." He touched the edge of the form, not the form itself. "That's a Public Confidence containment order. Three officers and a vehicle. Signed by the Director of Public Confidence personally. I requisitioned it from Marunouchi the next morning under Internal Affairs authority. They didn't want to give it to me. They had to." He looked up. "Do you know what's interesting about it?"

Rei shook his head.

"The form." Moriya turned it, very slightly, so the bottom corner caught the light. There was a tiny printed number there. A code. "That's a version number. Every Division form has one. That version was withdrawn on the first of June. Replaced. Every office was told to destroy their old stock." He sat back. "This one was printed in April. And then it sat in a drawer in Marunouchi, filled in, all of it, except the date and the address. For three months. Waiting."

Rei's mouth had gone dry. "Waiting for what?"

"For somebody to tell her where your brother would be." Moriya's voice did not change. "She didn't make that order on Thursday. She made it in the spring. All she needed was a time and a place." He folded his hands. "And on Thursday the twenty-first of July, at five o'clock in the afternoon, somebody went up twenty-six floors and gave her one."

[break What the paper said]

Rei looked at the form for a long time.

He looked at the signature and not at Moriya. "You want me to read it."

"I want you to read it," said Moriya. "I already know. The Marunouchi visitor log is accurate. Records usually are." He took the form out of its plastic sleeve and laid it on the desk in front of Rei. "But I'd rather you found out from the paper than from me. Paper doesn't have an opinion about it. I do."

Rei put his hand on the form.

He opened the latch a finger's width. The way he had once read a watch at the Assessment floor until it burned his hand.

It came up through the paper at once. It always did with paper. Paper held things. Thick cream paper, very expensive, from a mill in the mountains somewhere. A drawer. Dark. Cool. For a long time. A woman's hand taking it out. Long fingers, short clean nails. A fountain pen. The nib on the paper, slow, scratching, like a small animal in a wall. A feeling coming off the hand like cold off a window. Not cruelty. Not anger. Something flat and tired and certain, the feeling of someone closing a door very quietly so as not to wake a child.

And on the other side of the desk, across a long white surface, another pair of hands.

Holding a bag. On a pair of knees in a dark skirt. Holding it so tight that the knuckles had gone white. A woman's hands. Shorter fingers. A little rough at the tips, the way hands go when they work with clay. A faint smell, under everything, of cat. And coming off them, so strong that it went up Rei's arm like a burn, a feeling he knew, because he had felt it in his own chest for eleven months. *Come home. Come home. Please come home. I'll do anything. Anything. Just come home.*

And under that, very small, very far down, a picture. A brown bowl. Mended with gold.

Rei took his hand off the paper.

He sat in the old map room with his hand flat on the desk beside the form and did not say anything for a long time. Moriya did not say anything either. He sat very still under his daughter's drawing and watched Rei's face the way he watched everything, and he did not look away, and he did not pretend not to see.

"She came to the funeral," said Rei at last. His voice was quite steady. "I thanked her. For Kuga."

Moriya nodded once. "I know."

Rei turned his own hand over on the desk and looked at the palm. "She didn't say anything."

"No." Moriya looked at the drawing on the wall for a moment. "She came to see me yesterday afternoon. Before I asked. She sat in that chair and told me everything. The circle in Asagaya. The woman. What she was promised. She'd written it all down first, in pencil, so she wouldn't leave anything out." He paused. "She's suspended. She asked to be. She said she couldn't sit at her desk with the bowl on it."

Rei closed his eyes.

*Because she loved him,* the radio had said. From the very end of a love song, a woman singing alone with no music behind her.

He had known. He had known since the evidence archive. He had written it very small in the back of Gin's book. He had not let himself think any further. And then he had walked up to her desk and told her everything, because she had a kind face and a mended bowl and his brother had once told her to go home.

Moriya slid the form back into its sleeve. "Do you want to know what I think?"

Rei opened his eyes. "Yes."

"I think a woman who loved a man for two years without telling him walked into a room where something very old and very clever had been waiting for exactly that, and it found the one door she'd never locked." Moriya picked up the strip of white paper and turned it over in his fingers. "I think she's guilty. I've written that down. It's accurate." He put the strip down. "I also think she's the second person that thing has done it to this summer. And the first one was you. You just didn't drink the tea."

[break The paper]

There was a knock on the map-room door, and it opened before Moriya could answer, and Fujiwara-Kage Sena came in with a folder under his arm.

He bowed to Moriya. A small exact correct bow. He looked at Rei for one second. Then he walked to the desk and picked up the strip of white paper and held it up to the window.

"I was right," he said, in his quiet measured voice. "I'm sorry to be right. I often am." He turned the strip so the light came through it. Inside the paper, very faint, there was a pattern. A watermark. A small round crest, like a flower seen from above. "This isn't Division paper. Division seal stock is made in Saitama and it's white all the way through. This is mulberry, hand-laid, from a mill outside Kyoto that has sold to exactly nine buyers for two hundred years. All of them old houses." He lowered the strip. "My family is one of them. I recognised the crest because I've been writing on paper like this since I was four."

Moriya looked at him for a long moment, and then back at the watermark. "Is it your family's?"

"No. Different crest." Sena set the strip down on the desk, exactly where it had been, and squared it with one fingertip. "But it was bought by a Tower family, Moriya-san, and used by a Division office, and it doesn't appear anywhere on that office's procurement records. I checked this morning. Marunouchi doesn't buy seal paper. Officially, they've never needed to." He folded his hands. "Somebody is paying for Public Confidence's equipment off the books. I've filed that. It's my fifth concern this quarter. It's a reasonable one."

Rei turned on his chair to look up at him. "You came to tell us. You didn't have to."

Sena turned his head and looked at him. For a second something moved in his face. Something careful.

"I'm an Auditor," he said. "I write down what's true. It doesn't matter whose house it embarrasses." He paused. "And my family voted yes on your brother's file. I told you I wasn't here to talk about that." Another pause. "I find I'd like it on the record that I've stopped not talking about it."

He bowed, the same small exact bow, and went out.

[break Marunouchi]

The summons came at eleven. A cream envelope, hand-delivered by a young man in a grey suit who would not meet Rei's eyes. Inside, a single sheet in that same beautiful even hand. *The Director of Public Confidence would be grateful for a conversation with Uedera Rei-san at his earliest convenience. Today, three o'clock, would be convenient.*

Moriya read it twice and laid it flat. "You'll go. I'll go with you."

"Can she..." Rei stopped. "Can she do something? To me? Like she tried to do to Gin?"

Moriya put the letter down. He opened the bottom drawer of the map cabinet beside his desk and took out a form. A different one. Grey. With an Internal Affairs crest at the top. He had already filled it in, Rei saw, all of it, in small square careful pencil, except the signature.

"This designates you as a material witness in an active Internal Affairs investigation," said Moriya. "Investigation number seven-three-one-four, concerning an unauthorised containment operation in Kita-ku on the twenty-third of July resulting in the death of a civilian." He uncapped a biro. "While the investigation is open, no office of the Division may audit you, ground you, transfer you, reassess you, or contain you, without the written approval of Internal Affairs. Which is me." He signed it. A short, plain, quick signature, nothing like the one on the cream form. "It's called witness immunity. It's very old. It was written in 1952 by somebody who'd seen what happens to witnesses. Nobody's used it in eleven years. Nobody remembers it's there." He blew on the ink. "That's the thing about records. They stay true whether anybody's reading them or not."

[break The glass office]

Hagiwara Mizuki was standing at the window when they came in, with her back to the door and the whole of Tokyo spread out below her in the afternoon heat, white and shimmering.

She turned round. She looked at Rei. Then she looked at Moriya, standing one step behind him, in his dark plain suit, with a cardboard folder under his arm.

For one second, Rei thought he saw something in her face. Like a woman who has opened her front door expecting the post and found a policeman.

Then it was gone. She smiled. The smile arrived on her face exactly on time, exactly the right size, and went no further than her mouth.

She let her eyes rest on the folder under his arm. "Moriya-san. I didn't invite you."

"No," said Moriya. "Uedera-san is a witness in an open Internal Affairs matter. He doesn't speak to other offices without me." He held out the grey form. "This was filed at nine-fifteen this morning. You'll find a copy in your inbox."

Hagiwara did not take it. She looked at it in his hand for a long moment. Then she sat down behind the long white desk, and folded her hands on it beside the fountain pen, and looked at Rei.

She opened one hand toward the chair across from her. "Sit down, Uedera-san. Please."

He sat.

"I'm sorry about Tanizaki Seiichi," she said. Her voice was low and calm and perfectly pleasant. "It was not intended. My officers were instructed to contain an entity, not to engage civilians. Something interfered. Something we didn't expect." She tilted her head very slightly. "You know what interfered. Don't you. You were there."

Rei did not say anything.

"I'm not your enemy, Uedera-san." She picked up the fountain pen and turned it in her fingers, not uncapping it. "I know you think I am. I know what they say about this office downstairs. But I'm the only person in this building who is trying to solve the actual problem." She set the pen down. "The veil is failing. You know it is. You've seen it. Every thin spot that opened in 2011 is still open. Every year more things come through. Every year more people like you are born who can see them, and hear them, and let them in. And every year the Division registers a few more of you and gives you a lanyard and a crow and sends you out to stand in the gaps with a bucket of salt." She smiled. "That's not a solution. That's a bucket."

"And yours is," said Rei. His voice came out very quiet. "Closing it."

The smile did not move. But the hand on the desk went still.

"Closing everything," said Rei. "With strips of paper. Doors and walls. You don't care which. You don't know which." He looked at her. "You sealed an old man's house. Forty-one years of him writing *not here* on every board, so people could come and drink tea and be safe, and you put *closed* on top of it, and it broke. And something walked in through the place where it broke and killed him."

Hagiwara looked at him for a long time.

"Your brother asked questions too," she said at last. Very softly. "Last July. About the ninth floor. About things that were none of his business. I reported him. Moriya-san closed the report." Her eyes went to Moriya, and back. "Two weeks later, something took him off a doorstep. If he'd been contained then, in the sublevel, under my authority, he would be safe now. He'd be in a white room, asleep, with a guard on the door, and that thing would be in a box." She leaned forward. "I could still do that, Uedera-san. I could still bring him in. Safely. All I need is for you to tell me where he goes."

Rei looked at her.

He thought of a long white room in Asagaya, and a woman in a soft dress saying *I could give him back to you*.

He thought of the strips of paper on Tanizaki's gate.

He thought, very clearly, *they're the same*. Not the same thing. Not the same reasons. But the same offer. *Put him in a white room. Put him in a chair. Put it all down and let me carry it.*

He kept his hands flat on his knees. "No."

[break What she felt]

Moriya opened his folder.

He did it slowly. He took out three sheets of paper and laid them on the long white desk in a row, very neatly, the way he did everything. A copy of the containment order, with the version number circled in red pencil. A copy of the Marunouchi procurement records for the last three years, with no seal paper on them anywhere. And a single page from Sena's report, with a photograph of a small round crest, like a flower seen from above.

Hagiwara looked at them. She did not touch them.

"I'm not going to do anything with these today, Director," said Moriya. "I don't need to. Internal Affairs doesn't act on suspicion. It acts on record." He squared the edge of the third sheet with one fingertip. "The record now says that this office prepared an operation against a registered officer three months before it had grounds. It says this office uses equipment it doesn't pay for, from people it doesn't name. And it says that operation killed a man who had kept this city's back door shut since before you joined the Division." He looked up. His pale grey eyes were very calm. "That's all accurate. It'll stay accurate. There are copies. Some of them are in places I won't tell you about. And Uedera Rei is a witness, which means if anything happens to him, the record says why."

Hagiwara did not move.

For a long time, there was no sound in the glass office except the hum of the air conditioning and, very far below, the city.

Then she stood up. She walked to the window. She stood with her back to them, the way she had been standing when they came in, looking out over Tokyo.

"Moriya-san," she said, without turning round. "You have children. Don't you."

Moriya's hand moved, very slightly, toward his inside pocket, and stopped. "Two."

"Then you understand." Her voice was very low. "Somebody has to keep them safe. From all of it. From the things that stand at the end of their beds at night, and the things that whisper on platforms, and the things that came through in 2011 and never went back." She paused. "Somebody has to shut the door."

Rei looked at her back. At the perfect grey suit. At the short perfect hair.

And he felt it.

Faint. So faint. Like a sound in another room. Coming off her, the way things came off people now that the door in his chest would not quite close. A little thread of something. Very old. Very small. Pushed down so hard and for so long that it had nearly gone. A child, a long time ago, in a room at night, seeing something at the end of the bed, and lying very still, and telling herself it was nothing. It was nothing. It was a draught.

He did not mean to say it. It came out of him the way Tanizaki's words had come out of Gin, on a bench, two streets away.

"You feel them too," he said quietly. "At the edges of rooms. Don't you."

Hagiwara's shoulders went very still.

Rei kept his voice as low as hers. "You call it a draught."

She did not turn round. She did not answer. She stood at the glass with the whole city in front of her and her hands at her sides, and the fingers of her right hand closed slowly into a fist, and opened again.

"Thank you for coming, Uedera-san," she said at last. Her voice was perfectly pleasant and perfectly even, and it was the voice of a woman closing a door very quietly so as not to wake somebody. "Moriya-san. I'm sure we'll speak again."

They went out.

In the lift, going down, very fast, so fast Rei's ears popped, Moriya stood with the folder under his arm and his eyes on the numbers.

"That," he said, as they passed the tenth floor, "was not in the record." He did not look at Rei. "I'm going to leave it out. Some things are true and nobody else's business." A pause. "But you should be careful, Uedera. People who've spent their whole lives telling themselves something is a draught don't like being told it's a ghost. They tend to want to close the window harder."

[break The Cattery]

He went up to the Cattery before he went home.

He did not know if she would be there. She was suspended. She was not supposed to be in the building.

She was there. Sitting on her low stool under the highest shelf, in a grey cardigan, with a mug in both hands that had gone cold a long time ago. The kintsugi bowl was not with her. The tortoiseshell was asleep. The grey was washing. The white one with the odd ears was watching. The fat orange one was upside down on his cushion.

On the highest shelf, Kuga lay with his back to the room and his face to the wall.

Katagiri looked up when Rei came in. Her face did not do anything. She had nothing left for it to do.

"I'm not supposed to be here," she said. "I came to say goodbye to them. In case they don't let me back." She looked up at the high shelf. "He hasn't turned round since that Saturday. He won't eat if I'm in the room. I've been feeding him through the door."

Rei sat down on the floor in front of her, with his back to the shelves, the way he sat by Watanabe's chair.

He did not know what he was going to say. He had thought about it all the way up the stairs, and he still did not know.

He pulled his knees up and looked at the cold mug in her hands. "Why?"

He had not meant it to come out like that. Not angry. Just tired. Just a question.

Katagiri looked down at her cold tea.

"Because she said she could bring him home," she said. "And nobody else had said that. Not once. Not in eleven months. Everybody said *administrative review* and *we're looking into it* and *you should take some leave, Noa*. She was the only one who said *home*." Her voice did not shake. She had made it not shake. "I knew what she was. I'm not stupid. I'm a Channel. I could feel it under the floor. I knew, and I went anyway, and I told myself the Division would take him somewhere safe, and then she'd take the thing out, and I'd be..." She stopped. "I'd be the first thing he saw. She said that. She said she'd make sure."

Rei leaned his head back against the shelf. "She said the same to me. Almost exactly."

Katagiri looked at him.

"She offered me the same thing," said Rei. "In the white room. Gin, at our kitchen table, complaining about the rice cooker. All I had to do was sit in a chair." He looked at his hands. "I nearly drank it. I wanted to. I'd have drunk it if Kato hadn't been shouting in my head."

The Cattery was very quiet. The orange cat snored.

"She said something else to me," said Katagiri, very low. "At the end. On Thursday night. I didn't listen. I've been not listening to things for a year." She turned the mug in her hands. "She said, *by Obon you'll have him. Sitting in a chair.*"

Rei went cold.

"I didn't think about it," said Katagiri. "Not then. *A chair.* I thought she meant... I don't know what I thought she meant. I thought she meant our kitchen. My kitchen." She closed her eyes. "Moriya told me yesterday what the Archive thinks she wants. The seats. Thirteen of them. Sitting in a chair." Her voice cracked, at last, very slightly, and she let it. "She wasn't going to give him back to me, was she. She was going to put him in one."

Rei did not answer. He did not need to.

They sat on the floor of the Cattery for a long time, in the warm dim room that smelled of paper and cat and dried bonito.

Then Rei got up. He went to the high shelf, and stood in front of it, and bowed. Low. The way he would have bowed to Tanizaki.

"Kuga-san," he said. "Thank you for coming that night. You didn't have to. You came on your own, across half the city." He paused. "She sent you too, you know. She came in early and asked you. She asked you to keep a promise she'd made to me. Even after. Even when she knew."

Kuga did not move.

"She was wrong," said Rei. "She was very, very wrong. But she asked you to watch over me. She did that too." His voice went. He made it come back. "That's all. I just wanted you to know both things."

He turned to go.

Behind him, on the high shelf, there was a long slow sound. A heavy body shifting. A tail.

He did not look round. He went to the door. At the door, he could not help it, and he looked back.

Kuga had not turned to face the room. He was still lying with his face to the wall. But his tail had come down over the edge of the shelf, long and black and heavy, and it hung there, just above Katagiri's bowed head, and moved, very slowly, from side to side. Not touching her. Not quite.

Katagiri did not see it. She was looking at her cold tea.

Rei did not tell her. He thought she would see it herself, sooner or later. He thought it was better if she did.
