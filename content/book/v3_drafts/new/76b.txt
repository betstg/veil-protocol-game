#title Hearts

Moriya Tatsuki had been reading bodies for fifteen years, and the thing he had learned in all that time was that a body almost always wants to live.

That was his gift. Not the reading. The reading was only the start. He could put two fingers on the side of somebody's neck and know their heart, and their lungs, and their blood, and what was wrong with each of them, as easily as you know the time from a clock. But what he did with it, what Field paid him for, what had kept four of his teammates alive in fifteen years who should not have lived, was asking. He asked the heart to slow down. He asked the blood to stay where it was. He asked a lung that had been punctured to wait, just a little, just until the ambulance. And because a body almost always wants to live, it almost always listened.

He was going to have to ask a great many bodies a great many things tonight. He knew that as soon as he came round the corner into the square outside Tabata station on his crutch and saw how many of them there were.

[break Tabata]

Tabata is the station before Nishi-Nippori on the Yamanote line, the big green loop that goes all the way round the middle of Tokyo, and it is built at the bottom of a cliff. The tracks run along the foot of it, a dozen of them, side by side, with the trains going in and out all day. The station is down on the flat by the tracks. The town is up on top of the cliff, reached by a long steep bridge and a flight of concrete stairs. A hundred years ago, the top of that cliff was where the writers lived, and the painters, and the potters, in small wooden houses with gardens, because it was cheap and quiet and close to the city and you could see a long way. One of the most famous writers in Japan wrote his last stories in a house up there and then died in it. There is a small museum about it now, beside the station, which almost nobody visits.

At twenty to one on the last night of Obon, the square outside the station was full of people walking up the stairs to the top of the cliff.

They came from the river. Hundreds of them. Along the road by the tracks and across the square and up the long concrete stairs to the town on top, slowly, one step at a time, with white flowers held in front of them. The stairs were lit by street lamps on poles, and in the yellow light Moriya could see their faces. Calm. Patient. Black-eyed.

Okada was already there, at the foot of the stairs, with six field cars pulled up across the square in a loose line, and fourteen officers standing beside them in their dark jackets with bags of salt and iron, waiting to be told what to do. She had her three notebooks open on the bonnet of the nearest car, held down against the night breeze with a can of coffee.

She looked at Moriya coming across the square on his crutch.

"How many can you do?" she said.

"I don't know," said Moriya. "Let's find out."

[break Asking]

It worked like this.

An officer went up the stairs beside one of the hollow ones. A hand on the shoulder. Not hard. And while the hollow one stopped, and turned its head to look at the hand, Moriya came up the step below on his crutch and put two fingers on the side of its neck.

And asked.

*Slow down,* he asked the heart. *Just a little. You're tired. You've been walking a long way. You can sit down now.*

The heart slowed. Not much. Enough. The hollow one's knees went soft, the way a child's knees go soft on a long walk home, and it sat down on the step where it was. And while it sat, with its head drooping and its breath coming slow and deep, the officer put a pinch of iron filings into its open palm and closed its fingers round them, and whatever was riding it, the small grey thing under the hum, let go, and went up into the dark over the tracks like steam off a kettle.

Then the officer sat down on the step beside a frightened stranger who did not know where they were, and Moriya went down a step on his crutch to the next one.

One. Then another. Then another.

His leg hurt. It had hurt for three months, since the temple, since the bell had come through the wall and caught him below the knee and broken it in three places. It was out of the brace now but it was not right. It would never be quite right, the doctors said. He had stopped listening to the doctors. Every step down the concrete stairs on the crutch sent something hot and sharp up into his hip, and he asked his own body to wait, the way he asked everybody else's, and his own body, which knew him very well, mostly listened.

By one o'clock he had done thirty.

By a quarter past he had done fifty, and his fingers had started to go numb.

It was like this every time. He had never told anyone. When he asked a heart to slow down, a little of his own slowed with it. Just for a moment. Just a beat. It came back. It always came back. But fifty times in half an hour, fifty beats he had lent out and taken back, and his own heart had begun to forget, a little, which beats were his.

*Moriya,* said Haruki, in his head. Calm. Dry. *Your heart's doing something funny. I can feel it from the yard.*

*I know.*

*Sit down for a minute.*

*There's two hundred more on the stairs.*

*Then sit down for a minute and do two hundred more after,* said Haruki. *Gin's orders. He said if you ever went past fifty in a night I was to tell you to sit down. He said you'd ignore me. He said to tell you he said so.*

Moriya stopped on the stairs with his hand on the rail.

*He said that?*

*In 2014,* said Haruki. *At my bar. After the thing in Ōtsuka. You did sixty that night and couldn't feel your feet for a week. He was very cross about it.*

Moriya sat down on a step.

He sat there for exactly one minute by his watch, with his crutch across his knees and his eyes shut, asking his own heart to remember which beats were his. Then he got up and went down another step.

[break The taxi]

At twenty-five past one a taxi came across the square.

It came from the road by the tracks, fast, too fast, a black taxi with its light still on and its indicator still blinking, and it did not slow down at the line of field cars. It went round them. It went up onto the pavement and through a row of bicycles in a stand, scattering them, and straight toward the foot of the stairs, where forty people were sitting on the bottom steps with officers beside them, waiting for an ambulance, frightened and awake.

Moriya saw the driver. An old man in a white driver's cap. Black-eyed. Smiling.

He saw it the way you see things in the last half second. Very clearly. With nothing he could do.

There was a crack like a branch breaking, and a white flash, and every light on the taxi went out at once.

The engine died. And the two front tyres went white. Moriya saw it. White with frost, in August, from the rim outward, as if somebody had poured liquid nitrogen on them, and then they burst, both of them, with a sound like a gun. The taxi slewed sideways on its rims in a long shriek of metal on stone and slid, and slid, and stopped against the end of the bicycle stand a metre from the bottom step, with the old man in the white cap thrown sideways against his door.

Sena was standing at the edge of the square, by the little museum, in his grey suit, with one hand held out in front of him, the fingers spread.

His face was perfectly still. His hand was not. It was shaking, and there was frost on his fingertips, and the smell of burnt air hung round him like the smell after a thunderstorm.

"Somebody," he said, quite calmly, in his quiet measured voice, "take the keys out of that."

An officer ran to the taxi and reached through the window. Sena lowered his hand, and looked at the frost on it, and rubbed his fingers together until it went.

Okada was looking at him across the square.

"I didn't know you did that," she said.

"Nobody does," said Sena. "It's not in my file." He took his black notebook out of his inside pocket and opened it and wrote something in it, very small, and put it back. "It is now."

Two officers took the old man out of the taxi. He came quite easily. When they gave him iron he cried, and said he had been on his way home from the depot and didn't know what had happened, he had a grandson who was coming to stay, he had never had an accident in forty years. The officers sat him on a step.

Sena walked across the square and stood beside Moriya at the foot of the stairs, looking up at them. At the long line of hollow ones still climbing, slowly, in the lamplight, toward the town on the cliff.

"I'll take the left side," he said. "You take the right. I can't make them sit. I can make the steps very cold. They'll stop to look at their feet." He took his jacket off and folded it over the railing, neatly. "I'm told my family voted to keep your friend's file shut, last November. I have been thinking about what I'd like to do about that." He pushed his sleeves up. "This seems a start."

[break Itō]

At twenty to two, Haruki said it.

He did not say it in everyone's head. He said it in Okada's, and Moriya's, because they were standing beside each other at the foot of the stairs, and because they were the ones who needed to know first.

*Itō Kenta,* he said. *In Ōji. In the lane behind the Inari shrine. On the step of a family's flat. Kato heard it.* A pause. *I'm sorry, Okada.*

Okada did not move.

She stood at the bonnet of the car with her three notebooks open in front of her and a pen in her hand, and she did not move at all, for a long time.

She had brought him in. Moriya knew that. Everybody in Field knew that. Okada did not bring people in. She had brought in four people in twelve years, and Itō was the fourth. She had found him working nights in a convenience store in Akabane, because he had a habit of telling the old people who came in at three in the morning to buy one can of beer which of their dead relatives was standing behind them, and kindly, so that they did not mind. She had watched him for a month. She had written him a recommendation two pages long. Moriya had read it. It said things like *reliable* and *good with frightened people* and, at the very bottom, in a different pen, as if she had added it later, *he will go on a step when he should not. Somebody should teach him not to.*

She picked up her pen.

She opened the first of her three notebooks at a page near the back, where there was a list. Moriya had seen the list before. He had never asked. It was a list of names, in her small careful handwriting, with dates, going back many years. Some of the names he knew. Most he did not. The last one was written a long time ago, in another colour of ink.

She wrote *Itō Kenta* at the bottom, and the date, and the place. *Ōji. The step.*

Then she closed the notebook and put the rubber band back round all three, and picked up her radio, and said into it, quite steadily, "Hirata. Stay with him. Don't let anyone move him until I come. I'm coming."

She looked at Moriya.

"Can you hold this?"

"Yes," said Moriya.

She went. She did not run. She walked across the square to her car and got in and drove away up the road toward Ōji, and Moriya watched her tail lights go round the corner, and then turned back to the stairs, and the next one, and put two fingers on its neck, and asked.

[break The canal]

In Higashi-Jūjō, behind the shopping street, there is a little canal.

It is not much of a canal. It is a stone channel two metres wide that used to carry water from somewhere to somewhere else and now carries nothing in particular, with a narrow lane along one side and the backs of old houses along the other. The houses are the oldest in the neighbourhood. Wooden, two storeys, built in long joined rows after the war, when there was no money and no time and everybody needed somewhere to sleep. They lean on each other. Their roofs are corrugated iron gone brown with rust. Their walls are thin wooden boards, and behind the boards there are old people who have lived there for sixty years and will not move, and who light their sending fires every year in clay dishes on their doorsteps, right up against the wooden walls, because they always have.

At five to one, somebody knocked on one of those doors, and an old man opened it, and the hollow woman on the step said *come and rest*, and the old man stepped out to go with her, and his foot caught the clay dish on the step, and it went over.

There was still a little fire in it. There always is. A few red embers in the hemp ash, the last of them. They went across the step and against the bottom of the wooden wall, and the wall was dry as paper after a hot August, and it caught.

By the time Mori got there, three houses were burning.

[break Mori]

Rei had told Moriya once that he thought Mori was the most frightening man he had ever met. Not because he was angry. Because of how carefully he was angry. Moriya had not understood, until he heard about the canal.

Mori came down the lane along the canal at a walk, with Hoshino and Inoue behind him, and stopped in front of the fire.

It was big already. Three houses in a row, wooden, sixty years old, going up from the inside with a roar like a train. The heat was enormous. It came across the lane and the canal in a wall, and the water in the stone channel steamed. The neighbours were coming out of the other houses in the row, old people in nightclothes, in slippers, carrying cats and photographs, and standing in the lane with their hands over their mouths. Somewhere a siren was coming, very far away, through streets full of people walking toward the river.

And in the middle house, on the second floor, at a window with no glass in it, there was a child.

Mori looked at the fire.

He did not shout. He did not lift his hands. He stood in the lane in his black jacket that did not fit him across the shoulders, and he looked at the fire with his coal-coloured eyes, and Hoshino said afterwards that you could see him decide. That his face did not change at all, but something in the way he was standing did, as if he had been leaning back and now he was leaning forward.

"No," he said. "That is wrong."

And the fire leaned toward him.

Not all at once. The way a crowd turns toward somebody who has started to speak. The flames on the front of the three houses bent, all together, in no wind at all, across the lane, toward the big man standing in it. And then they came. Off the wooden walls, off the rusted roofs, out of the empty windows, in long bright curling tongues, across the lane and into him. Into his chest. Into his hands. Into the air round him, which went from shimmering to white.

He took it.

That was the only way anybody who saw it could ever describe it afterwards. He took the fire. He had been holding Rage for this city for twenty-one years, and Rage is heat, and he took the heat out of three burning houses and held it, in himself, the way you hold your breath under water.

The houses stopped burning.

They did not go out like a fire goes out, slowly, smoking. They stopped. Black and smoking and ruined, but not burning, as if somebody had turned a switch. And Mori stood in the lane with all of it in him, white-hot, and the cobbles under his feet cracked, and his black jacket began to smoke at the shoulders, and his face was the colour of a coal at the very bottom of a fire that has burned all night.

"Satoru," he said, through his teeth. "The child."

Inoue Satoru was already gone.

He did not walk. He did not run. He was standing behind Mori in the lane, and then he was not, and then he was at the empty window on the second floor of the middle house, inside the black smoking frame, with a small coughing shape in his arms. And then he was in the lane again, on the far side of the canal, on his knees on the stones, and the child was in his arms, a girl of about six in pyjamas with cartoon rabbits on them, coughing and crying and alive.

"Hoshino," said Inoue. "Here. Here, quick."

Hoshino Haru came across the little bridge over the canal at a run, in his apron, and knelt down on the stones beside them, and put his two square hands flat on the little girl's chest.

He was not a healer. Not the way Moriya was. He could not read a body or ask a heart to slow. But he held Joy, and Joy is the warm thing in a body that wants very much to be alive, and he put it into her. Moriya heard about it afterwards from Inoue and did not quite believe it, and then he went and asked Hoshino, and Hoshino went red and said he didn't know what he'd done, he'd just thought about rice balls. Whatever it was, the little girl's breathing came back. Slowly. Then all at once. And she looked up at the man in the apron kneeling over her and said, in a hoarse small voice, "You smell like soup."

Hoshino laughed out loud, on his knees by the canal, with tears running down his face.

Mori had not moved.

He was still standing in the lane in front of the black houses, with all the heat in him. The air round him was white. The cobbles under him had melted into glass. His jacket had burned off his shoulders. And he could not let it go. Moriya understood that later, when Hoshino told him. There was nowhere to put it. If he let it go in the lane, the lane would burn, and the houses on the other side, and the old people standing in their nightclothes with their cats.

So he walked.

He turned round and walked down the lane, slowly, white-hot, to the end where it met the canal at a little stone landing, and went down the three stone steps to the water, and stood in the canal up to his knees, and let it go.

The canal boiled.

All of it, from the landing to the bridge. A great white cloud of steam went up into the night over Higashi-Jūjō, so big and so bright that people saw it from Ōji, from Tabata, from the freight yard by the river, and thought for a moment that the whole of Kita was on fire. And then it drifted, and thinned, and blew away over the roofs toward the river, and the canal was a canal again, very warm, with a big man standing in it up to his knees in the ruins of his clothes, with his hands hanging at his sides and his head bowed, breathing.

The old man whose foot had caught the dish was standing at the top of the landing in his pyjamas. The hollow woman was gone from beside him. He did not know where she had gone. He looked down at the man in the canal.

"I'm sorry," he said. His voice shook. "I'm sorry. It was my dish. It's my fault. I've lit that dish on that step every year for sixty years."

Mori looked up at him from the water. His face was red and blistered on one side, and his eyebrows had gone, and his eyes were the colour of very old coals.

"Then light it again next year," he said. "Further from the wall."

[break Quiet]

By two o'clock, the stairs at Tabata were empty.

Moriya sat on the bottom step with his crutch across his knees. He had done two hundred and twelve. He knew because he had counted. He always counted. Sena was sitting on the step beside him with his sleeves still pushed up, writing in his black notebook, very small and very fast, and his hand was not steady.

All around the square, on the steps and the kerbs and the bonnets of the field cars, people were sitting with blankets round them. Woken people. Frightened. Not knowing where they were, or why they were holding a flower. Officers were going from one to the next with tea from a flask and their phones out, ringing families. The museum about the dead writer had turned its lights on, and somebody had opened its door, and some of the old people had gone inside to sit down out of the wind among the glass cases of manuscripts and the photographs of men in round glasses.

*Moriya,* said Haruki. *How's your heart?*

Moriya put two fingers on the side of his own neck.

*Mine,* he thought. *All of it.*

*Good,* said Haruki. A pause. *Mori's in the canal at Higashi-Jūjō. Up to his knees. He won't get out. Hoshino says he's all right. He says he just wants to stand there for a bit.*

*I'd want to stand there for a bit.*

*Okada's in Ōji with Itō,* said Haruki. *Kato's on a step in Ōji. Usami's on another step. There's a boy nobody knows the name of.* A pause, longer. *Rei got Yua out of the white house ten minutes ago. He's on his way back to the yard. The priest in Takinogawa has been ringing his bell every hour since one o'clock, and he's got eleven other temples doing it. Every time it rings, she slips a little more.*

Moriya looked up the empty stairs toward the town on top of the cliff, where the writers used to live.

Somewhere far off, very faint, over the roofs, a temple bell rang for two o'clock. Once. Low and huge and slow, so low you felt it in the step under you before you heard it.

Beside him, Sena stopped writing and lifted his head.

All over the square, the woken people looked up.
