#title One Question

The eighth death came on the twenty-fourth of September. An old man in Shimo. For a week before he died, the neighbours said, rats had sat on the walkway outside his door every evening, very still, all facing it.

Rei read about it in the morning on a page that Kato sent him, folded inside a photograph of a cat. That night, before he left, he sent Kato a cat back. The answer came in four seconds, a photograph of a different cat, very fat, asleep in a flowerpot, and under it one word. *careful.* Then he called Itsuki and Haruki, and at midnight the three of them walked out along the top of the Arakawa bank toward the old red floodgate at Iwabuchi.

[need ch13_iwabuchi_gate | The old red brick and iron floodgate at Iwabuchi on the Arakawa at midnight, two square towers, the wide black river, three young men walking the grass bank with torches, a thin mist on the water]

The Iwabuchi floodgate is a hundred years old. It stands where the Arakawa splits from the smaller Sumida river, a heavy brick building with two square towers and great iron gates painted red, rusted at the edges. It was built after a flood drowned half the city. It does not work any more. A newer gate stands beside it, grey and plain. But the old red one has been kept, because people in Kita love it, the way you love an old dog that cannot do tricks any more. In the day, people take photographs of it. At night it is a dark red shape against a dark sky, and the water goes past it with a sound like a long breath.

"Why here?" said Itsuki.

"Because all eight are around it," said Rei. He had drawn the deaths on a paper map. A ring of eight small crosses around the old gate. "It started here. It goes out, and it comes back here. It's like a nest."

"A nest," said Itsuki. "Of a rat demon. At midnight. Great. Love it. Can't wait."

"You didn't have to come."

"Shut up. I'm complaining. It's how I do brave." Itsuki kicked a stone off the bank into the black water and listened to it go. "Gin used to say complaining is just courage with the volume up."

"He never said that."

"He said it to *me*. You weren't there. You were at cram school being boring."

Haruki walked a little ahead of them. He had come straight from the bar, in a dark knit sweater with the sleeves pulled down over his hands against the river wind. He had brought a long cloth bag on his shoulder. He had not said what was in it. He had been quiet the whole way, looking at the river.

"Rei," he said, without turning round. "How much sleep have you had this week?"

"Enough."

"How much."

Rei did not answer. Since Akabane he had slept perhaps three hours a night. Every time he closed his eyes he saw Mrs. Tachibana's black eyes. He felt her hand on his wrist. He had gone to Tanizaki's on Saturday and sat for an hour, and at the end of it Tanizaki had looked at him for a long time and said only, *You are thin.* Rei had thought he meant his body. Walking home, he had started to wonder.

"That's what I thought," said Haruki quietly. "Stay behind me. Don't open the door in your chest unless you have to. If you have to, open it a crack, and close it fast."

[break The nest]

They smelled it before they saw it.

Under the old floodgate, where the brick met the water, there was a dark arch, a kind of tunnel where the river had once run through. The smell came out of it. Wet fur. Rot. Something sweet and sick underneath, like milk left in the sun. Itsuki pulled his T-shirt up over his nose. Haruki put down his cloth bag and took out what was inside.

It was a sword. Not a real one. A short wooden sword, old and dark, with lines of tiny writing carved up both sides of the blade and a strip of white paper tied round the handle.

"Tsukishiro family," he said, when he saw Rei looking. "Old. Older than the gate. My grandmother would kill me if she knew I took it out of the house." He held it loosely in his right hand, point down. "It won't cut anything that's alive. It cuts the other things."

They shone their torches into the arch.

The rats were there. Hundreds of them. They covered the floor of the tunnel and the ledges of the wet brick walls, brown and grey and black, and every one of them was still. Sitting up on their back legs, with their front paws held against their chests, perfectly still. And every head was turned the same way, toward the mouth of the arch. Toward the torchlight. Toward Rei.

Hundreds of small black eyes. One look.

"Oh," said Itsuki faintly. "Oh, I hate this. I hate this so much."

"They're only eyes," said Rei. His voice did not sound like his. "It's not them. It's what's behind them."

At the back of the arch, beyond the rats, something moved.

It was a man. Or it had been. He had been sleeping there, Rei understood later. One of the men who sleep under the bridges along the Arakawa, in a nest of cardboard and blue plastic sheeting, with a shopping trolley full of cans. He was standing up now, slowly, in the dark at the back of the tunnel, among the rats. He was very thin. His beard was long and grey. His clothes hung on him. And his eyes, in the torchlight, were two black holes, huge and wet and shining, drinking in the three of them all at once.

The rats did not move. The man walked through them, and they did not move, and they did not stop looking at Rei.

"*Room,*" said the man. His voice was cracked and dry, the voice of someone who had not spoken aloud in weeks. "*So much room. So much room in you.*"

[break What it wanted]

Rei felt it reach for him.

It was not like the platform. On the platform the thing had leaned on the door in his chest. This was different. This was a pull. A soft, steady pull from the dark tunnel, like a current in a river pulling at your legs. It reached into the empty places in him. The tired places. The places scraped hollow by the arcade and the cup and Mrs. Tachibana. The three hours of sleep a night. The forty days of waiting for a message to say *Read*. It found all the hollow places, and it pulled at them, gently, the way a tide pulls sand out from under your feet, and the hollow places wanted to be filled. God, they wanted it.

*Hungry,* it said inside him, not in the man's voice now. In a voice like his own. *Aren't you hungry? Aren't you so tired? Let me in. Let me fill it. I fill everything.*

Rei's knees started to bend.

"Rei!" Haruki was beside him, a hand clamped on his shoulder, the fingers digging in hard enough to bruise. His sleepy handsome face was not sleepy at all. "Rei, close it. Close the door. Now."

He tried. The latch would not drop. He was too tired. The door in his chest was too heavy, and the pull was too strong, and he could not find the iron latch in the dark.

He put his hand flat on his chest, on the key under his shirt.

It was cold. It had always been cold. Six years of Gin's hand on it, six years of Gin holding on to the world with it. For a second, under his palm, Rei felt something that was not his. A steadiness. A stubbornness. A man standing very straight in a doorway at three in the morning, saying *no*.

"No," said Rei.

The man in the tunnel screamed. And every rat in the arch screamed with him, the same thin shriek, from hundreds of small throats at once, and then broke. They ran. Not at Rei. Away. Every way at once, out of the arch, along the ledges, into the water, into the grass, a brown flood scattering into the dark, as if a hand that had been holding them all had suddenly let go.

The thing did not need its eyes any more. It had found what it was looking for.

It came out of the man.

Rei saw it go. The thin bearded man folded at the knees and fell onto his cardboard, and out of his open mouth came the grey-yellow shape Rei had seen leave Mrs. Tachibana. Bigger now. Fed. Smoke with too many elbows. It came straight across the tunnel, straight at him, at the hollow places, at the door that would not close.

Haruki stepped in front of him and swung the wooden sword. It went through the grey shape like a hand through smoke, and the shape tore, and shrieked, and did not stop. Itsuki was throwing iron. He had both jars and he was throwing it by the fistful, screaming "get off him, get *off* him, you fucking rat bastard, get off," over and over, which was not in any of the books but seemed to help him. The filings went through the shape and it flinched and flickered and came on.

It reached Rei. It wrapped round him like wet cloth.

And Rei felt something else. Much bigger. Much further away.

Behind the thing. Behind the tunnel and the river and the old red gate. Behind everything. A light.

[break The light]

It pulled him the way the moon pulls the sea.

He did not choose to go. He did not know he was going. One moment he was standing on the grass bank with the key under his hand and Haruki shouting his name. The next moment the bank was gone, and the river, and the gate, and the night. He was falling. Not down. Inward. Through the open door in his chest and out the other side.

The demon came with him. He felt it, holding on to him, wrapped round him like wet cloth, filling the hollow places, still whispering *room, room, so much room*. It came with him through the door.

And then it met the light.

It did not scream. There was no time. It burned. Rei felt it burn the way you feel a match flare in a dark room, a bright hot snap, all at once, and then nothing. It was gone. Burned off him like paper. And Rei was alone.

He was standing in white.

Not a white room. Not white snow. White everywhere, in every direction, without edges. White without a floor or a ceiling or a horizon. He could not see his own hands. He was not sure he had hands. It was not bright, exactly. It did not hurt his eyes. It was simply the only thing there was.

[need ch13_white_light | A young man seen from behind, small and dark, standing at the edge of an endless soft white light with no horizon, a faint tall column of brighter white far ahead, everything else erased]

And far away, very far away, so far that distance did not mean anything, there was something brighter. A column. A tall, thin, steady column of light, rising out of the white into more white. He recognised it. He had seen it on black paper in white chalk, glued into the back of Gin's book, a drawing Itsuki had made without knowing why, with a single word written under it in his brother's handwriting.

*Don't.*

He wanted to go toward it. He wanted to more than he had ever wanted anything. It was the feeling of falling asleep after three days awake. It was the feeling of putting down something very heavy. The column did not call him. It did not need to. It simply was there, and everything in him leaned toward it the way plants lean toward a window.

He remembered the book. He had read the page a week ago, at three in the morning, and not understood it.

*A low-rank practitioner can push bodily into the veil. The rule is deliberately punishing. They get one question answered. One true thing the far side will tell them. And then they are shoved back.*

One question.

He did not have to think about it. There was only ever one question.

"Is my brother alive?"

He did not say it with his mouth. He did not know if he had a mouth. He said it with all of himself, into the white, toward the far bright column.

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the answer came. It did not come in words. It came the way warmth comes into cold hands, from the inside, all at once. It came with the smell of hōjicha. It came with the sound of rain on a frosted window. It came with the feeling of a little finger moving one centimetre across a wooden table toward a phone.

*Yes.*

And then something put a hand flat on his chest, very gently, and pushed.

He woke up on his back on the wet grass of the Arakawa bank with his face full of stars.

Itsuki was kneeling over him, holding his head. Itsuki's face was white and streaked with dirt and he was crying, openly, without seeming to know it. Haruki knelt on his other side with two fingers on Rei's neck, feeling his pulse. The wooden sword lay on the grass beside him.

"Rei," Itsuki was saying. "Rei, Rei, come on, come on, you idiot, wake up, I swear to God..."

"I'm awake," said Rei. His voice was a croak.

Itsuki made a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob and put his forehead down on Rei's chest and stayed there.

"You were gone," said Haruki quietly. His fingers were still on Rei's neck. "Two hours. You were breathing, but you were gone. Your eyes were open and there was nobody behind them." He took his hand away. It was shaking. "Your nose bled. A lot. And then you said something."

"What did I say?"

Haruki looked at him. "You said yes."

Rei put his hand on his chest. The key was there. It was cold. Under it, his heart was beating, slow and steady.

He turned his head. The grass all around them was empty. The rats were gone, every one, scattered back into the river and the drains and the dark, ordinary rats again, going about their ordinary rat business. Under the old red gate, on his cardboard, the thin bearded man was sitting up, holding his head, crying quietly. Haruki had pulled off his own sweater and put it round the man's shoulders, and was sitting in the cold in his shirt as if he had not noticed.

The thing on the river was gone. He had felt it burn.

"Is it over?" said Itsuki, into his chest.

"That one is," said Rei.

He lay on the grass with his friend's head on his chest and looked up at the stars over the river. They were very faint, the way stars always are over Tokyo. Behind his eyes, very quietly, something hummed. A thin, high, steady note, like a tuning fork, or like a light that had been switched on in another room and left on. He did not think it had been there before. He did not think it would go away.

*Yes,* he thought. *He's alive.*

For the first time in forty-three days, he slept until morning without dreaming at all.
