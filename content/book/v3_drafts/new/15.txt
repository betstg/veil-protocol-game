#title One Question

The bar under the comb shop was nearly empty on Friday. The old man was at the piano, playing something slow with a lot of space in it. A couple in their sixties sat at the far end of the counter, sharing a plate of crackers and not talking, the comfortable kind of not talking. The lamps were low and yellow, and the long wooden counter shone under them where forty years of elbows had polished it.

Haruki was behind the counter in a dark green sweater with the sleeves pushed up, making coffee the slow way, pouring water from a kettle with a long thin spout over a paper filter in a circle. In the breast pocket of his shirt, under the sweater, Rei could see the edge of the dried flower he always kept there, pale and flat as a pressed leaf. He did not look up when they came down the stairs. He put two cups on the counter, then a third, and went on pouring.

Itsuki climbed onto a stool and put his head down on his folded arms on the counter.

"He went back alone," he said into his arms. "On Sunday night. He didn't tell me. He told me on Monday, at lunch, like it was a funny story. He had a bruise on his face from the floor."

"I know," said Haruki. "He told me at two in the morning."

"Of course he did." Itsuki lifted his head and looked at Rei with great dislike. "Two in the morning, Haruki gets a message. Lunchtime on Monday, I get a funny story."

"You'd have come," said Rei.

"Yes, I would have come. That's the *point* of me."

Haruki put a cup of coffee in front of each of them and leaned on the counter on his forearms.

"Tell me about the fish shop," he said. "All of it. Slowly."

Rei told him. The padlock. The pole. The rattle of the shutter. The ice and the mackerel and the broom. A thousand mornings worn smooth together. The arcade emptying, shop by shop, over years, until there was nobody. An old man standing behind his counter with his hands folded, waiting for people who were never coming.

He told him about the night. The grey coming in through the open door, slow and enormous. Lying down on the tiles. Not minding.

Haruki listened without interrupting once. When Rei stopped, he was quiet for a while, looking at his own coffee.

"You went in to fight it," he said at last.

"I went in to help."

"You went in to fix it. Same thing, the way you did it." Haruki turned his cup on the saucer a quarter turn. "That old man isn't a thing you fight, Rei. There's nothing to fight. He doesn't want you. He doesn't want anything from you. He wants to keep his shop open, because closing it means admitting it's over. You walked up to him in the middle of the night and opened your whole chest at him, and he didn't even see you. He just poured into the space. Grief does that. It fills whatever you leave open."

Rei looked at the counter.

"So what do I do?"

"You don't fight him. And you don't fix him." Haruki finished his coffee. "You help him close the shop."

[break Six o'clock]

They went on Sunday morning, in the dark before dawn, the three of them.

Kamiya at half past five was blue and cold. The streetlight at the entrance was still on. The plastic flowers under the faded sign moved a little in the wind. The tofu shop was dark, but somewhere behind it a light was on, and they could hear water running into a tank and the old woman's radio playing the morning news very quietly.

Haruki had brought a broom. An ordinary one, with a red plastic handle, from the cupboard behind his bar. Itsuki had brought a sheet of thick white paper rolled up under his arm, and a brush, and a bottle of ink, and a roll of tape. He had been up all night at Geidai and there was black ink on his fingers and on one ear.

They stood in front of the fish shop shutter. The cartoon fish smiled at them in its chef's hat.

"Five to six," said Haruki. "He'll come at six. He always comes at six." He leaned the broom against the shutter. "I'll go in first. I'll find him, and I'll hold him steady, so he can hear you. That's what I'm for. Telepaths are good for one thing, and that's it." He looked at Rei. "When I say, open your door. Not all the way. Enough that he can see you're there. And then you say it. Only that. Nothing else."

"What if he doesn't listen?"

"Then we come back tomorrow at six. And the day after." Haruki gave him the crooked smile. "He did it for fifty years. We can do it for a week."

At six o'clock exactly, Rei felt the arcade change.

It was very gentle. The air went heavy along the tiles, and cold, and grey, as it had on Sunday night. But this time Rei's door was shut, and he only felt it pressing on the outside, like weather on a window. In front of the fish shop, nothing moved. The padlock did not move. The shutter did not move.

But Rei knew, the way you know someone has come into a room behind you, that an old man in rubber boots and a long apron and a white towel round his head had just bent down to unlock a padlock that was rusted shut, and was pushing up a shutter that was not going up, and was not surprised, because it had not gone up for years and he did it anyway.

Haruki closed his eyes.

Rei could not see anything happen. Haruki just stood there in his green sweater, with his hands in his pockets and his eyes closed and his head a little on one side, as if he were listening to somebody tell a long story in a quiet voice. His face went still. Then it went sad. Then, very slowly, his mouth moved, and Rei understood that he was answering.

"His name is Ōta," said Haruki, without opening his eyes. His voice was low and careful, as if he were carrying something full to the brim. "Ōta Kichizō. He opened in 1958 with his father. His father died in 1971. He kept it open. His wife did the books. She died in 1999. He kept it open." A pause. "He knows. He's known for years. He doesn't mind being dead. He minds the shop."

"Why?"

Haruki was quiet. Listening.

"Because nobody said thank you," he said.

Rei looked down the arcade. At the shutters, one after another. At the notices taped to them, curling in the damp. *Closed. Thank you for many years.* *Closed. Thank you for many years.* On every shutter, from every family that had gone. Somebody had written it for them. Somebody had put it up, and the neighbours had read it, and that had been the end. A proper end. A bow at the door.

Nobody had written one for Uokichi. Ōta Kichizō had died behind his counter, probably early one morning, probably with the shutter halfway up. And there had been nobody left in the arcade to put up a notice.

"Itsuki," Rei said.

Itsuki was already unrolling the paper on the tiles. He knelt over it with the brush, and opened the ink, and for a moment did not move. Then he wrote. Rei had seen Itsuki paint a thousand times and had never once seen him write characters with a brush, slowly, properly, the way old men did at New Year. He did not know Itsuki could. The strokes went down black and thick and sure.

*UOKICHI. Ōta Kichizō. 1958 to 2010. Thank you for many years.*

Itsuki sat back on his heels and looked at it. His hands were shaking a little.

"My grandmother made us practise," he said, to nobody. "Every Sunday. I hated it."

He taped it to the shutter, under the smiling fish, with four long strips of tape at the corners, pressing each one down flat with his thumb.

"Now, Rei," said Haruki.

[break What Rei said]

Rei opened the door in his chest.

Not all the way. Enough. The grey came in, cold and slow, but this time Haruki was there, somewhere just behind Rei's eyes, a calm steady presence like a hand on the back of a chair, and the grey did not fill him. It stopped. It looked at him. For the first time in fifty years, an old man behind a fish counter looked up and saw a customer.

Rei bowed. Low, from the waist, the way you bow to someone who has served you well for a long time.

"Thank you for many years," he said.

Haruki picked up the red broom and began, slowly, to sweep the tiles in front of the shop.

Nothing happened for a long moment. The tap dripped somewhere. The tofu woman's radio said that it would be fine today, clear skies, a high of twenty-four.

Then the grey moved.

It did not rush. It went the way a tide goes out, slowly, a little at a time. It drew back from the tiles and off the shutters and out of the cold corner upstairs. Rei felt it pass him, close, and he felt in it something that was not sadness any more. Something like a man taking off a long rubber apron at the end of a day and hanging it on a hook. Something like a sigh.

And it went *through* him.

That was the part nobody had told him about. His door was open, and the old man was leaving, and the only way out that Rei could feel was the one standing open in his own chest. The grey went through it, toward the far side of everything, gently, like a guest going out through the nearest door. And it took Rei with it.

[break The white]

He did not fall. There was nothing to fall through.

One moment he was standing on the cold tiles of the Kamiya arcade, with Haruki sweeping and Itsuki kneeling and the tofu woman's radio talking about the weather. The next moment there was no arcade.

There was white.

Not a white room. Not snow. White in every direction, without edges, without a floor or a ceiling or a horizon. He could not see his hands. He was not sure he had hands. It did not hurt his eyes. It was simply the only thing there was.

Ahead of him, very far away, the old man was walking. Rei could not see him. He could feel him, a small grey shape going away across the white, getting lighter, getting smaller, not looking back. Then he could not feel him at all.

And behind everything, so far away that distance meant nothing, there was a light.

He knew it was there the way you know the sun is there with your eyes shut. It was enormous and quiet and steady. It was working. He did not know how he knew that, but he did. And everything in him wanted to turn round and look at it.

He did not turn round.

There had been a drawing in the fat folder on the shelf in Itsuki's studio. A thin column of light in white chalk on black paper. And under it, in Gin's small exact pencil handwriting, one word. *Don't.*

He kept his eyes on the place where the old man had gone. He did not look at the light.

He remembered a page in the book that he had read at three in the morning and not understood. *Nobody goes through by choosing to. If you are ever pushed through, you will get one question. Ask it fast and ask it well. Then you will be sent back, and you will pay for it.*

One question.

There had only ever been one.

*Is my brother alive?*

He did not say it with a mouth. He did not know if he had a mouth. He said it with all of himself, into the white.

For a moment nothing happened. Then the answer came. It did not come in words. It came from behind him, from the light he was not looking at, warm and steady, filling him from the inside, all at once, the way heat comes into cold hands.

*Yes.*

And then the white pushed him back, gently and completely, the way the sea puts something back on the sand.

[break Back]

He woke on his back on the tiles of the arcade with the yellow roof above him, gone pale gold now with the morning.

Itsuki was kneeling over him with Rei's head in his lap. His face was white and wet and there was ink on it, and he was saying Rei's name over and over, very fast, as if he were trying to stop it from getting away. Haruki knelt on Rei's other side with two fingers pressed to Rei's neck. The red broom lay on the tiles beside him where he had dropped it.

"I'm here," said Rei. His voice was a croak.

Itsuki made a noise that was half a laugh and half something else, and bent over until his forehead was on Rei's chest, and stayed there.

"Two hours," said Haruki quietly. He did not take his fingers away. "You were breathing. Your eyes were open. There was nobody behind them. I went after you as far as I could." His voice shook, just once. "I couldn't go further. I've never not been able to go further."

"Where was I?"

Haruki looked at him for a long time.

"You went through," he said. "You opened the door to let him out, and you went out with him, much too far. Nobody goes through on purpose, Rei. Nobody sane. Your nose bled all over Itsuki's paper." He took his hand away at last, and it was trembling. "Did you see anything?"

Rei thought about it. About the white. About the light behind everything that he had not turned round to look at.

"I asked something," he said. "Somebody answered."

Haruki did not ask what. He looked at Rei's face, and Rei felt him look, very lightly, at the surface, the way Kato did. Then Haruki closed his eyes and let out a long breath and did not say anything at all.

Rei turned his head on Itsuki's knees.

The fish shop shutter was still down. The notice was still taped under the smiling fish, a little spotted with blood at one corner. *Thank you for many years.* The arcade was just an arcade. The air along the tiles was ordinary air. Cold, because it was early, and smelling of rust and old fish and the tofu woman's water tanks. Nothing else.

Above them, a window opened.

Mr. Hirose leaned out of the window of number 3, in a grey T-shirt and pyjama trousers, with his hair standing up on one side. He looked down at the three of them on the tiles, a young man lying in another young man's lap with blood on his face, and a third holding a red broom, under a notice on a closed fish shop.

He did not seem to find it strange. He looked like a man who had just woken up and could not remember the last time he had woken up like this.

"Good morning," he said, puzzled.

"Good morning," said Haruki.

Mr. Hirose looked at the sky above the arcade roof for a while. Then he took a deep breath of the cold air, all the way down, and went back inside, and left the window open.

[break What it cost]

Rei slept for twenty hours.

When he woke up he could not open the door in his chest at all. He tried, very carefully, lying in bed, and it would not move. It was not locked. It was too heavy, like a limb that has gone dead from being slept on. For six days he could not feel anything that anyone else could not feel. He walked through Ōji station at rush hour and it was only a station. It frightened him more than anything that had happened since August.

He could not keep food down for two days. Itsuki slept on the sofa for all six, and made rice porridge that was too salty, and did not go to class, and did not mention it.

On the seventh day the door opened a crack again, by itself, while Rei was standing at the kitchen window. He felt the building around him, the old woman downstairs and her radio, the boy across the walkway practising the trumpet badly. He sat down on the floor and put his face in his hands.

But behind his eyes, very quietly, something was humming. A thin, steady note, like a light left on in another room. It had been there since Kamiya. He did not think it was going to go away.

*Yes,* he thought. *He's alive.*

Three weeks later Itsuki sent him a photograph without any words. It was the plant shop by Ōji station, and in the doorway, bending over a tray of small ferns, choosing one, was a man in a white office shirt that fitted him, with a little colour in his face.
