#title The Platform

The next day was Friday, and Rei did what Kumagai had told him to do. He stayed home and waited.

He was not good at it. Nobody is. He sat on the sofa with Gin's book on his knees and read until the words stopped meaning anything, and then he got up and walked round the flat, and then he sat down again. He read about thin places and how they move with the seasons. He read about salt, and iron, and what chalk is actually for. He read a page about how to tell a grieving spirit from a hungry one, and it was written so calmly, in such small neat letters, that he had to stop halfway down and go and stand on the balcony for a while with a cigarette, looking at nothing.

The cold key lay on the kitchen table all day, on a folded tea towel, in a small damp circle of its own water.

Itsuki texted at noon. *eaten?* Rei sent back a photograph of a rice ball. Itsuki sent back *thats a photo of a rice ball not a photo of u eating a rice ball. i know ur tricks.* Rei ate the rice ball, and sent a photograph of the empty wrapper, and Itsuki sent back a thumbs up and a small drawing of a raccoon.

At two o'clock the phone buzzed again, and it was not Itsuki.

*Come to the Division tomorrow at nine. Give your name at the front desk. I will see you myself. Adachi.*

Rei looked at it for a long time. Twenty-two hours after he had sent his four polite lines, the Director of the Civil Veil Division had answered them. Not with *on assignment*. Not with *extended operations*. With a time, and a door, and *myself*.

He wrote back *Thank you. I'll be there.* Then he sat on the sofa with the phone in one hand and the cold key in the other, and for the first time since Wednesday night the waiting felt like it was going somewhere.

At six in the evening the flat had gone so quiet that Rei could hear the fridge thinking. He put the key in his pocket and walked down the hill to Ōji station, because he did not want to be inside any more, and because the long way round, through Akabane and back, took an hour and a half, and an hour and a half was an hour and a half of not sitting on the sofa.

[break The platform]

The platform was packed.

It was the Friday evening rush in the middle of Obon, and half of Tokyo seemed to be going somewhere with a bag. A family with two small children and a box of sweets wrapped in pretty paper for a grandmother. A group of office women in summer dresses on their way out to drink, laughing at something on one of their phones. A university student with a guitar case strapped to his back. An old man in a dark suit, much too heavy for August, holding a bunch of white chrysanthemums against his chest. Chrysanthemums are the flowers you take to a grave in Japan. He was going to visit somebody.

[img place_oji_station_dusk.png | Ōji station]

The heat lay on the platform thick and still, like a blanket. Paper lanterns hung along the edge of the roof and turned slowly in the warm air coming up from the tracks. A calm recorded voice said that the next Keihin-Tōhoku line train would arrive in three minutes.

Rei stood near the middle of the platform, beside a vending machine. And beside the vending machine, a man was buying a coffee.

He was about forty-five. A grey suit, the jacket folded over his arm. A white shirt gone soft at the collar after a long day. A wedding ring. A clear plastic umbrella from a konbini hooked over his wrist, although it had not rained for a week. He was the kind of man you see a thousand times a day in Tokyo and never once remember. He pressed the button for a hot canned coffee, not a cold one, which seemed strange in that heat, and the machine dropped it into the tray with a clunk. He bent down to take it out.

And he stopped.

He stopped in the middle of the movement, bent over, with his hand in the tray. Not the way a man stops when he remembers he has left the gas on. The way a video stops when it freezes. His body stayed exactly where it was, half folded, perfectly still, and the rest of the platform went on moving round him.

The cold reached Rei a second later.

It came off the man the way heat comes up off a road in summer. It rolled over Rei's arms and down the back of his neck, and every hair on his skin stood up. The pressure behind his eyes did not begin softly this time. It hit him, hard, like a hand pressed flat over both eyes. He breathed in for four. He held for four. He began to breathe out, and the door in his chest would not close.

Something was leaning on it from the other side.

[break What it looked like]

The man straightened up.

He did it slowly, and in the wrong order. A person stands up back first and head last. This man stood up head first. His head lifted, and tilted back, and kept tilting back, until his face was pointing straight up at the roof of the platform and the skin of his throat was stretched tight over the bones inside it. Then the rest of him followed, one part at a time, shoulders, chest, hips, the way a puppet comes up off a table when someone lifts it by a single string from the top of the skull.

The coffee can was still in his hand. Rei could see the steam coming off it.

The man's head came down again, and turned, and faced Rei.

His face had changed. It was still his face. The same nose, the same small mole beside the left eye, the same tired lines of a man who had worked too many years in an office with no windows. But it hung differently. The skin had gone slack, as if every small muscle that holds a face together had let go at the same moment. The cheeks had dropped. The lower lip hung away from the teeth. For one second it was the face of a man who had died sitting up in a chair.

Then the skin tightened again.

It tightened from the inside, all at once, the way a rubber glove goes tight when somebody pushes a hand into it. Rei watched the shape of the cheekbones come up under the skin. He watched the jaw set. He understood, with a cold certainty that went all the way down into his stomach, that something was wearing this man now. And that it was not the same size as him.

The eyes were the worst.

They were open much too wide. The white showed all the way round. And the pupils had swollen. They had swollen until there was almost no brown left in either eye, only black, wet and shining, the way a cat's eyes go black in the dark just before it jumps. Rei had never seen a human being look at anything like that. It was a look of total, starving attention. As if the thing behind those eyes could feel every person on the platform at once, every footstep, every heartbeat, every breath, and wanted all of them, and was already choosing.

And the eyes did not move together. The left one was fixed on Rei. The right one was looking somewhere past him, down at the tracks, and slowly sliding, as if it were searching the ground for something it had dropped. They moved separately, like the eyes of a lizard. They did not blink. Not once.

Then the man smiled.

The corners of his mouth went up. They kept going up, much further than a smile should go, out toward the ears, until the lips were pulled thin and white against the teeth. It was the bottom half of a smile with nothing above it. The skin round the eyes did not move. There were no lines at the corners, no crinkle, no warmth at all. Only the mouth stretched, wider, and wider, until Rei could see the lower teeth and the pink of the gums under them, and a thin string of spit running from the corner of the lip down to the chin.

"There you are," said the man.

The voice did not belong to the throat it came out of. It was too low, much too low, a deep flat sound like somebody talking into a metal bucket. And it did not breathe. When a person talks they take small breaths between the words without noticing. This voice did not. It came out in one long even line, every word at exactly the same height, like a recording played back too slowly. And at the end of each word there was a small wet click, the sound a tongue makes when it comes away from the roof of a dry mouth.

"There. You. Are." *Click.* *Click.* *Click.*

Round them, people had started to notice. Not the way Rei noticed. They noticed that a man in a grey suit was standing much too still and smiling at a young man by the vending machine, and that something about it was badly wrong, though none of them could have said what. The office women had stopped laughing. The mother had pulled both her children behind her legs without seeming to know she had done it. The old man with the chrysanthemums was staring, and the flowers were shaking in his hands.

"Sir?" said the student with the guitar, taking half a step forward, the way kind people do. "Sir, are you okay? Do you need to sit down?"

The man's right eye stopped sliding along the ground. It swung up, smoothly, and fixed on the student. The left eye stayed on Rei.

"So much room in you," the voice said to Rei. "So much empty room. Just like the other one."

Rei could not move. He knew he could not move because he was trying very hard and nothing was happening. His legs belonged to somebody else. The weight against the door in his chest was enormous now, and through the narrow gap at the edge of it he could feel the thing inside the man looking in at him. It was like standing in a dark room and knowing, without seeing, that somebody is standing in the corner. It was curious. It was hungry. It was very, very pleased with itself.

*The other one.*

"Where is he?" said Rei. His voice came out small and cracked, a boy's voice. "Where's my brother?"

The smile stretched one more millimetre. A thin line of blood appeared at the corner of the man's mouth, where the lip had started to split.

The recorded voice announced that the train was arriving. Rei heard it coming. He felt the wind that a train pushes ahead of itself, warm, smelling of hot metal and brakes. The lights came round the curve.

The man walked backward.

He did not turn round. He did not look. He took one step back, and another, toward the edge of the platform, with the hot coffee still in his hand and the umbrella swinging from his wrist and his left eye still on Rei, wide and white and never blinking. The yellow line at the edge of the platform passed under his heels.

"Stop," said Rei. Or he thought he said it. Afterward he was never sure.

The man said one more word. It was not in the deep voice. It was in a different voice, an ordinary man's voice, high and frightened and very quiet. The voice of somebody who has just woken up in a place he does not recognise.

"Haruna?"

Then he stepped back off the edge.

[break Personal injury accident]

Rei did not see the train hit him. He had shut his eyes.

He heard it. He heard the scream of the brakes, long and high and metal, and under it a sound he would hear for months afterward, every time he tried to fall asleep. A short, heavy, final sound. Then people screaming. Then the train, still sliding, still braking, the whole long length of it going past him with its windows full of faces turning to look.

When he opened his eyes, the clear plastic umbrella was lying on the platform in front of his shoes, turning slowly on its side.

The cold had gone. The pressure behind his eyes had gone. The door in his chest swung loose, unlatched, and nothing was leaning on it any more. Whatever had been wearing the man had left him the moment he stopped being useful.

The coffee can had rolled back across the platform and come to rest against the vending machine. It was still steaming.

A voice came over the speakers, calm and pleasant and recorded. *Due to a personal injury accident at Ōji station, Keihin-Tōhoku line services are suspended. We apologise for the inconvenience.*

When somebody falls or jumps in front of a train in Tokyo, the announcement calls it a *personal injury accident*. It happens often enough that the city needed a phrase for it, and the phrase it chose is gentle and tells you nothing at all. All over the city that evening, on other platforms, people would hear those words and sigh and look at their phones and find another way home. That is not cruelty. It is just what a very big city does with things it cannot afford to look at for long.

[break The cleaning]

The CVD arrived in eleven minutes.

Rei counted. He was sitting on a bench by then, though he did not remember sitting down. His hands were shaking so badly that he had put them between his knees and pressed his knees together to hold them still.

The first ones were field officers. Three of them, in plain grey clothes with a small white owl badge on the chest. They moved fast and talked little. A woman walked the whole length of the platform with her eyes half closed and one hand held out flat in front of her, palm down, as if she were feeling for the heat off a stove. A man climbed down onto the tracks without anybody telling him to. The third stood very still beside the vending machine, exactly where the man in the grey suit had stood, and after a moment he lifted his head and looked straight at Rei. Then he looked away again, quickly, the way you look away from somebody you have recognised and do not want to talk to.

The second group arrived at nineteen minutes, and they were different.

[need ch4_public_confidence | After an accident on a station platform, calm smiling officials in good dark blue suits crouching beside shaken witnesses, handing them water and small white cards, a mother with two children nodding slowly]

They wore good suits, dark blue and charcoal grey, well cut, and they carried tablets and clipboards held against their chests. They smiled. They were the calmest, most pleasant people Rei had ever seen in his life. They went to each witness one at a time and crouched down to their level and spoke very softly. They gave each person a cold bottle of water and a small white card. Rei could read the one somebody had left on the bench beside him. It had a telephone number for counselling, and at the bottom, in small letters under the smiling white owl, *Office of Public Confidence*.

He watched them with the mother.

At first she was crying and shaking her head and pointing at the edge of the platform, over and over. Rei heard her say *he was smiling, he was smiling at that boy*. The woman in the blue suit nodded and nodded and held the mother's hand in both of hers and talked to her, gently, for a long time. Rei could not hear the words. But he watched the mother's face change while she listened. The fear went out of it slowly, the way water goes out of a bath when you pull the plug. When the woman in the blue suit finally stood up, the mother was nodding too, and wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist, and Rei heard her say it, quite clearly.

"The heat. Yes. He must have felt faint. The poor man."

They did the same with the office women. They did the same with the student with the guitar, who argued for much longer, getting louder and pointing at Rei, and then, quite suddenly, stopped arguing and sat down. They did the same with the old man with the chrysanthemums. He had dropped his flowers on the platform when the train came. When they had finished with him he bent down and picked them up and stood looking at them as if he could not remember why he was holding them.

Twenty minutes. That was all it took. Twenty minutes, and a man had stood by a vending machine and felt faint in the heat and fallen. A terrible accident. The kind of thing that happens in August.

One of the blue suits was walking toward Rei. A young man with a kind round face and a tablet held to his chest. He was already smiling.

And somebody sat down on the bench between them, so close to Rei that their shoulders touched, and said, very quietly, without looking at him, "Don't talk to him."

[img char_okada-shizuka.png | Okada Shizuka]

It was a woman of about thirty. She wore a plain dark suit, not nearly as good as the blue ones, a little shiny at the elbows. Her hair was tied back so tightly it looked painful. There were three small notebooks in her jacket pocket, held together with a rubber band. She did not smile. She had the flat, tired, unimpressed face of somebody who has read everybody's file and was not surprised by a single one of them.

She lifted one hand toward the young man in the blue suit. Just two fingers, held up, not even looking at him. He stopped walking. He stood there for a second, still smiling, with his tablet against his chest. Then he turned round and went to talk to somebody else instead.

Rei stared at the two fingers. Then at her.

"Who are you?"

"Okada. Investigation. Kita district." She was watching the blue suits work their way down the platform, her eyes moving from one to the next, the way a teacher watches a classroom during an exam. "You saw it."

"I saw a man walk backwards off a platform. Smiling at me."

"Yes."

"There was something in him."

"Yes."

"It *talked* to me." His voice cracked on the word and he hated it, hated how young it sounded. "It said *there you are*. It said I had room in me. It said *just like the other one*."

At that, Okada turned her head and looked at him properly for the first time.

Her eyes were dark brown and very steady. She looked at his face, at the grey skin and the shaking jaw. Then her eyes went down, to his trouser pocket, where his right hand had gone without his noticing and closed tight round the cold key. Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not much. A very small thing, there and then gone, like somebody who has been waiting a long time for a bus and has just seen it turn the corner.

"Uedera Rei," she said.

He went very still.

"How do you know my name?"

"Your brother asked me to keep an eye out for you." She stood up and pushed her hands into her jacket pockets, and looked down at him with that flat unimpressed face. "I've been keeping it out for four years. Get up. We're not having this conversation here."
