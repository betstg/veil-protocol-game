#title The Paintings

Itsuki moved in at the end of August.

He did not ask. He arrived on a Sunday afternoon in Takuya's car, which still smelled of dog and incense, with eleven canvases, four boxes of paint, a mattress tied to the roof with washing line, and Sei, who carried everything up three flights of stairs without a word and then stood in the genkan looking at the size of the flat with an expression of polite horror.

"There are three rooms," said Sei.

"Two and a kitchen," said Itsuki. "I'm having the small one. Gin's got his. Rei's got his. I've got the one with the washing machine in it." He pushed past his brother with a box of brushes. "I gave up the Ueno flat. I told Dad the family could stop paying for it. I told him I'd pay my own rent now." He put the box down. "I haven't worked out how yet."

Gin was standing in the kitchen doorway with a cup of tea, watching all of it.

"You're paying rent?" he said.

"Yes."

"To me."

"To Okada, technically. She's the guarantor." Itsuki looked at him. "Is that a problem?"

Gin looked at the canvases stacked against the wall of the hall, and the mattress on the walkway, and the painter with white paint still under his fingernails from a freight car in Tabata. He looked at Rei, who was sitting on the floor of the hall with his back against the wall, trying very hard not to smile.

"No," said Gin. "It's not a problem." He went back into the kitchen. "Don't use my mug."

[break The room]

The small room had a window onto the back of the next building, and a washing machine in the corner, and by the end of the first week it had nothing else in it that anybody could see, because every surface was covered in paper.

Not new paintings. Old ones. Itsuki had brought everything from the Ueno flat, and he had not unpacked it so much as let it fall where it landed. Canvases leaning three deep against the walls. Sketchbooks in towers on the floor. Rolls of cheap drawing paper on top of the washing machine. Charcoal on the window sill.

He did not paint. Rei noticed that. He had always painted. Every day since he was thirteen. In the first week of September he sat on his mattress in the small room in the evenings with the window open and looked at the walls and did not pick up a brush once.

"Are you all right?" said Rei, on the Thursday, from the doorway.

"Yes," said Itsuki. He was looking at a canvas against the far wall. A big one. The one he had painted the summer before, and shown at the Geidai, and hung in the corridor of the studio the night Rei came with a cold key in his hand. A walkway, at night, under an orange lamp. A brass key lying on the concrete outside a front door.

"I finished that at midnight," said Itsuki, "on the twelfth of August. Last year." He did not look away from it. "The key came home at a quarter to four."

"I know."

"I didn't know what it meant. I thought it was just a good painting." He was quiet. "I've been going through all of it. Since the yard. Every sketchbook. Every canvas. Everything I've done since I was thirteen." He turned his head and looked at Rei. "Rei. I think they're all like that."

[break The family]

The Kuroda came on the second Sunday in September.

All of them. Rei heard them on the stairs before he saw them. Too many feet. A walking stick. Sei saying something low and apologetic. And Grandmother Mitsu's voice, very clear, saying that she had not climbed three flights of stairs since the Tokyo Olympics and did not intend to make a habit of it.

Gin opened the door.

Kuroda Sōun stood on the walkway in a dark summer kimono, very upright, with a walking stick he was plainly ashamed of. He was thinner. The silver hair was whiter. He had slept for nine days after the yard and woken up and asked for his son, and his son had not come, and he had asked again every day for two weeks.

Behind him, Grandmother Mitsu, small and fierce, leaning on nobody. Sei with his hands in his pockets. And a woman Rei had never met.

She was about fifty, slight, with her hair pinned up loosely and coming down at the back, in a plain grey dress with a cardigan over it. She had paint on her fingers. Blue and yellow, dried into the creases of her knuckles. She had Itsuki's face. The same quick dark eyes. The same mouth.

"Kuroda Yae," she said to Gin, and bowed. "I'm his mother. I'm sorry to come without warning." She looked past Gin into the narrow hall, at the canvases stacked against the walls. "He wouldn't have let us come if we'd warned him."

Gin stood aside.

They came in, one after another, and took their shoes off in the genkan, which was not large enough for six pairs of shoes, and stood in the narrow hall among the paintings. Itsuki came out of the small room with charcoal on his face and stopped dead.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Sōun did a thing Rei had never seen the head of a Tower family do. He put down his walking stick against the wall. And he knelt, slowly, on the floor of a rented flat in Jūjō, among the canvases, and put his hands flat on the boards in front of him, and bowed until his forehead nearly touched them.

"We came to apologise," he said. To the floor. "All of us. For the hospital. For the Division. For calling it the bones of the trade. For ten years of not asking you what you wanted." He did not lift his head. "For not answering you in the hall when you were ten."

Behind him, Grandmother Mitsu lowered herself to her knees with a great deal of grumbling and bowed too. Then Sei. Then Itsuki's mother.

Itsuki stood in the doorway of the small room with charcoal on his face and stared at four of his family kneeling on the floor of the hall.

"Get up," he said. His voice cracked. "Get up. Dad. Your back. Get up, all of you, you're going to knock the paintings over."

[break What they saw]

They did knock a painting over.

Grandmother Mitsu did it, getting up. Her elbow caught the edge of a canvas leaning against the wall and it slid sideways and fell face up on the floor of the hall, and everybody looked down at it.

It was a long white room. Cushions on the floor in a circle. People sitting on them with teacups. And on the wall behind them, faces. Hundreds of photographs, pinned edge to edge. It was painted in grey and white and a very pale green, and nobody in it had a face at all. Only smooth pale ovals where the faces should be.

"That's Asagaya," said Rei.

He did not mean to say it. It came out of him.

Itsuki's mother crouched down and looked at it. She did not touch it.

"When did you paint this?" she said.

Itsuki did not answer.

"He painted it in April last year," said Rei. "I've seen the sketchbook. It's dated. He didn't know what it was. Nobody did. Gin hadn't even been there yet."

Yae looked up at her son. Then she stood up, very slowly, and looked round the hall. At the canvases against the walls. At the stacks. She went to the next one, and tilted it out from the wall to see its face.

A shrine yard full of paper kites. A tall man in a grey jacket standing very still in the crowd. On the far side, a young man with a crow on his shoulder, laughing.

The next. A green telephone box in the rain, with a small black pager on the pavement in front of it.

The next. A rusted freight car in a field of weeds, with a white door painted on its side.

She stood in the narrow hall of a flat in Jūjō and went from canvas to canvas, and her face did not change, and her hands were shaking.

Sōun had not moved. He was standing by the wall with his stick, looking at the painting of the kite market. At the tall man in the crowd.

"That was February," he said quietly. "This year." He looked at his son. "When did you paint it?"

Itsuki's jaw worked.

"October," he said at last. "The October before. I didn't know who he was. I didn't know it was Gin. I just painted a tall man in a crowd." He looked at the floor. "I've been trying to hide it. Since the yard. I thought if I didn't paint any more, it would stop. I thought if nobody saw them, it wouldn't be true." His voice went very small. "It's not doors, Dad. I can't open a single door. I've never been able to. It's this."

[break The seer]

Grandmother Mitsu was the one who said the word.

She had sat down on the step stool from the bathroom, which Gin had brought her without being asked, and she was looking at the painting of the walkway with the key, which Itsuki had carried out of the small room and propped against the kitchen door.

"My grandmother's sister had it," she said. "Kuroda Shige. In the Taishō years. She painted fans. She painted a fan with a fire on it in the summer of 1923 and gave it to my grandmother, and in September the city burned." She looked at Itsuki. "They said she was mad. They said she was a fraud. They sent her to the country." She was quiet. "She was neither. She saw. That's all. It's very rare. It's so rare that nobody believes it, and so many liars claim it that nobody believes the ones who have it. It isn't even in the Division's files. They threw it out of the files in the fifties, with the fortune tellers."

Nobody said anything.

"You're not a door," said Grandmother Mitsu. "You never were. You're a window."

Itsuki sat down on the floor of the hall, quite suddenly, with his back against the wall, among the paintings.

His mother came and knelt in front of him. She took his hand. The one with charcoal on it. She turned it over and looked at the palm, and the fingers, and the black under the nails, and then she put her own hand beside it. Paint in the creases of her knuckles. Blue and yellow.

"I used to see you in the doorway of the studio," she said. "Every evening. When the training was done and I could stop being what the house needed and just paint. You'd sit there for hours." She held his hand. "I thought you were watching me paint. You weren't, were you. You were watching me be myself."

Itsuki's face crumpled.

"I didn't know you could see," she said. "I'm so sorry. I should have known. I saw your hands."

[break The house]

Sōun waited until the tea had been made.

Gin made it, too strong, in the brown pot with the chipped lid, and there were not enough cups, so Rei and Sei drank out of glasses, and Grandmother Mitsu said the tea was terrible and drank two cups. Then Sōun set his cup down on the floor beside his knee, where he was sitting, very upright, with his back against the wall of the hall, and looked at his younger son.

"The house should be led," he said, "by somebody who can see what it will cost before it does a thing."

Itsuki looked up.

"Not by somebody who opens doors because his father opened doors and his grandfather opened doors and nobody ever asked whether they should be opened," said Sōun. "I opened every door I was asked to open, for forty years, for whoever asked. For the families. For the Division. For men I should not have opened anything for." He looked at Rei. "One of those men bought seal paper for a woman in Public Confidence. Fujiwara-Kage is going to find that out next month, and he is going to be right, and I am going to sign whatever he puts in front of me." He looked back at Itsuki. "I am tired. I would like to sit in the garden and watch your mother paint. I would like you to take the house."

Itsuki stared at him.

"I'm not even a Kuroda," he said. "Not a real one. I can't open a cupboard."

"The house is not doors," said Sōun. "I thought it was, for sixty years. It is what we decide to do with them." He picked up his cup again. "You decided to come to me and ask. You decided to paint a street on a freight car for the dead. You decided to stand in front of a door with your brother at your back and not let go." He drank. "That is what the house is. I had forgotten. You remembered."

Sei, by the kitchen door, with a glass of tea, did not say anything. But he lifted the glass, very slightly, toward his younger brother.

Itsuki looked at all of them. At his father with his ashamed stick. At his grandmother on the step stool. At his mother with paint in her knuckles. At Sei. At Gin in the kitchen doorway, watching. At Rei.

"If I take it," he said slowly, "we stop selling things. All of it. Favours. Secrets. Gates for whoever pays. We stop. We do it honestly or we don't do it. We tell the Division what we can do and we do it with them, not for them, not against them. No forms. No filling in a form instead of talking to somebody." He looked at his father. "And nobody in the house ever says *bones of the trade* again. Ever."

"Yes," said Sōun.

"And I'm not living in Nezu."

"No," said his father. "I didn't imagine you would."

[break The last painting]

They left at six. Grandmother Mitsu went down the stairs one at a time, complaining about each one. Sōun stopped at the door and looked back down the hall at the canvases, and at his son standing among them, and bowed again, a small one this time, the bow you give somebody you will see next week.

Yae stayed a moment longer.

"Will you keep painting?" she said.

Itsuki thought about it for a long time.

"No," he said. "Not like that. Not every day." He looked at the canvases. "If I keep painting, I'll keep finding out things I don't need to know yet. What's going to happen to Rei. To Gin. To you. When. How." He swallowed. "I don't want to know. I want to find out. Like everybody else. I want to have a Tuesday and not know what's going to happen on Wednesday."

His mother looked at him.

"But if somebody comes," said Itsuki. "If somebody comes to the house with something really bad. Something nobody else can see the end of." He shrugged. "Then I'll paint them an answer. One. And then I'll stop again."

Yae put her hand on his cheek, very lightly, with the paint in her knuckles, and went down the stairs after the others.

Itsuki stood in the doorway of the flat for a long time after she had gone.

Then he went into the small room, and Rei heard him moving things, and when Rei looked in, Itsuki was turning the canvases round. One by one. Face to the wall. All of them. Except one.

The walkway at night, under the orange lamp. The key on the concrete.

He carried it out into the hall and hung it on the nail by the door, where Gin's old calendar used to be. Beside the hook. Where the real key hung now, on its steel ring, every night.

"That one I'm keeping," he said, and straightened it with one finger, and went back into the small room to turn the rest of them round to the wall.
