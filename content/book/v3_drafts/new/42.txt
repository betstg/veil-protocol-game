#title Mori

Mori Takao found him on a Tuesday night, in the boiler room under Tsuru-no-yu.

Rei had not meant to be there. Hara Setsu had sent him. He was sitting in her kitchen after his bath, telling her how he was, *properly, not fine*, when she put down her knitting and said, "The boiler's making a noise. Go and look at it."

"I don't know anything about boilers."

"Nobody's asking you to fix it." She picked her knitting up again. "Go and look at it. Down the stairs behind the laundry. Take a towel. It's hot."

[img scene_rei_mori_boiler.png | The boiler room under Tsuru-no-yu]

The stairs went down behind the laundry, steep and narrow, into the dark. At the bottom there was a long low room with a concrete floor, and the heat hit Rei in the face like an open oven. The boiler filled half the room. It was huge and black and very old, a great iron belly with pipes coming out of it in every direction, and a round iron door in its front, open, and through the door a fire. A real fire. Wood and coal, roaring, orange and white. Tsuru-no-yu was one of the last baths in Tokyo still heated by fire.

A man was sitting on an upturned crate in front of the open door, feeding the fire with lengths of wood.

He was big. Not tall, exactly, but heavy and broad and dense, like something built to hold up a roof. Black hair cut very short. A plain dark shirt with the sleeves rolled up over thick forearms. He was about fifty. His face, lit orange by the fire, was hard and tired and severe, with deep lines from the nose to the mouth and two deeper lines between the eyebrows that looked as if they had been there for twenty years and would never go away.

He did not look up. He threw another length of wood into the fire.

"Sit," he said. His voice was low and rough, like gravel in a bucket.

There was another crate. Rei sat on it. The heat from the fire was so strong that within a minute his face was wet with sweat.

"Hoshino says you ate his mackerel," said the man. "Tanizaki says you drank his third cup. Setsu says you sit in her kitchen and tell her how you are." He threw in another piece of wood. "I don't feed people. I don't make tea. And I don't care how you are." He turned his head and looked at Rei for the first time.

His eyes were dark and very steady and full of something Rei had never seen in a face before. Not anger exactly. Anger was hot and fast and went away. This was slow. Deep. Old. Like a coal that has been burning at the bottom of a fire for twenty years, so long that it has forgotten what it is like to be anything else.

"Mori," said the man. "I hold Rage."

[break What rage is]

They sat in front of the fire for a long time without talking. Mori fed it. Rei sweated.

"You know what people think rage is?" said Mori at last. "Shouting. Hitting. Breaking things. Some idiot in a bar." He threw in wood. "That's not rage. That's a tantrum. Rage is what you feel when you watch something happen that shouldn't happen. To someone who didn't deserve it. And you can't stop it." He watched the fire. "A child hit by a car. An old woman cheated out of her savings. A man who does his job right for twenty years and gets thrown away. Rage is the thing in you that says *no*. That says *this is wrong*. That gets up in the morning and goes to fight it, even when you know you can't win." He was quiet for a moment. "It's the most useful feeling in the world. It's also the one that eats you alive if you hold it long enough."

"How long have you held it?"

"Twenty-one years." Mori looked at the fire. "I was twenty-seven. The man who held it before me burned out. Literally. Heart. At a protest in Shinjuku. I was standing next to him. The current came into me like a train." He threw in another piece of wood. "I've been angry every second since."

Rei did not know what to say. He looked at the fire.

"Your brother came here," said Mori. "Three times. Sat on that crate. Asked me questions." He looked at Rei. "He was the only person in the Division I ever respected. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because he was angry too." Mori's face did not change. "Not like me. He didn't hold it. It wasn't his current. But he saw the same things I see. The Division covering things up. The families voting on people like they were furniture. Old people dying along the river while men in nice suits made sure nobody asked why. He saw all of it. And he was angry. And he never once shouted about it. He just got up every morning and went to work and did his job right, and wrote it all down in that black book, and tried, one case at a time, to fix it." Mori threw the last piece of wood into the fire and closed the iron door. The roar went quiet. The room went darker. "That's not rage. That's Duty. But it looks like rage from the outside, if you know what you're looking at."

[break The test]

Mori stood up. He was much bigger standing. He stood over Rei in the hot dark room with the fire ticking behind the iron door.

"I'm going to make you angry," he said. "I want to see what you do."

"Why?"

"Because if you come to the meeting, you'll sit in a room with twelve people who hold the feelings that keep this city from falling into the dark. And some of them are going to look at you and see what Hoshino saw. And what I see." Mori's eyes did not move from his face. "And when they do, some of them are going to be afraid. And frightened people say cruel things. And if you lose your temper in that room, Uedera, if you throw that door in your chest wide open in front of twelve holders, you could hurt someone. Or they could hurt you." He paused. "So. I'm going to make you angry."

"Okay," said Rei. His mouth was dry.

Mori looked at him.

"Your brother is dead," he said.

It went into Rei like a knife. Exactly like Itsuki's words in the training hall. *Gin is gone.* He felt the door in his chest jump. He felt the latch on his wrist go cold and hold, hard, like a hand gripping his arm.

"He's not," he said.

"He's dead. Something took him on the twelfth of August and it ate him like the thing on the river ate those old people. There's nothing left. You're looking for a body."

"He's not dead. I asked. The light told me."

"The light lies."

"The light doesn't lie." Rei was standing now. He did not remember standing. "You don't know anything about it. You sit in a basement and feed a fire and you don't know *anything*."

"I know your brother was stupid enough to go looking for the dark seat on his own," said Mori. His voice was very calm. "I know he left you behind in a flat with a cold key and no money. I know he lied to you for eleven years about what you are. I know he made you into a little copy of himself, all closed doors and good manners, and then he walked out and left you to fend for yourself." He leaned closer. "He abandoned you, Uedera. He chose the job over you. That's what Duty does. It always chooses the job."

Rei's hands were fists. The door in his chest was hammering against the latch. The latch was holding. Barely. He could feel it straining, the little iron bar in the hook, the bridge, the dented key. He could feel, behind the door, all of it. Everything he had. Everything he had been holding down since August. It was enormous. It was pressing to get out. It wanted to get out. It wanted to hit this man with everything it had.

He could have. He knew it. He could have lifted the latch and opened the door all the way and thrown it at him.

He thought of Tanizaki's garden. An hour. Not asking for anything.

He thought of Mrs. Tachibana's hand loosening on his wrist.

He thought of a clean bell ringing.

He breathed in. Four. Held it. Four. Breathed out. Six.

"You're doing it on purpose," he said. His voice was shaking. "You're saying it to see what I do."

"Yes."

"Okay." Rei breathed in again. "Then this is what I do." He unclenched his fists, slowly. One finger at a time. "My brother didn't abandon me. He went to look at something because he thought it would hurt people if nobody looked. And he left me a key, and a book, and a list of people who would help me. And they did. Every one of them." His voice cracked. He let it. "And you're on it. You're not on his list. But Hoshino sent me to you. So you're on mine."

Mori looked at him for a long moment.

Then he sat back down on his crate.

"Good," he said.

[break The invitation]

He opened the iron door of the boiler again and looked into the fire. The orange light came back over his face. He looked older in it. Very tired.

"Sorry," he said. He did not sound sorry. He sounded like a man who said sorry about once a year and did not enjoy it. "I had to know."

"Did I pass?"

"You didn't hit me." Mori picked up another length of wood. "That's more than half the holders managed, the first time I did that to them." He threw the wood in. "Hoshino cried. Kasumi set my coat on fire. Nakashima ran away for three days."

Rei sat down on his crate. His legs were shaking. The latch on his wrist was so cold it ached.

"The meeting's on the thirty-first of March," said Mori. "Midnight. The Grid Archive in Ochanomizu. Chen Lian holds Wonder. She'll let you in." He did not look at Rei. "You don't speak unless you're spoken to. You don't open that door. You sit and you listen. You're there to see us. And for us to see you."

"Why do you want them to see me?"

Mori fed the fire.

"Because the dark seat's been empty for seven years," he said. "And this city is running out of time. And when you walked into this room, Uedera, the fire changed colour." He nodded at the iron door. "Look."

Rei looked.

The fire in the old boiler, under Tsuru-no-yu, was burning. Orange and yellow and white, the way fire burns. But at the very bottom of it, at the base of the flames, there was a colour that had not been there before. A pale, cool, steady blue. Very faint. Very still. Like the blue at the bottom of a gas flame. Like a light that had been switched on in another room.

"That's not mine," said Mori quietly. "Rage burns orange. That's something else. That's what happens to a fire when somebody with the right shape sits in front of it." He closed the iron door, and the blue was gone. "Your brother made it do that too. Once. In 2013. Very faint." He stood up and wiped his hands on his trousers. "It's never been that bright."

He walked to the stairs. At the bottom step he stopped.

"Uedera."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry about what I said." He did not turn round. "About your brother. I don't think he's dead." A pause. "And I don't think he abandoned you. I think he was trying to find a way to keep you out of that chair." He started up the stairs. "I think he failed."
