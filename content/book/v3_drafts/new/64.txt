#title Cold Tea

*Gin.*

He was awake for all of it. The voice let him be.

They had been walking for an hour, down from the yellow kitchen, through the backstreets, the long way round, as the voice always went now, avoiding the main roads and the bright stations and the places where the Division had cameras. It was a hot night. The cicadas were screaming in every tree. Gin's body walked with its hands in its pockets and its long hair tied back, and Gin sat behind his own eyes and watched the streets go by, and for the first time in a year he was not fighting.

It was the twelfth of August. He knew that. He did not know if the voice knew what it meant.

He was nervous. That was the strange thing. He could feel the voice being nervous too, low down, in the stomach, like a train coming through the soles of your feet before you hear it.

*He said three cups,* said the voice. *Last week. On the step. Come on the twelfth, he said, there will be three cups.*

*I know. I was there.*

*He has never given me three.*

*He never gave me three until I was eleven.*

*Then it means something.*

*It means the boy will be there,* said Gin. *Rei won't be able to stay in the flat tonight. He'll go to Tanizaki.* He did not say why. The voice did not ask. *Tanizaki wants us in the same room.*

The voice was quiet for a long time. They turned into the old shopping street below Ōji. Shutters down. A cat on a step. The smell of the bakery long closed, and fried food from somewhere, and hot tarmac.

*He asked me my name,* said the voice. *In October. The first time. I did not have one to give him. On this side you are given one whether you want it or not, and nobody had given me one.* A pause. *I had one once. A long time ago. When I was a man. Before the far side. I have been trying to remember it. I think I have most of it now.* Another pause. *I would like to tell him. Tonight. Even with the boy there.*

Gin stopped walking.

He did not mean to. His feet stopped. The body stopped with them. For a second they were both standing in the middle of the empty shopping street, and neither of them was sure who had done it.

*You've never told anyone,* said Gin. *Not me.*

*No.* The voice did not move the feet again. It let them stand. *I will tell you first. On the way. If you like.*

Gin could not say anything at all.

*Walk,* said the voice gently. *We will be late. He does not like people to be late.*

[break The cup]

They felt it from the end of the lane.

Gin felt it first. It was his gift, not the voice's. He had been feeling places since he was nine, and Tanizaki's house he knew better than any place in the world except the flat in Jūjō. The long grass. The fig tree. The step. Forty years of the old man's patient writing woven through all of it like roots. He reached for it, two streets away, as he would reach for a light switch in a dark room he knew, and found the garden and the step and the open door with the yellow lamplight coming out of it.

And the windowsill.

A cup on the windowsill. Set apart from the others. Upside down.

The body stopped walking again. This time it was the voice.

*No,* said Gin.

*He has turned the cup.*

*I can feel that. No. Go. Keep walking. We're two streets away. We can be there in three minutes.*

*He has turned the cup,* said the voice again, very quietly, as if it were saying something in a language it had only just learned. *He has never told me anything. In all those months. He gave me the tea and the newspaper and he never once told me what to do.* A long pause. *Now he has told me. He has said, do not come.*

*Something's wrong. Something's wrong, that's why. Go.*

*Yes. Something is wrong. That is why he said it.*

The body sat down.

There was a bench outside a closed tobacconist's, under a dead street lamp, and the voice sat Gin's body down on it, with the knees together and the hands flat on the knees, as the boy sat at Tanizaki's table. And Gin, behind his own eyes, threw himself at the inside of his own skull like a dog at a locked door, and the voice let him, and did not get up.

*Let me go,* said Gin. *Let me go, let me go, Rei's in there, let me go...*

*He told us not to come.*

*I don't care what he told us...*

*You do,* said the voice. *You have cared what he told you since you were nine years old. You would not be who you are if you did not.* A pause. *I am not going against him. I do not know how. Nobody has ever told me anything that I wanted to obey before.*

[break What came]

They felt her pass.

Not saw. Felt. Like the cold that comes off an open freezer in a hot shop. The voice went rigid in Gin's chest. Down at the end of the shopping street, under the dead lamps, a crowd of people came out of a side alley, silent, slow, with white flowers held in front of them, and walked up toward Tanizaki's lane. And behind them, last of all, barefoot, in white, a woman.

She did not look toward the bench.

She knew they were there. Gin was sure of it. He felt the voice feel her know. But she did not look. She walked past the end of the street with her river stone swinging, and her smile, and she did not even turn her head.

*She is not here for me,* said the voice. Very slowly. *She is here for him.*

*For Rei...*

*No. For the old man.*

Gin felt it before he understood it. The voice knew things in its body, in his body, before it knew them in words. A cold went down through his chest and his arms and into his hands where they lay on his knees, and his hands began to shake.

*She cannot get me,* said the voice. *He has stopped me coming. So she will take the one who stopped me.* It was not a question. *She will take the door.*

Two streets away, through the garden he knew better than his own hands, Gin felt the old man come out onto the step with the teapot, and sit down cross-legged, and pour.

[break The step]

They watched it all.

The voice did not pull Gin away from it, and Gin did not try to look away. Through the house and the grass and the step, as his gift had always worked, not seeing but knowing, they felt the white woman walk up the path through the grass that did not bend. They felt her kneel. They felt her put her hand on the old man's face.

Two streets away, on a bench, they felt the old man hold. Like standing on a bridge and feeling a long train go underneath, through all the iron, into your feet. A great weight, a whole life of it, and one thin old man holding it up from underneath and not putting it down.

They watched him lift the cup.

*Have some tea,* Gin said, at the same moment the old man did. He did not know he was going to say it. It came out of him like a hand coming up to catch something thrown. *You came all this way. Have some tea.*

On the bench, Gin's hands stopped shaking.

They felt the woman not take the cup. They felt her get up and go. They felt the crowd come over the fence, and a broad man come over after them like a weight dropped into water, and the kitchen door at the back of the house come off its runners. They felt the boy go down on his knees on the boards. They felt the old man fold the newspaper in half, and in half again.

They felt his hand go still on top of it.

And then the garden was only a garden, full of people, and the thing Gin had reached for since he was nine years old was not in it any more.

[break The bench]

Gin did not know how long they sat there.

Long enough for the field cars to come, and the blue lights to go past the end of the shopping street, and the cicadas to start again, all at once, as if someone had turned them back on. Long enough for a man in a convenience-store uniform to come out of a side door with a bag of rubbish and look at them and go back inside.

He could feel the voice.

He had felt it for a year. Every part of it. Its patience. Its politeness. Its hunger, at first, for nothing but the one thing it had come through for. Its cold, the night on the kerb, when it could have mended a boy in the road and did not. Its curiosity, these last months, growing slowly like a plant in a dark room. He thought he knew all of it.

He had never felt this.

It was not in the chest. It was lower. It was in the place under the stomach where Gin had felt things when he was nine and had nobody to tell. It was heavy and it was cold and it would not move. It was like swallowing a stone. It was like the whole of the inside of him had been filled with wet sand.

*What is this,* said the voice.

It was not really asking. It was like a man saying *what is this* when he puts his hand in his pocket and finds a letter he did not know was there.

*He was nothing to me,* it said. *He was yours. I drank his tea and he gave me the newspaper and I went away. I did not come through for old men.* Its voice was very strange. *He was nothing to me, and something has been taken, and I cannot put it back. I can put anything back. Bones. Skin. I cannot put this back.*

Gin closed his eyes.

He was crying. He had not known. His body was crying, sitting on a bench outside a closed tobacconist's at midnight, with its hands flat on its knees, and he did not know if it was him or the voice, and then he understood that it did not matter. It was both. They were both crying. Out of the same eyes.

*That's grief,* said Gin.

The voice was quiet for a very long time.

*I have eaten grief,* it said at last. *Many times. In many people. I know how it tastes.* A pause. *I did not know how it weighs.*

*No.*

*How long does it weigh like this?*

Gin thought about being nine, and seeing faces on the stairs every day, and having nobody to tell, and knocking on a door behind Ōji because somebody on the Grid had given him a name. He thought about two cups of tea and a newspaper, and being sent home, and coming back, and being sent home again, for two years. He thought about it for a long time, and then he put it away, because it was his, and the voice did not need all of it.

*It gets lighter,* he said. *It doesn't go. It gets lighter. You get stronger. It's the same weight. You just get used to carrying it.*

*Like a cup.*

*Like a cup,* said Gin. *Yes.*

[break Two cups]

They went home before it got light. The long way round.

In the small yellow kitchen with the frosted window, the voice put the kettle on the gas ring. Gin let it. When the kettle whistled, the hands turned off the gas, and lifted the kettle, and poured the water into the clay teapot, slowly, as the old man had.

They took down two cups.

Then, after a long moment, a third.

The hands poured the first cup. And the second. And then they filled the third, very carefully, right to the top.

And the voice turned it upside down on the table, and the tea ran out over the wood in a wide hot puddle and dripped off the edge onto the floor, and neither of them moved to wipe it up.

*I will find her,* said the voice, very quietly. *I came through to end her, and I did not care what else she broke on the way. Now I care about one thing she broke.*

Gin did not say anything.

*Tachibana,* said the voice.

Gin looked at the cup. At the tea dripping.

*That was my name. When I was a man. Tachibana. There was more of it. I have not got the rest back yet.* A pause. *I was going to tell him first. I am telling you instead. He would have said that was correct.*

*Yes,* said Gin. *He would.*

They sat at the table in the yellow kitchen, in one body, with the tea dripping off the edge onto the floor, and drank the first two cups, one each, slowly, until they were cold. The voice did not push him down when they had finished. It let him stay where he was, close under the surface, behind the eyes, and they sat there together until the sun came up on the thirteenth.
