#title Cold Tea

*Gin.*

He was awake for all of it. The voice let him be.

They had been walking for an hour, down from the yellow kitchen, through the backstreets, the long way round, the way the voice always went now, avoiding the main roads and the bright stations and the places where the Division had cameras. It was a hot night. The cicadas were screaming in every tree. Gin's body walked with its hands in its pockets and its long hair tied back, and Gin sat behind his own eyes and watched the streets go by, and for the first time in eleven months he was not fighting.

He was nervous. That was the strange thing. He could feel the voice being nervous too, low down, in the stomach, the way you feel a train coming through the soles of your feet before you hear it.

*He said three cups,* said the voice. *Three. Not two.*

*I know.*

*He has never given me three.*

*I know,* said Gin. *He never gave me three until I was eleven.*

*Then it means something.*

*It means he thinks you're ready to ask a question,* said Gin. *Or he thinks you're ready to hear the answer. With Tanizaki it's hard to tell which. Sometimes it's both.*

The voice was quiet for a long time. They turned into the old shopping street below Ōji. Shutters down. A cat on a step. The smell of the bakery long closed, and fried food from somewhere, and hot tarmac.

*I am going to tell him my name,* said the voice. *Tonight. In front of the boy.*

Gin stopped walking.

He did not mean to. His feet stopped. The body stopped with them. For a second, just a second, they were both standing in the middle of the empty shopping street, and neither of them was sure who had done it.

*You've never told anyone your name,* said Gin. *Not in eleven months. Not me.*

*I know.* The voice did not move the feet again. It let them stand. *I thought about what the boy said. That he wanted to know what to call me. I told him names are debts. That is true.* A pause. *I think I would like to owe him something. I think I would like to owe both of you something. I have never wanted to owe anyone anything.*

Gin could not say anything at all.

*Walk,* said the voice gently. *We will be late. He does not like people to be late.*

[break The cup]

There was a rat on the fence.

The voice had put it there an hour ago. One of its eyes. A small brown rat sitting very still on the top of the old wooden fence of Tanizaki's garden, under the fig tree, facing the house. Not inside. Rats could not go inside. Nothing the voice owned could go inside. But from the fence you could see the garden, and the step, and the open door with the yellow lamplight spilling out of it, and the windowsill.

Gin saw it through the rat's eyes at the same moment the voice did. Grainy. Colourless. Close.

A cup on the windowsill. Set apart from the others. Upside down.

The body stopped walking again. This time it was the voice.

*No,* said Gin.

*He has turned the cup.*

*I can see that. No. Go. Keep walking. We're two streets away. We can be there in three minutes.*

*He has turned the cup,* said the voice again, very quietly, as if it were saying something in a language it had only just learned. *He has never told me anything. In all those months. He gave me two cups and the newspaper and he never once told me what to do.* A long pause. *Now he has told me. He has said, do not come.*

*Something's wrong. Something's wrong, that's why. Go.*

*Yes. Something is wrong. That is why he said it.*

The body sat down.

There was a bench outside a closed tobacconist's, under a dead street lamp, and the voice sat Gin's body down on it, with the knees together and the hands flat on the knees, the way the boy sat at Tanizaki's table. And Gin, behind his own eyes, threw himself at the inside of his own skull like a dog at a locked door, and the voice let him, and did not get up.

*Let me go,* said Gin. *Let me go, let me go, it's Rei in there, let me go...*

*He told us not to come.*

*I don't care what he told us...*

*You do,* said the voice. *You have cared what he told you since you were nine years old. You would not be who you are if you did not.* A pause. *I am not going against him. I do not know how. I have never been told anything by anyone that I wanted to obey before.*

[break What came]

They felt her pass.

Not saw. Felt. Like the cold that comes off an open freezer in a hot shop. The voice went rigid in Gin's chest. Down at the end of the shopping street, under the dead lamps, a crowd of people came out of a side alley, silent, slow, with white flowers held in front of them, and walked up toward Tanizaki's lane. And behind them, last of all, barefoot, in white, a woman.

She did not look toward the bench.

She knew they were there. Gin was sure of it. He felt the voice feel her know. But she did not look. She walked past the end of the street with her river stone swinging, and her smile, and she did not even turn her head.

*She is not here for me,* said the voice. Very slowly. *She is here for him.*

*For Rei...*

*No. For the old man.*

Gin felt it before he understood it. The voice knew things in its body, in his body, before it knew them in words. A cold went down through his chest and his arms and into his hands where they lay on his knees, and his hands began to shake.

*She cannot get me,* said the voice. *He has stopped me coming. So she will take the one who stopped me.* It was not a question. *She will take the door.*

Through the rat's eyes, very small and very far away, Gin saw the old man come out onto the step with the teapot in his hand, and sit down cross-legged, and pour.

[break The step]

They watched it all.

The voice did not look away and did not let Gin look away. Through the rat on the fence, grainy and silent, they watched the white woman walk up the path through the grass that did not bend. They watched her kneel. They watched her put her hand on the old man's face.

They felt it. Two streets away, on a bench, they felt it. Not the despair. The voice was a Duty thing. Despair could not get into it. But they felt the old man hold. The way you feel, standing on a bridge, a long train go underneath, through all the iron of the bridge, into your feet. A great weight, a whole life of it, and one thin old man holding it up from underneath and not putting it down.

They watched him lift the cup.

*Have some tea,* Gin said, at the same moment the old man did. He did not know he was going to say it. It came out of him the way your hand comes up when someone throws you something. *You came all this way. Have some tea.*

On the bench, Gin's hands stopped shaking.

Through the rat, they watched the woman not take the cup. They watched her get up and go. They watched the crowd come over the fence, and a broad man in dark clothes come over after them, and a little hole open in the air somewhere at the back of the house. They watched the boy go down on his knees on the boards. They watched the old man fold the newspaper in half, and in half again.

They watched his hand go still on top of it.

The rat on the fence turned and ran.

The voice had let it go.

[break The bench]

Gin did not know how long they sat there.

Long enough for the field cars to come, and the blue lights to go past the end of the shopping street, and the cicadas to start again, all at once, as if someone had turned them back on. Long enough for a man in a convenience-store uniform to come out of a side door with a bag of rubbish and look at them and go back inside.

He could feel the voice.

He had felt it for eleven months. Every part of it. Its patience. Its politeness. Its terrible accounting, everything owed, everything paid. Its curiosity, these last months, growing slowly like a plant in a dark room. Its fury, once, at a boy on a kerb, at a debt it could not pay. He thought he knew all of it.

He had never felt this.

It was not in the chest. It was lower. It was in the place under the stomach where Gin had felt things when he was nine and had nobody to tell. It was heavy and it was cold and it would not move. It was like swallowing a stone. It was like the whole of the inside of him had been filled with wet sand.

*What is this,* said the voice.

It was not really asking. It was the way a man says *what is this* when he puts his hand in his pocket and finds a letter he did not know was there.

*Nothing was owed,* it said. *He owed me nothing. I owed him nothing. I drank his tea and he gave me the newspaper and I went away. No debt. No claim. There is no debt.* Its voice was very strange. *There is no debt, and something has been taken, and I cannot get it back, and there is no one to pay.*

Gin closed his eyes.

He was crying. He had not known. His body was crying, sitting on a bench outside a closed tobacconist's at midnight, with its hands flat on its knees, and he did not know if it was him or the voice, and then he understood that it did not matter. It was both. They were both crying. Out of the same eyes.

*That's grief,* said Gin.

The voice was quiet for a very long time.

*I have eaten grief,* it said at last. *Many times. In many people. I know how it tastes.* A pause. *I did not know how it weighs.*

*No.*

*How long does it weigh like this?*

Gin thought about being nine, and seeing faces on the stairs every day, and having nobody to tell, and knocking on a door behind Ōji because somebody on the Grid had given him a name. He thought about two cups of tea and a newspaper, and being sent home, and coming back, and being sent home again, for two years. He thought about it for a long time, and then he put it away, because it was his, and the voice did not need all of it.

*It gets lighter,* he said. *It doesn't go. It gets lighter. You get stronger. It's the same weight. You just get used to carrying it.*

*Like a cup.*

*Like a cup,* said Gin. *Yes.*

[break Two cups]

They went home before it got light. The long way round.

In the small yellow kitchen with the frosted window, the voice put the kettle on the gas ring. Gin let it. When the kettle whistled, the hands turned off the gas, and lifted the kettle, and poured the water into the clay teapot, slowly, the right way.

They took down two cups.

Then, after a long moment, a third.

The hands poured the first cup. And the second. And then they filled the third, very carefully, right to the top, the way the old man did.

And the voice turned it upside down on the table, and the tea ran out over the wood in a wide hot puddle and dripped off the edge onto the floor, and neither of them moved to wipe it up.

*I will find her,* said the voice, very quietly. *I will find her, and I will not ask her for anything. And then I will go to the boy, and I will tell him my name. And I will owe him that old man for the rest of whatever I am.*

*He'd hate that,* said Gin. *Tanizaki. He'd hate you owing anybody anything over him.*

*Yes,* said the voice. *I know.* A pause. *I will owe it anyway. He cannot stop me now.*

It was the first joke Gin had ever heard it make.

He did not laugh. Neither did the voice. They sat at the table in the yellow kitchen, in one body, with the tea dripping off the edge of the table onto the floor, and drank the first two cups, one each, slowly, until they were cold.
