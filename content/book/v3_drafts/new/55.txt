#title Kuga

Shimizu sent him to the fourth floor in the first week of August with a form and no explanation.

"Katagiri," she said. "Investigation. She's expecting you. Don't be late, she doesn't wait."

Katagiri Noa's desk was at the far end of the Investigation office, by a window that looked onto the railway, under a strip light that flickered. She was thirty-eight, a senior investigator, an Arbiter, and she looked it. A narrow tired face, no make-up, hair cut short at the back and longer at the front so that it fell across one eye and she pushed it away without noticing. A navy suit that had been good once. Reading glasses on a cord round her neck. On her desk there were four neat stacks of files, a cold can of coffee, and nothing else at all. Rei had spoken to her once, on the phone, in August, the week Gin went. She had promised to look. She had looked. She had found nothing and told him so, in two lines, which he had hated at the time and respected later.

She read his form standing up, and signed it, and handed it back.

"Shimizu thinks you need to see the other side at work," she said. "Not through a hole in an emergency. Not in a fit. On an ordinary day. The busy part. Where things live." She picked up her jacket from the back of her chair. "Gin went through at your age. Somebody took him. I was the one who signed his form. Come on."

[break The Cattery]

The Cattery was at the bottom of a back staircase behind a door with no plate on it, two floors below the street. Rei had heard of it. He had never been down.

It was a small warm room, dim, with a low ceiling and a single lamp with a paper shade. Shelves ran round all four walls at different heights, like a staircase for something that did not use stairs, and on the shelves there were cushions, and on five of the cushions there were cats. It smelled of paper and cat and dried bonito. A small radio on a shelf by the door was playing an afternoon programme about gardening, very low.

An old man in a grey cardigan was sitting on a stool in the middle of the room, combing a fat orange cat on his knee with a flea comb and talking to it.

"Tsuji-san," said Katagiri. "Post room. He keeps them."

"Nobody else will," said Mr. Tsuji, without looking up. He had a soft round face and very large ears and a pencil behind one of them. "They forget to feed them. They forget to talk to them. Investigation is the worst. You forget everything that isn't a file." He looked at Katagiri over his glasses. "Hello, Katagiri-san."

"Hello, Tsuji-san."

Each cushion had a small brass tag with a name on it. Sera, a tortoiseshell, asleep. Itou, a grey, washing. Mochida, white, with odd ears, watching them with great interest. Hara, the orange one on Mr. Tsuji's knee, upside down with his legs in the air. And on the highest shelf, alone, a big dark cat with a heavy head and still yellow eyes, looking down at Rei and nobody else.

"They're named after people," said Mr. Tsuji. "Five people from this building who went through and didn't come back." He combed Hara's stomach. "Kuga was the first, in 1978. He was the cat's handler before me. The cat took his name when he didn't come back, and he's taken everybody through since."

"You ask him," said Katagiri to Rei. "Not me. Not Tsuji-san. He's the door. It's his choice."

Rei stood in front of the high shelf. He did not know how you asked a cat for anything.

He bowed. Low. As he would bow to Tanizaki.

"Kuga-san," he said. "I'd like to go through. If you'll let me. I need to see how it works over there. On an ordinary day." He hesitated. "I'll be careful. I'll come back when you say."

Kuga looked at him for a long time. Then he stood up on the shelf and stretched, very slowly, every part of him, from the front paws to the end of his heavy tail. He jumped down, one soft thump, onto the low shelf, and then onto the floor, and walked across to Rei, and sat down in front of his feet, and looked up.

"Oh," said Mr. Tsuji. "Well. That was quick."

"Kneel down," said Katagiri. "Put your hand on his back. Open your door. All the way. Lift the latch." She sat down on a folding chair by the wall with her reading glasses in her hand. "He'll take you. He'll bring you back. You don't let go of him. Not for anything. Whatever you see. If you let go of Kuga on the other side, nobody can come and get you."

"Has anyone ever let go?"

Katagiri looked at the five brass tags.

"Don't let go," she said.

Rei knelt down on the floor of the Cattery. He put his left hand flat on Kuga's back, on the thick dark warm fur. He felt the cat breathing under his palm, slow and steady. He put his right hand on his wrist, on the latch.

He lifted it.

[break Through]

Kuga stood up under his hand and walked forward.

Not anywhere. There was nowhere to walk. There was the Cattery wall, a metre away, with an old filing cabinet against it. Kuga walked toward it, and Rei went with him, on his knees, his hand on the cat's back, and the wall was not there.

It was not like the white, with no floor or ceiling. It was not like stepping from one place to another, as the Kuroda did. It was like walking through a curtain into the same room, and the room being full.

They were in the Cattery. The same shelves. The same cushions. The same cats. Mr. Tsuji on his stool, grey and still, like a man in an old photograph. But everything was grey and soft at the edges, like a photograph left in the sun, and the air was cold and thick, like the air at the bottom of a lake, and the room was full of other things.

Something like a thin old man sat on the cushion where Hara slept, stroking the orange cat that was not there, over and over, very gently. On the shelf where Sera slept, a young woman in an old-fashioned Division uniform, the kind with a high collar, sat with her knees up, reading a file that had no words on it. By the door, something small and round and grey, the size of a cat, with too many eyes, was eating the shadow of Mr. Tsuji's stool. Not eating it. Tasting it. Delicately. Like someone trying a new kind of tea.

Rei went very still.

*Don't stop,* said something. Not a voice. A feeling, coming up through his hand from the warm back of the cat. Patient. Bored. Very old. *They don't see you. You're with me. Keep walking.*

Kuga walked out of the Cattery, through the door that was not open, and Rei went with him.

[break The ordinary other side]

He never forgot that walk. He tried to write it down afterward in the back of Gin's book, and could not, and in the end wrote only *Tuesday*, and underlined it, because that was what it was.

It was a Tuesday on the other side.

The Division building was full of the dead. Not frightening. Ordinary. A man in a suit from the seventies walking down the fourth-floor corridor with a cup of coffee that steamed grey. Two women by the lifts, talking with their heads close together, and when they laughed it came out as cold. A child sitting on the stairs to the third floor, swinging her legs, watching the living go up and down through her and now and then reaching out to touch one of them on the sleeve, like a child touching a fish in a pool.

And other things. Things that had never been people. A long pale shape lying along the top of the skirting board in Assessment like a draught excluder, breathing slowly, feeding on the nervousness of trainees waiting outside the glass rooms. Something like a nest of black threads in the ceiling above Records that pulsed very slightly every time someone in the room below lost a piece of paper. A thing in the Sigil Room, by Uruma's table, which looked like a small fire that had decided to be a cat, sitting very close to the drying seals as if they were warm, and watching Uruma's long black-nailed hands with total devotion.

*Pets,* said Kuga, bored. *He feeds it. He thinks nobody knows.*

Out of the building. Out into the street by Ōji station, grey and soft and full.

The dead on the platform, waiting for trains that had stopped running decades ago. A whole row of them, patient, in the clothes of their decades, looking down the line. A salaryman from the bubble years. A girl in a sailor school uniform from the war. An old man in a farmer's straw hat. When a real train came in, silver and loud and full of the living, they looked at it politely and did not get on.

Something enormous and slow under the road, moving like a whale under ice, so big Rei could not see the ends of it, going north toward the river. *Old,* said Kuga. *Older than the city. It sleeps mostly. Don't wake it.*

On the corner, something from the far side that had never been a person.

Rei felt it before he saw it. A small grey thing, no bigger than a coat, wrapped round a man standing outside a pachinko parlour with his last thousand-yen note in his hand. Not driving him. Not yet. Just leaning on him. Whispering. *One more. One more. You'll win it back. One more.* The man's eyes were dilated. He went in. The demon went in with him, delighted.

*Most of it is that,* said Kuga. *Not the big things. That. Small things leaning on people. All day. Every day. Your Division catches one in a hundred.*

"Can't I..."

*No. Not from here. Not with me. You hold on.* The cat's back was warm and steady under his hand. *Look. Learn. That's all today is.*

And kindness. That was what Rei did not expect. A dead grandmother sitting on a bench outside a primary school, watching the children go in at the gate, and when a small boy tripped and fell, reaching out a grey hand and catching his elbow, so lightly that the boy only felt a cold breeze and stood up again and did not cry. A spirit like a soft lantern hanging above the door of an old sentō, the light of it falling warm on the people going in with their towels. Something with wings like dry leaves sitting on the roof of a hospital, very still, keeping something off.

*They're not all bad,* said Kuga. *They're not all good. They're like you. They want things. They do what they want. Mostly small. Mostly nothing.* A pause. *Your brother cried the first time. On the bench. With the grandmother.*

Rei realised his own face was wet.

"Can we go to Jūjō?" he said. "To my flat."

The cat stopped walking.

*Why?*

"I want to see it from this side."

Kuga looked at him with his still yellow eyes for a long moment. Then he turned south.

[break The flat]

The building where he lived was the same old five-storey block with the open walkways and the rusty railings and the potted plants outside the doors. On this side it was grey and soft like everything else.

Except his door.

His door burned. Not with fire. With a pale steady light, like a lamp seen through paper, and it came from the mark above the lintel, the three strokes and a hook and a dot that he had walked under ten thousand times without seeing. From this side it was not faint at all. It stood out on the concrete as clearly as a house number. And the light ran from it along the wall, round the frame of the kitchen window, in a long bright line, layer over layer, and in through the walls, faintly, everywhere, so that the whole flat sat inside it like an egg in a hand.

Things stood outside.

Not many. Three or four. On the walkway, at a polite distance from the door. Something tall and thin with a long neck that leaned toward the kitchen window and leaned back again, like a heron wondering whether to fish. Something low and dark that sat on the railing opposite with its back to him. An old woman in a quilted jacket, a real dead woman, sitting on an upturned crate by the door of the flat next door, knitting with grey wool, who looked up at Rei as he came along the walkway on his knees with his hand on a cat, and nodded as if she saw him every day.

None of them touched the light. They looked at it. That was all.

*It has been like this a long time,* said Kuga. *Since you were small. Your brother kept a good house. They come to look. They don't go in. They can't, and after a while they don't want to.*

The tall thin thing by the window turned its long neck and looked at Rei. It had no face. It looked anyway.

*It used to stand there every night,* said Kuga. *When you were a boy. Looking in at you at the kitchen table. It wanted you very much. It has been standing there for twelve years, wanting.* The cat's back was warm and bored under Rei's hand. *It got tired. It comes on Tuesdays now. For the habit.*

Rei looked at the thing he had been afraid of for half his life. It was thinner than he remembered. Greyer. It leaned toward the window, and leaned back, and leaned toward it again.

He did not feel afraid of it. He felt, oddly, a little sorry for it.

In the top corner of the kitchen window frame, beside the newest of Gin's marks, there was a small new one, crooked, much too heavy in the strokes. It shone as brightly as the rest.

*That one is yours,* said Kuga. *It's ugly.* A pause. *It holds.*

*Time,* said the cat.

[break Back]

He came back on his knees on the floor of the Cattery with his hand on Kuga's back and his nose bleeding onto the cat's fur. Mr. Tsuji had a towel round his shoulders and was holding him up. Katagiri was standing over them with her watch in her hand.

"Three hours and forty minutes," she said. "Kuga brought you back." She put the watch away and handed him a box of tissues. "Gin was gone two hours the first time. He came back and said he'd seen a Tuesday. I wrote it in the log. I thought he was being clever."

"It was a Tuesday," said Rei.

Katagiri looked at him for a moment. Then she sat down on the folding chair and put her reading glasses on and took a small notebook out of her jacket.

"Tell me what you saw," she said. "In order. Leave nothing out. Especially the one by the vending machine at Ōji, if it's still there. We've been trying to find out what it is for six years." She uncapped her pen. "Take your time."

He told her. It took an hour. She wrote down every word, in small neat handwriting, and asked short exact questions, and did not once look surprised. When he told her about the walkway in Jūjō, and the tall thing at the window, she stopped writing and looked at him over her glasses for a long moment.

"Your brother never told anyone about that," she said.

"He didn't tell me either."

"No." She wrote it down. "He wouldn't."

Kuga had climbed back up to his high shelf, slowly, one jump and then another, and lay down on his cushion and closed his eyes. Mr. Tsuji gave Rei a cup of very sweet tea from a flask and a rice ball wrapped in cling film and told him to eat it all, and Rei ate it all, sitting on the floor of the Cattery with his back against the shelves, while Hara the orange cat climbed into his lap and fell asleep upside down.

He went home that night and stood on the walkway outside his door for a while before he went in. Next door's light was off. There was an old crate by their door that he had never noticed, turned upside down, and a ball of grey wool on top of it.

He said good evening to it. Then he went in and made dinner.
