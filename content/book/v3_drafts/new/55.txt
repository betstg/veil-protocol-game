#title Kuga

Katagiri Noa kept a bowl on her desk.

Rei had never noticed it before the radio. Now he could not stop noticing it. It sat on the corner of her desk on the fourth floor, between a stack of transport forms and a pot of pens, a small rough pottery bowl, brown and uneven, the kind a beginner makes in a weekend class. It had been broken once. Badly. Into five or six pieces. And someone had put it back together with lacquer and gold, so that the cracks ran across it in bright shining lines like rivers on a map.

"Kintsugi," said Katagiri, when she saw him looking. She did not seem to mind. She picked it up and turned it in her hands. "You mend the break with gold so everyone can see where it was. The idea is that the bowl is more beautiful for having been broken." She put it down again, very carefully, in exactly the same place. "I made it. Badly. I dropped it. Somebody told me not to throw it away." She smiled the small smile that went all the way up. "So I didn't."

She did not say who. Rei did not ask. He found he already knew, and did not want to.

"You want to go through Kuga," said Katagiri.

He had not said so. He had only come up to the fourth floor with a form for the Cattery, the way Shimizu had told him to, and stood by her desk.

"Shimizu called me," said Katagiri. "She says you need to see the other side at work. Not the Font. Not the white. The ordinary other side. The busy part. Where things live." She stood up and picked up her mug. "She says you've only ever seen it in emergencies. Through holes. And you need to see it on a Tuesday." She looked at him over the rim of the mug. "Gin did it at your age. Through Kuga. I took him."

[break The Cattery]

The Cattery was warm and dim and smelled the same as before, of paper and cat and dried bonito. The tortoiseshell, Sera, was asleep. The grey, Itou, was washing. The white one with odd ears, Mochida, watched them come in with interest. The fat orange one, Hara, was upside down on his cushion with his legs in the air and did not wake up at all.

On the highest shelf, by himself, Kuga looked down at Rei with his heavy head and his still yellow eyes.

"You ask him," said Katagiri. She sat down on her low stool under the shelf with her mug in both hands, the way she always sat. "Not me. I'm the handler. He's the door. It's his choice. It's always his choice."

Rei stood in front of the shelf. He did not know how you asked a cat for anything. He thought about the first time, in December, when he had bowed, and Kuga had closed his eyes.

He bowed again. Low. The way he would bow to Tanizaki.

"Kuga-san," he said. "I'd like to go through. If you'll let me. I need to see how it works over there. On an ordinary day." He hesitated. "I'll be careful. I'll come back when you say."

Kuga looked at him for a long time. Then he stood up on the shelf, stretched, very slowly, every part of him, from the front paws to the end of his heavy tail. He jumped down, one soft thump, onto the low shelf, and then onto the floor, and walked across to Rei, and sat down in front of his feet, and looked up.

"Kneel down," said Katagiri quietly. "Put your hand on his back. Open your door. Not a finger's width. All the way. Lift the latch." She paused. "He'll take you. He'll bring you back. You don't let go of him. Not for anything. Whatever you see. If you let go of Kuga on the other side, I can't come and get you."

"Has anyone ever let go?"

Katagiri looked at the five cushions with their five small brass tags.

"Five people," she said. "That's who the cats are named for." She drank her tea. "Don't let go."

Rei knelt down on the floor of the Cattery. He put his left hand flat on Kuga's back, on the thick dark warm fur. He felt the cat breathing under his palm, slow and steady. He put his right hand on his wrist, on the latch.

He lifted it.

[break Through]

Kuga stood up under his hand and walked forward.

Not anywhere. There was nowhere to walk. There was the Cattery wall, a metre away, with an old filing cabinet against it. Kuga walked toward it, and Rei went with him, on his knees, his hand on the cat's back, and the wall was not there.

It was not like the white. It was not like the Font, with no floor or ceiling. It was not like Itsuki's doors, from one place to another. It was like walking through a curtain into the same room, and the room being full.

They were in the Cattery. The same shelves. The same cushions. The same cats. But everything was grey and soft at the edges, like a photograph left in the sun, and the air was cold and thick, like the air at the bottom of a lake, and the room was full of other things.

Something like a thin old man sat on the cushion where Hara slept, stroking the orange cat that was not there, over and over, very gently. On the shelf where Sera slept, a young woman in an old-fashioned Division uniform, the kind with a high collar, sat with her knees up, reading a file that had no words on it. By the door, something small and round and grey, the size of a cat, with too many eyes, was eating the shadow of the stool where Katagiri sat. Not eating it. Tasting it. Delicately. Like someone trying a new kind of tea.

Rei went very still.

*Don't stop,* said something. Not a voice. A feeling, coming up through his hand from the warm back of the cat. Patient. Bored. Very old. *They don't see you. You're with me. Keep walking.*

Kuga walked out of the Cattery, through the door that was not open, and Rei went with him.

[break The ordinary other side]

He never forgot that walk. He tried to write it down afterward in the back of Gin's book, and could not, and in the end wrote only *Tuesday*, and underlined it, because that was what it was.

It was a Tuesday on the other side.

The Division building was full of the dead. Not frightening. Ordinary. A man in a suit from the nineteen-seventies walking down the fourth-floor corridor with a cup of coffee that steamed grey. Two women by the lifts, talking with their heads close together, and when they laughed it came out as cold. A child sitting on the stairs to the third floor, swinging her legs, watching the living go up and down through her and occasionally reaching out to touch one of them on the sleeve, the way you touch a fish in a pool.

And other things. Things that had never been people. A long pale shape lying along the top of the skirting board in Assessment like a draught excluder, breathing slowly, feeding on the nervousness of trainees waiting outside the glass rooms. Something like a nest of black threads in the ceiling above Records that pulsed very slightly every time someone in the room below lost a piece of paper. A thing in the Sigil Room, by Uruma's table, which looked like a small fire that had decided to be a cat, sitting very close to the drying seals as if they were warm, and watching Uruma's long black-nailed hands with total devotion.

*Pets,* said Kuga, bored. *He feeds it. He thinks nobody knows.*

Out of the building. Out into the street by Ōji station, grey and soft and full.

The dead on the platform, waiting for trains that had stopped running decades ago. A whole row of them, patient, in the clothes of their decades, looking down the line. A salaryman from the bubble years. A girl in a sailor school uniform from the war. An old man in a farmer's straw hat. When a real train came in, silver and loud and full of the living, they looked at it politely and did not get on.

Something enormous and slow under the road, moving like a whale under ice, so big Rei could not see the ends of it, going north toward the river. *Old,* said Kuga. *Older than the city. It sleeps mostly. Don't wake it.*

On the corner, a demon.

Rei knew it at once. He felt it before he saw it. A demon of Hunger, small, Class D or C, wrapped round a man standing outside a pachinko parlour with his last thousand-yen note in his hand. Not driving him. Not yet. Just leaning on him. Whispering. *One more. One more. You'll win it back. One more.* The man's eyes were dilated. He went in. The demon went in with him, delighted.

*Most of it is that,* said Kuga. *Not the big things. That. Small things leaning on people. All day. Every day. Your Division catches one in a hundred.*

"Can't I..."

*No. Not from here. Not with me. You hold on.* The cat's back was warm and steady under his hand. *Look. Learn. That's all today is.*

And kindness. That was what Rei did not expect. A dead grandmother sitting on a bench outside a primary school, watching the children go in at the gate, and when a small boy tripped and fell, reaching out a grey hand and catching his elbow, so lightly that the boy only felt a cold breeze and stood up again and did not cry. A spirit like a soft lantern hanging above the door of an old sentō, the light of it falling warm on the people going in with their towels. Something with wings like dry leaves sitting on the roof of a hospital, very still, keeping something off.

*They're not all bad,* said Kuga. *They're not all good. They're like you. They want things. They do what they want. Mostly small. Mostly nothing.* A pause. *Your brother cried the first time. On the bench. With the grandmother.*

Rei realised his own face was wet.

"Can we go to Akabane?" he said. "The hospital. The maternity ward."

The cat stopped walking.

*Why?*

"There's someone I have to tell something."

Kuga looked at him with his still yellow eyes for a long moment. Then he turned north.

[break The ward]

The maternity ward on the fifth floor of the big hospital in Akabane was very quiet at that hour. The living nurses moved between the rooms in soft shoes. The babies slept.

On this side, it was full of light. Rei had not expected that. Every small new life in every cot was a little light, bright and warm and very new, like a candle just lit. The whole ward glowed. And standing in the corridors and the doorways, on this side, very quietly, there were the dead. Grandmothers. Grandfathers. A young father in a hospital gown who had died, Rei understood, in the cardiac ward two floors down on the night his daughter was born, and had walked up the stairs to see her, and had been standing beside her cot for eleven years. They watched the little lights. They did not touch them. They only watched.

And in the cot at the end of the ward, there was no light at all.

There was a baby. Small. Asleep. Breathing. But the light in him was not a new candle. It was an old one. A grey, thin, tired flame, the colour of the cigarette smoke in old cafés, very low. And sitting inside the baby, curled up small in a body much too small for him, with his knees to his chin, was an old man in a grey hat.

He was crying. Without a sound. The way old men cry.

Rei knelt by the cot, on this side, with one hand on Kuga's back.

"Okamura-san," he said.

The old man looked up. Out of the baby's eyes. Tired and frightened and very ashamed.

"*Who are you,*" he said. His voice was a whisper, like the inside of a shell. "*How do you know my name. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't mean to. It was cold. I came out of the old men's ward and it was so cold and the little one was empty, nobody had come yet, and it was warm, and I only meant to sit for a minute...*"

"I know," said Rei. "I know. It's okay."

"*I can't get out. I've tried. I don't know how. And the mother. She looks at me. She knows. She cries.*" The old man pulled his grey hat down over his eyes. "*I've ruined it. I've ruined everything. Again. I always ruin everything. I went out for cigarettes in nineteen sixty-two and I never went home. I was a coward. I left her. I left her in the shop.*"

Rei breathed in. Four. Held it. Out for six.

"She waited," he said.

The old man went still.

"Outside the shop," said Rei. "Okamura Watches and Clocks. In Jūjō. Every day. In her best coat. She waited for you for fifty years. After she died, she kept waiting. On the pavement. Under the awning. Looking down the street. She wasn't angry. She was worried. She thought you'd got lost."

The grey flame in the baby flickered.

"I sent her on," said Rei. "In August. I didn't know about you. I gave her salt and a bun from the bakery across the street and told her she could stop waiting." He swallowed. "She went. She went on. She's not there any more. That's why you couldn't find her in March. It's my fault. I'm sorry."

The old man looked at him for a long time out of the baby's face.

"*She waited,*" he said. "*Fifty years.*"

"Yes."

"*She thought I was lost.*"

"Yes."

The old man took off his grey hat. He held it in his small hands. He looked at it.

"*I was,*" he said.

He looked up, past Rei, past the end of the ward, past the window, out into the grey soft Tuesday of the other side. And something changed in his face. As if, very far away, at the end of a long street, someone had come out of a shop under an awning and was looking down the road toward him, shading her eyes.

"*She's not angry,*" he said wonderingly.

"No."

The old man stood up. Inside the baby, in a body too small for him, he stood up, and put his hat back on his head, and straightened his coat.

"*I'm late,*" he said. "*I'm very late.*"

And he went.

He went out of the baby the way Kōhei had gone out of Rei. All at once, like a breath. Grey and tired and old, and at the very last second, light. Out through the window, out over the roofs of Akabane, toward the south, toward Jūjō, toward a closed watch shop. Gone.

The cot was dark. Empty. For one second, two, three, there was nothing in it but a small sleeping body with no light at all.

Then, out of nowhere, from no direction Rei could see, something tiny and bright and new came down into it like a spark landing in dry grass, and caught.

The baby woke up.

He opened his mouth and screamed. A baby's scream. Furious, outraged, enormous, with his whole small red body. And down the corridor, on the living side, a door opened, and a young woman in a hospital gown came running, barefoot, with her hair everywhere, and snatched him up, and held him against her, and he went on screaming, and she started to laugh, and cry, and laugh, saying *oh, oh, there you are, there you are, oh, you're so loud, there you are.*

On this side, the young father in the hospital gown who had been standing by his daughter's cot for eleven years turned his head and looked at Rei, and nodded, very slightly, the way one stranger nods to another on a train.

*Time,* said Kuga.

[break Back]

He came back on his knees on the floor of the Cattery with his hand on Kuga's back and his nose bleeding onto the cat's fur, and Katagiri's hands on his shoulders, holding him up.

"Four hours," she said. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "You were gone four hours. Kuga brought you back." She wiped his face with a tissue, very gently, the way you wipe a child's face. "Gin was gone two hours the first time. He came back crying."

"I'm crying," said Rei.

"I can see." She sat back on her stool and picked up her mug, which had gone cold, and held it in both hands anyway. "What did you see?"

"A Tuesday," said Rei.

Katagiri laughed. It was the first time he had heard her laugh. It was low and surprised and it made her look younger.

"That's what he said," she said. "Word for word. *A Tuesday.*" She looked at the mug. "He was sitting exactly there. Exactly like that. With Kuga. I held his shoulders." She was quiet for a moment. "I've never told anybody that."

Kuga climbed back up to his high shelf, slowly, one jump and then another, and lay down on his cushion and closed his eyes, as if nothing had happened at all.

"Katagiri-san," said Rei.

"Mm."

"If you could get him back. Gin. If someone told you there was a way. Any way." He did not know why he asked it. It came out of him before he could stop it. "Would you take it?"

Katagiri did not answer for a long time. She looked at the mug. She looked up at the cat on the shelf.

"Go home, Rei," she said gently. "You've been on the other side for four hours. You're asking strange questions." She smiled the small smile. "Eat something hot."

He went home. He ate something hot. He slept for eleven hours without dreaming.

He did not notice, until much later, that she had not said no.
