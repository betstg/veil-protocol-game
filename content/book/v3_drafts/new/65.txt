#title Sinking

They buried Tanizaki on the Tuesday, from a small temple behind Ōji that Rei had walked past a thousand times without knowing it was there.

There was no family. Nobody had ever known if there was family. The monk who did the sutras was very old and very short and had known Tanizaki for fifty years, and at the end, when everyone had offered incense, he stood at the front of the little hall with his hands in his sleeves and said, *He came here every New Year's morning and drank one cup of tea with me and left. He never said why. I never asked. That is the whole of what I know about him, and it is more than most people know about anybody.*

The hall was full.

Rei had not expected that. He stood at the back by the door in his one suit and watched them come in. Hara Setsu from the bath house, square and solid in black, with her knitting in her bag, not knitting. Mori in a black jacket that did not fit him across the shoulders, his face like a closed furnace. Hoshino with his apron off for once, which made him look strange and naked. Matsuda Keiko from Café Asahi. Switch, from the Gathering, in black, their hair pulled tight, their signet ring turned inward. Chen Lian with her glasses steamed up. Majima at the very back, against the wall, with the old dog lying at his feet and nobody telling him it could not be there.

People Rei had never seen. Dozens of them. Old men in cardigans. A woman with a pram. A young man with tattoos up both arms who cried the whole way through the sutras with his face in his hands. Grid people, all of them. People who had knocked on a door behind Ōji once, years ago, in a hurry, and been given two cups of tea and sent home.

Katagiri came. She stood near the door, in grey, with her hands folded in front of her, and she did not offer incense. When she saw Rei, her face did something he did not understand.

He went to her afterwards, outside, under the cicadas, and bowed.

"Thank you," he said. "For Kuga. He came. He sat on the wall all night. I don't know how he got out. I know you must have..." His voice went. He bowed again. "Thank you."

Katagiri looked at him.

She did not say anything at all. She bowed back, very low, lower than he had, and stayed bowed for a long time. When she straightened up, she turned and walked away down the hill toward the station without a word, and he watched her go and thought she was crying and did not know why, and was too tired to wonder.

[break What did not close]

It started on the Wednesday.

He went to work. He sat at his desk on the third floor and filled in a form about Tanizaki's house, which had been sealed by Public Confidence and then unsealed by Okada and then sealed again by somebody from upstairs whose name was not on the paperwork. He put the form in the tray. He went down to the canteen and bought a curry and sat in front of it until it went cold and then put it in the bin.

On the way home, at Ōji station, on the platform, he saw the dead.

He always saw the dead. He had seen them since he was small. But he had learned, at the kitchen table at eight years old with Gin's hand on his back, to close the door in his chest so they stayed outside, at the edge of things, faint, like people on the other side of a frosted window. And he had kept it closed, mostly, for eleven years.

On Wednesday, on the platform at Ōji, it did not close.

He reached for it, the way you reach for a light switch in your own house in the dark without thinking, and it was not there. The latch on his wrist was there. Cold iron. He pressed it. He pushed. It did not catch. It was like pushing a door with a broken hinge. It swung, and swung back, and did not shut.

And they were everywhere.

All down the platform, standing among the living. A woman in a summer kimono with a burnt face. A boy in a school cap from a long time ago. A salaryman from the bubble years, very neat, with a hole in the side of his head. They were not doing anything. They were waiting for trains, the way the living were waiting for trains. But they were *there*. Clear and solid and close, closer than the living, so that the living people on the platform looked like the faint ones, like the ghosts.

And one of them, at the far end of the platform, by the yellow line, was a thin old man in a faded blue jinbei, with a newspaper under his arm.

Rei got on the train with his eyes shut and did not open them until Higashi-Jūjō, and missed his stop, and walked back.

[break Thursday Friday Saturday]

He stopped going to work on the Thursday.

He did not decide to. He woke up on the sofa with his foot off the end, and it was eleven o'clock, and he had not heard his alarm, and he lay there and looked at the ceiling and could not think of a single reason to get up. Not one. He tried. He thought of Gin. He thought of Yua counting her heartbeat in a white room. He thought of Watanabe's grey arms. He thought of the old man at the bottom of the light, holding something too heavy, alone. The thoughts came and sat on his chest and did not lift him. They only made him heavier.

Gorō bit his ear at noon. He did not get up.

Gorō bit his ear at three. Harder. He felt it bleed. He did not get up.

His phone buzzed. Every time it buzzed, before he could stop it, a voice in his head said *am I late, am I late*, a cheerful Kansai voice that was not his, the price of a dead delivery rider's last ride. He had learned to live with it. He could not live with it now. Every buzz was like a fingernail on a bruise. He turned the phone face down and then put a cushion on top of it and then put it in the rice cooker with the envelope of money and shut the lid.

*Eat,* said the lid.

He did not eat.

At night, in the kitchen, the kettle sang on its own. He had not put it on. He went in and looked at it. The gas was off. The kettle was cold. It was singing anyway, very softly, a long thin note, and under the note, very faint, a voice he knew.

*The tea. It has gone cold.*

He sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge and his hands over his ears and Gorō screaming at him from the top of Gin's chair.

On Friday he did not open the curtains.

On Saturday he did not go to Tanizaki's house, for the first Saturday since September, because there was no Tanizaki's house. There was a sealed door with a strip of white paper across it that said *Closed*, and a wild garden that nobody would ever cut, and a fig tree, and a step.

He sat on the sofa all day and looked at the wall. At the hook by the door. *Come home.* At the window frame. *Not here. Not this one. Not him.* The words did not mean anything. They were marks on a wall. Grey pencil. Some of them were so old they had nearly gone. In a month, somebody else's name would be on the post box, and somebody else would paint over them, and they would be gone completely, and nobody would ever know they had been there.

The thirty-first of July came and went. He did not go to the office above the dry cleaner's. He did not ring the kind woman in the cardigan. The envelope sat in the rice cooker under the lid that said *Eat*, with his phone, buzzing, and buzzing, and buzzing.

[break The platform]

That night, the last night of July, he went back to Ōji station.

He did not know why. He got up off the sofa at eleven at night and put on his shoes and went out without locking the door. Gorō came after him, flying from lamp post to lamp post down the hill, screaming the whole way. Rei did not look at him.

The platform was nearly empty. Last trains. A drunk man asleep on a bench. A girl in a convenience-store uniform eating a rice ball. And the dead, everywhere, thick as the living at rush hour, standing in silent rows along the yellow line, looking down the track.

Rei went and stood among them.

He stood on the yellow line, at the place where a man in a grey suit with a konbini umbrella and a can of hot coffee had once said *there you are* in somebody else's voice and stepped backwards in front of a train. A year ago. Almost exactly. The first day of everything.

It was very quiet inside him. That was the thing he would remember afterwards. Not loud. Not frightening. Very quiet and very grey and very calm, like the inside of a long white room with cushions on the floor. All the weight had gone. He was not carrying anything any more. He had put it all down. It was such a relief to put it down.

He could feel Watanabe. Far away, in a room at the back of a library in Ochanomizu, with the grey up to his shoulders now. He could feel the thread running out of Watanabe across the city, and what was spilling out round it, slow and grey and cold like water over the lip of a dam. Despair, without its holder holding it. Seeping into the city. Into the platforms. Into the people standing at the edge of them at midnight.

*People at the edge of platforms,* Watanabe had said, in the spring, in the library. *If I fight, it spills.*

He was not fighting. He was too tired to fight. It was spilling.

Rei understood that. Quite clearly. He understood it the way you understand something in a dream, with no feeling about it at all. *This isn't mine. This is his. This is hers. It's coming in through the door that won't close.*

He understood it, and he did not step back.

A light at the end of the tunnel. The rails beginning to sing.

[break We're not doing this here]

"Get up," said a voice beside him.

It was very flat. Very tired. A woman's voice, not loud.

"Get up. We're not doing this here."

Rei turned his head.

Okada was standing on the yellow line beside him. In her grey suit with the shiny elbows. With her three notebooks held together with a rubber band in one hand. She was not looking at him. She was looking down the tunnel, at the light, the way she had looked at the platform a year ago, her eyes moving, counting, watching.

"I'm not on the ground," said Rei. His voice came from very far away. "You said get up. I'm not on the ground."

"You're on the ground," said Okada. "You've been on the ground for a week. I've been watching you." She did not look at him. "Your brother told me to watch for you. I've been watching for five years now. I'm not stopping tonight. I'm very stubborn and I'm very tired. I am not going to stop watching you on this platform. So you're going to have to step back, Uedera Rei, because I am not going to stand here and watch it."

The light was getting bigger. The rails were singing.

"I can't close it," said Rei. "The door. I can't close it. It's all coming in."

Okada did not move her eyes from the tunnel. "I know."

Rei's hands hung at his sides like somebody else's. "It's not mine."

"I know. It's his. Watanabe's. Kato told me." Okada held out her hand. Not toward him. Just out, to the side, palm up, the way you hold out your hand to a child at a crossing without looking down. "So it's not yours to carry. Put it down somewhere else. Not here. Step back."

The train came out of the tunnel.

Rei stepped back.

He did not know he was going to do it. His feet did it. His feet did it, and his hand went out, and found her hand, and held on, and the train came into the station with a long scream of brakes and a hot wind full of dust and stopped, and the doors opened, and the drunk man woke up and got on, and the girl in the convenience-store uniform got on, and the doors closed, and the train went away.

Okada did not let go of his hand.

Behind them, at the top of the stairs, there was the sound of people running. Three people. Itsuki, in paint-stained shorts and a T-shirt inside out, with no shoes on. Kato, with his king in his fist and his face wet. And behind them, slower, in a sweater in August, with a flask in his hand, Tsukishiro Haruki.

They stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

Nobody said anything.

Gorō came down from the roof of the station and landed on the platform, on the yellow line, between Rei's feet. He did not scream. He did not bite. He just stood there, black and white, very small, on the line, and looked up.

[break The bar]

They took him to Nezu. Haruki opened the bar at one in the morning and turned on one lamp over the counter and nothing else, and the piano sat in the dark like a big black animal asleep.

They put Rei on a stool at the counter. Itsuki sat on one side of him. Kato on the other. Okada sat at the end, by the wall, with her back very straight and her notebooks on the counter in front of her. Gorō sat on the till.

Haruki made coffee.

He did it slowly. The way he did everything at the counter. Grinding the beans by hand, in an old wooden grinder, round and round. Heating the water. Pouring it in a thin slow spiral over the grounds so that they rose and bloomed and smelled like the first morning of a holiday. Rei watched his hands. He did not think about anything. He watched the water go round.

Haruki put a cup in front of him. White. Thick. Hot.

Haruki pushed the cup an inch closer with one fingertip. "Drink that. Then you can fall apart. Not before."

Rei drank it.

And then he fell apart.

He did not know how long it went on. He cried with his face on the counter and Itsuki's hand on the back of his neck, and Kato's shoulder against his, and Kato crying too, quietly, saying *I can hear it, I can hear all of it, it's all right, it's all right, I've got it*. He said things. He did not know what. He said it was his fault. He said he had told people, he had told too many people, he had made it happen. He said he should have gone alone. He said Tanizaki would still be alive if he had never knocked on that door. He said he could not close the door and it was all coming in and he did not want to do this any more, he did not want to carry it, any of it, he was so tired, he was so, so tired.

Nobody told him he was wrong. Nobody told him it would be all right. They just stayed. Itsuki's hand on his neck. Kato's shoulder. Okada at the end of the counter, not moving, watching. Haruki behind the counter, drying a cup that was already dry.

When it was over, it was nearly four. The sky outside the little window was going grey.

Haruki put another coffee in front of him.

"I'm going to say something," said Haruki. "And you're going to let me, because I made the coffee." He leaned on the counter on his forearms. "Your brother kept every part of his life in a separate box. With a separate lock. You know that. He thought it kept us safe. He thought if he carried everything himself, nobody else would get hurt." He looked at Rei. "And then something came and took him off a doorstep, and none of us knew where he'd been going, or why, or who to call. Because he'd never let anybody carry anything."

Rei looked down at the coffee.

"You told people," said Haruki. "You told Itsuki. You told Kato. You told the man with the dog and the woman at the Archive. You let them carry it with you." He reached across the counter and put one finger on the back of Rei's hand. Just one. "That's not why the old man died. That's why you're still here."

[break The flat]

At seven, Okada stood up.

She put the rubber band round her notebooks. She put on her jacket. She looked at Rei.

She tucked the notebooks under her arm. "Your lease."

Rei stared at her. "How do you know about..."

"I'm an investigator." She did not smile. She never smiled. "And you left the door unlocked, and the letter was on the kitchen table, and your crow let me in." She took a folded piece of paper out of the inside pocket of her jacket and put it on the counter in front of him. "I went to Kita Residential on Friday. I spoke to a woman in a cardigan. She's held the file. She held it past the thirty-first, which she's not supposed to do, because she remembers your brother's cakes." She tapped the paper. "That's a guarantor form. It has my name on it. I have a steady job and a pension and nothing whatsoever to spend my money on except notebooks. The deposit and the rest is due on the seventh."

Rei could not speak.

"There's a hundred and fifty in your rice cooker," said Itsuki, beside him, quietly. "Kato saw it. Don't ask." He took a folded envelope out of his back pocket. It was creased and had paint on it. "I sold the big one. The river. The one at the Kōenji show. Some guy from an agency bought it for his office." He put it on the counter. "Ninety. Don't argue. I'll paint another river. I've got loads of rivers."

Kato put his king on the counter. Then, beside it, a bank envelope.

"Sixty," he said. His voice cracked. "It wasn't for anything. It was just sitting there. Being sixty."

Haruki did not put anything on the counter. He only looked at Rei for a long moment. Then he wrote a number on a coffee-shop napkin with a pencil and folded it and slid it across.

Rei unfolded it. It was the rest. Exactly the rest. To the yen.

"Not a loan," said Haruki. "Not a gift. Your brother paid for every coffee he ever drank in this bar three months in advance, in cash, every time, because he didn't like to owe anybody. I never cashed most of it." He took the napkin back, gently, and put it in his pocket. "It's his. He's paying your rent. He'd want to."

Rei sat on the stool at the counter in the bar in Nezu with the grey light coming in through the little window, and the envelopes in front of him, and his friends on either side of him, and Okada at the end of the counter with her notebooks, and Gorō on the till.

He reached for the door in his chest.

It was still broken. It still swung. But it was not alone. There were hands on it now. Four of them. Five, if you counted a crow. Holding it, from the outside, not quite shut, so that what came through came through slowly, and he did not have to carry it all at once.

He picked up the coffee. It was still warm.

He held the cup in both hands. "Okay."

It was the first thing he had said in a week that he meant.
