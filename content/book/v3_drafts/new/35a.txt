#title Twenty-One

In Japan, nobody makes much of a birthday once you are grown. Children get a cake with strawberries on it and their name in chocolate, and a candle for every year, and their friends sing the song in English because there is no Japanese one. After that it fades. Adults are wished a happy birthday by the people who remember and buy themselves something small on the way home, and go to work the next day. The birthday that matters is the one the whole country keeps together. On the second Monday of January every year, everybody who has turned twenty since the last one puts on their best clothes, the young women in long-sleeved silk kimono hired for the day at great expense, the young men in suits or, if their families are old-fashioned, in dark formal hakama, and goes to the town hall to be told by the mayor that they are adults now. Afterwards they stand about in the cold outside the hall in crowds, taking photographs of each other, and in the evening they get very drunk.

Rei had done his the January before. He had gone in Gin's second suit, which was too long in the arms, and stood in the crowd outside the Kita ward office with Itsuki, who had come in a borrowed hakama and a pair of trainers because he had lost one of the proper sandals on the train. Gin had taken their photograph by the fountain. It was still on the fridge in the flat in Jūjō, held up by a magnet shaped like a fish. Two boys squinting in the low sun, one in a suit too big for him and one in his grandfather's hakama and white trainers, both laughing at something the man with the camera had said.

They had been born a month apart in the same winter. Rei in January and Itsuki in February. In a Japanese school the year starts in April, so children born between January and the end of March go into the year above the one they would be in anywhere else. They are always the youngest in their class. The smallest at sports day. The last to be allowed to do everything. There is a word for them, *hayaumare*, early-born, and it is said with a kind of pity. Itsuki had told Rei that on the first day they met, at twelve, at the side of the pool, as if it were a secret that explained everything about both of them. Rei had thought at the time that it did.

This year he turned twenty-one on a Friday, in the third week of January, and nobody in the world remembered it except a man in a sealed room two floors under Marunouchi.

[break The corridor]

He went straight from work.

It had been a long day in Investigation with Okada and a box of files about a flat in Itabashi where the taps ran by themselves, and he came out of the lift at the bottom of the building at half past six with ink on his fingers and his tie in his pocket, and turned toward Containment, and stopped.

The corridor was full of people.

Two nurses in pale green. A woman Rei did not know, in a grey suit, with a clipboard and a face like a shut drawer. Two field officers he half knew from the third floor, standing either side of a door with their hands loose at their sides, as if they were waiting for something to come out of it. And the air. The faint sweet burnt smell that always lay under the disinfectant down here was not faint any more. It was thick, and sour, like sugar that has caught in the pan and gone black.

The door they were standing beside was the ninth on the left.

Round it, on the walls, the black seals were smoking. Not much. A thin grey thread rising off each circle, as off a stick of incense when somebody has just blown the flame out. One of the nurses was going along the wall with a roll of fresh paper and a pot of ink, pasting a new seal over each one that had gone brown at the edges, quickly and carefully, as someone patches a tyre.

Rei started to walk toward the door, and one of the field officers put an arm out across the corridor without looking at him.

"No visitors," said the officer. "Not tonight."

"That's my friend."

"Then you'll want him to have a quiet night." The man looked at him then. He was not unkind. He was very tired. "It woke up at about four. It's been trying the walls since. He's all right. He's strapped down and he's all right. But you're not going near that door tonight, son, and nor is anybody else."

Rei looked past his arm at the little square window in the ninth door. He could not see in. The glass had gone white, as glass goes white on a cold morning, and on the inside of it, very faint, he could see lines. Curved lines, and straight lines, drawn in the frost from the inside by a finger. The shape of a frame. The beginning of a handle.

Somebody had been trying to draw a door on the window.

One of the nurses saw it at the same moment, and went to the door with her roll of paper, and pasted a white seal flat over the glass, and smoothed it down with both hands. The lines went out under it.

[break The bench]

He did not go home.

There was a bench at the end of the corridor, by the lift, for families. It was hard and green and bolted to the wall. Rei sat on it with his coat on and his hands between his knees and watched the people at the ninth door, and nobody told him to leave, so he stayed.

He did not think about the date. He had decided on the train that morning that he was not going to. It was not a day that meant anything. Gin had always made something of it anyway, every year, without being asked. Ginger pork, which was the only thing Gin could cook properly, and a strawberry shortcake from the good bakery by Jūjō station, the small round kind for two people, and a single candle stuck in the middle of it at an angle because Gin could never get it straight. Every year since Rei was eight. Even the year Gin had been on nights for a whole month and come in at seven in the evening grey in the face, with the cake box in one hand and his bag in the other, and fallen asleep at the table before the candle was out.

Last year Gin had bought two candles. One for twenty, he said, and one for the year after, because twenty was a lot of candles to carry on the train. He had lit the first and put the second away in the kitchen drawer for next time.

Rei did not think about any of that. He sat on the green bench and watched the nurse go along the wall with her paper and her ink.

At half past eight his phone buzzed. A cat. Fat and orange, asleep in a cardboard box much too small for it, with its legs over the edge. Under it, from Kato, *Okada says Room Nine is having a bad night. Are you there.*

Rei sent back a photograph of the green bench.

The answer came in a minute. *Stay there.*

At half past nine the field officers changed. The new ones were the same kind of tired. At ten one of the nurses brought him a cup of green tea from a machine without saying anything and went away again. At a quarter to eleven the thin grey threads stopped rising off the seals by the ninth door, one at a time, like candles going out round a room at the end of a long evening, and the sour black smell began very slowly to fade.

At eleven the lift opened beside him, and Moriya came out of it.

He was in his slept-in suit, with his tie loose and a paper bag from the konbini in one hand. He did not look surprised to see Rei. He looked at the bench, and at Rei on it, and at the corridor, and then he went down to the ninth door and talked to the woman with the clipboard for a while in a low voice, and showed her something in the paper bag, and she looked at it, and shrugged, and wrote something down.

Moriya went in.

[break The page]

He was in there for twenty minutes. When he came out his face was grey and there was sweat on his upper lip, as there always was when he had been reading somebody for a long time. He shut the door behind him and stood for a moment with his hand flat on it. Then he walked back up the corridor to the bench and sat down at the other end of it, with a grunt, and stretched his legs out in front of him.

"Asleep," he said. "Properly. It's gone back down. It didn't get anything." He rubbed his face. "He's tired. He held it off all on his own for seven hours, strapped to a bed. You can tell him that, tomorrow. That he held it. Nobody else is going to bother."

Rei nodded. His throat had closed.

Moriya felt in the inside pocket of his jacket and took out a sheet of grey paper, folded twice, and held it for a moment without giving it to him.

"He did this yesterday," said Moriya. "Before it woke up. I took it out last night with the others and I checked it. I check everything. I'm meant to." He turned the folded paper over in his thick fingers. "I sat with this one for a long time. It isn't a door. I couldn't find a door in it anywhere. But I don't know what it is, either, and I've been doing this job for eleven years." He looked at Rei. "He said to give it to you today. Not before. He was very firm about the day. I didn't know why." A pause. "I do now. Okada told me on the stairs."

He held it out.

Rei unfolded it.

It was a drawing in soft charcoal, smudged at the edges where Itsuki's hand had rested on it. A kitchen. Not their kitchen in Jūjō. Rei did not know it. A bigger one, with a long wooden table and a window over the sink with a plant on the sill, and a calendar by the door, and on the table a small round cake with a strawberry on it and two candles stuck in the middle, crooked, leaning against each other.

And at the table, a young man. Rei, unmistakably. Older. Not much. A year, two years. His hair was shorter, and he was laughing at something with his whole face, the way he had not laughed for five months. He had one elbow on the table and his chin in his hand.

Across the table from him, at the very edge of the paper, where the drawing ran off the side of the page, there was a hand. Only a hand, and a wrist, and the cuff of a grey jacket with the sleeve pushed up. Long fingers. Flat on the table, by the cake, as somebody lays a hand on a table while he is telling a long story and getting to the good part.

Under it, in the corner, small, in Itsuki's handwriting, the right way round for once, it said *21. I don't know why I drew this. My hand did it. Happy birthday, Rei. Don't cry on Moriya.*

Rei did not cry on Moriya.

He sat on the hard green bench with the drawing on his knees and looked at the hand for a very long time. Moriya sat at the other end of the bench and did not say anything, and after a while he got a strawberry sandwich out of the konbini bag, two slices of soft white bread with cream and strawberries in the middle, cut into triangles, the kind every konbini in Japan sells and nobody over twelve admits to buying, and put it down on the bench between them, still in its plastic.

"They didn't have cake," he said. "It's eleven at night."

Rei laughed. It came out of him all wrong.

They ate it between them, one triangle each, at the end of the corridor, under the humming lights, at ten past eleven at night, with the smell of burnt sugar fading out of the air.

[break February]

Itsuki's birthday was on the eighteenth of February, a Thursday, and Rei nearly missed it.

He was in Saitama until ten at night, standing in a car park behind a closed pachinko hall with Shimizu and Tendo, while Shimizu read the outside of a building where four people in a row had stopped being able to sleep. It was raining, the cold thin February rain that is nearly snow. Shimizu did not let anybody leave until she had walked round the building three times and written eleven pages, and when she finally shut her notebook it was a quarter past ten, and the last fast train back into the city had gone, and Tendo had to drive them, slowly, in a field car with a broken heater, along the old highway in the rain, with Riki asleep across Rei's feet in the back.

Rei got out at Tokyo Station at twenty past eleven and ran.

Marunouchi at night is all glass and stone and nobody. The office towers stand round the old red brick station like grown men round a child's toy, lit floor upon floor, empty, with the cleaners going along the windows inside. The pavements are wide and swept and wet and the taxis go by slowly with their red lights on, looking for the last salarymen. Rei ran down the middle of it all with his bag banging on his hip and a small white box held flat in front of him in both hands, like a man carrying something across a crowded platform that he does not want to drop.

He had bought it at nine, from a konbini in Saitama, in the rain, while Shimizu was on her third walk round the building. It was the only one they had. A single slice of strawberry shortcake in a clear plastic box, a little crushed down one side. He had kept it on his knees in the field car for an hour and twenty minutes so that Riki would not lie on it.

The night guard at the Division door looked at his pass, and at his wet face, and at the box, and let him through without a word.

[break The door]

The corridor was quiet. Two of the night nurses were sitting at the desk by the lift doing paperwork. Moriya was not on. The officer by Room Nine was a young woman Rei did not know, sitting on a folding chair with a book.

"Five minutes," she said, before he could ask. "Okada left a note." She looked at the clock above the lift. "You've got till midnight, technically. It's his birthday, the note says."

"It is."

She nodded, and went back to her book, and moved her chair three metres down the corridor, which was more than she had to.

Rei knelt down on the polished floor in front of the ninth door, so that his face was level with the slot in the bottom of it where the nurses passed the trays through, and pressed the grey button.

Static. Then breathing.

"Itsuki?"

"You're late." Itsuki's voice was thin and crackly through the speaker, and very pleased. "You're very late. It's twenty-five to twelve. I'd given up on you. I was going to have a sulk."

"I was in Saitama."

"Nobody is in Saitama. Saitama is a thing people say." A pause. "Is it raining? You sound wet."

"It's raining." Rei held the white box up in front of the little window. "I brought you something."

There was a silence. Then a creak, and a shuffle, and Itsuki's face appeared at the window. Thinner than in December. Paler. With his hair grown out over his ears and the ends of it still faintly stained with blue from something he had painted in November, before. He looked at the box. At the single slice of shortcake, squashed down one side, behind the plastic.

"Oh," he said. "It's been in a car."

"It's been in a car with a dog. I held it the whole way."

"I can tell. It's got a dent the shape of your thumb." Itsuki put his forehead against the glass. "I can't have it. You know that. Nothing goes through the slot after nine. It's a rule. They think I'll make a door out of a cake."

"I know. I'm going to eat it for you. Out here. While you watch. It's traditional."

"That's not traditional."

"It's traditional now."

Itsuki laughed, and stopped, and put his hand over his ribs.

Rei got the lid off the box and found he had nothing to eat it with, and ate it with his fingers, kneeling on the floor of the corridor in his wet coat, with Itsuki's face at the window watching every mouthful and telling him he was doing it wrong. Down the corridor the young officer turned a page of her book.

"I've got a candle," said Rei, when the cake was gone. "I bought one. They give you one with the cake. I can't light it. There's a sign. No open flames near the seals."

"Of course there's a sign."

"So I'm going to do this." He took his phone out and turned on the torch and held it up flat against the little window, so that the white light went through the glass and lay across Itsuki's face. "That's the candle. Make a wish."

Itsuki looked at the light for a long moment with his eyes half shut.

"Okay," he said. "I've made it."

"What is it?"

"I'm not telling you. It's a rule."

"Everything's a rule down here."

"Yes," said Itsuki. "Isn't it awful." And then, without any change in his voice at all, "Rei. Open the door."

Rei did not move.

The torch was still against the glass. On the other side of it Itsuki's face was the same face, thin and pale with the blue in his hair, but the eyes had changed. Rei had not seen them change. One moment they were Itsuki's and the next they were very dark and very still and they were not blinking.

"Open it," said the voice. It was Itsuki's voice. It was exactly Itsuki's voice. "It's my birthday. I've been in here eleven weeks. You can do it, you know. You don't need a key. You never needed a key. You only have to put your hand flat on it and *want* it, and it'll open for you. Doors always do. You know that. You've always known. Go on. Just this once. For my birthday."

Rei's hand was flat on the cold grey steel of the door beside the window. He did not remember putting it there.

He could feel it. The door. Not the steel. The other thing. The seals all round the frame, black and careful and tight, and under them, under everything, very faint, a give. As if the whole door were only leaning shut. As if he could lean on it from his side, quite gently, and it would swing. He did not know whether that was true, or whether the thing behind the glass was only making him feel it, and he understood that it did not matter.

He took his hand off it.

"No," he said.

The dark eyes looked at him.

"No," said Rei again. His voice was shaking and he did not try to stop it. "Not for your birthday. Not for anything. You can have the torch and the cake and me on the floor out here till midnight, and you can't have the door. He'd never forgive me. And I wouldn't either."

The dark eyes held his for a long time. Then they blinked.

Itsuki's face crumpled. He put both hands flat on the inside of the glass, and his forehead between them, and stood there breathing hard.

"Sorry," he said. His own voice. Small and hoarse and furious. "Sorry. Sorry. I didn't... It came up while I was making the wish. It heard the wish." He was shaking. "It does that. When I want something very much. It comes up underneath the wanting and wears it."

Rei put his own hand flat on the outside of the glass, over Itsuki's.

"What was the wish?"

Itsuki laughed, wet, against the window.

"To go home," he said. "What do you think."

The officer down the corridor had put her book down. She was sitting very still on her folding chair with one hand inside her jacket, watching them. Rei shook his head at her. After a moment she took her hand out of her jacket, empty, and picked the book up again, and did not turn the page.

They stayed like that until the clock over the lift said midnight. The torch. The two hands on the glass. Itsuki's breathing slowing down, little by little, through the speaker. When the intercom clicked off at midnight, Itsuki was still standing at the window, and he lifted one hand off the glass and pointed at Rei's chest, and then at his own, and then at the door, and shook his head, and smiled his crooked smile, and Rei understood that he was saying thank you for not opening it.

Rei put the empty cake box in his bag and the candle in his coat pocket.

He walked home. It was too late for trains. It took two hours, north out of Marunouchi through the dark wet city, up through Ueno and past the zoo with its gates chained shut, through the back streets of Nishi-Nippori and along the railway lines where the night freight goes through, to Jūjō, in the rain. He did not mind. He had the candle in his pocket the whole way, unlit, and his hand round it.

At home he put it in the kitchen drawer, beside the one Gin had bought last January for the year after, which nobody had lit either.
