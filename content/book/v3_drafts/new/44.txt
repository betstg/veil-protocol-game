#title Kyoto

The Shinkansen left Tokyo station at 7:03 in the morning on the ninth of April, and Rei had never been on one.

It was white and long and shaped like a bullet, with a nose like a duck's beak, and it slid out of the station so smoothly that he did not feel it move. Then it was moving very fast. Three hundred kilometres an hour, the little screen above the door said. Tokyo went past in a grey blur, then the sea, flat and silver, then rice fields flooded for spring planting, shining like mirrors, then mountains with snow still on their tops. A woman pushed a trolley down the aisle selling coffee and lunch boxes and frozen ice cream so hard you had to wait twenty minutes to eat it. Rei bought nothing. Mori had paid for the ticket. Mori had put it in an envelope and given it to Hara Setsu to give to him, without a note.

Mount Fuji went past on the right at 7:45. Everybody in the carriage turned to look at it. Perfect, white-topped, enormous, standing alone above the clouds. Rei thought of the faded painting on the wall of the bath house. He took a photo and sent it to Itsuki. Itsuki wrote back, *the one in tsuru-no-yu is better. it has boats.*

[break The café]

Kyoto was old. That was the first thing he felt, coming out of the station. Not old like Kita, with its narrow streets and its tired houses. Old like a grandmother is old. Calm. Deep. Patient. The city had been the capital of Japan for a thousand years. It had temples older than most countries. Rei's wrist went cold the moment he stepped out of the station and stayed cold all day. Not sharply. Gently. The way your hand goes cold in a stream.

The café was on a small street by the Kamo river, near an old wooden bridge. It was tiny, with a counter of pale wood and five stools and a big window onto the river, where the cherry trees along the bank were just coming into flower. Pale pink. Like snow that had decided to be warm.

Behind the counter, a young woman in a barista's apron was making coffee.

She was about twenty-seven. Small and slight, with narrow shoulders, and black hair to her collar clipped to one side with a plain brown clip. She wore muted, soft clothes under the apron, brown and grey and faded blue. She was plain, at first. Ordinary. Then she looked up, and Rei saw her properly, and she was not plain at all. It was hard to say why. Her face was young. But something looked out of it that was much older, the way a very old tree looks out of a young leaf. And under her eyes there were dark circles, deep and soft and brown, like bruises that had never healed. She did not try to hide them.

"Uedera," she said. Her voice was low and quiet. "You found it. Sit by the window. I'll bring you something."

"Asakura-san."

"Mio." She was already making a coffee. "Everybody who's sat at that table can call me Mio. Even if they only stood at the back."

[break Grief]

She closed the café at noon. She put a sign on the door and pulled down the blind halfway and came and sat across from him at the window with two cups of coffee, and looked at the river.

"Mori said you can see the threads," she said. "Jun's thread. Going west."

"Yes."

"I can't. Not from here. Not in Tokyo." She drank her coffee. "But I can feel the cold of it, all the way from Kyoto. Like a draught under a door." She looked at him. "I hold Grief. Do you know what that means?"

"Sadness?"

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Grief is what happens when something ends that you didn't want to end. A person. A love. A house. A city. A life you thought you'd have." She looked at the cherry trees. "Every ending in this half of the country, I feel. Small ones and big ones. A child's goldfish. A marriage. A town in Tōhoku that the sea took away in 2011." Her voice did not change. "I've held it since I was twenty-three. These came the first week." She touched the dark circles under her eyes, very lightly, without embarrassment. "They never went away. I stopped minding."

Rei did not know what to say. He looked at his coffee.

"You came to find out what you are," said Mio.

"Mori says Duty."

"Mori says. Hoshino thinks. Kasumi felt it from a bookshelf." She smiled very slightly. "None of them have shown you. That's my job. I'm the one who reads." She put her cup down. "I'm a clairsentient, like you. Level ten. That's the top of the scale they write in the Division's books." She leaned back in her chair. "I'm going to teach you one thing today. Just one. The thing I can do at the top of the scale that you can't do yet. And then I'm going to use it on you. And then you'll know."

[break The rivers]

"Look out of the window," she said. "At the people on the bridge."

He looked. The old wooden bridge over the Kamo was busy. People walking. A schoolgirl on a bicycle. Two old men in hats. A young couple taking photographs of the cherry blossom. A businessman eating a sandwich. A mother with a pushchair. Tourists with maps.

"Open your door," said Mio. "Not a finger's width. More. Lift the latch."

He looked at her. She was looking at the bridge.

"I'm here," she said. "Nothing will get in while I'm here. Lift it."

He put his right hand on his left wrist. He felt the latch, cold under his fingers. The little iron bar, the bridge, the dented key. He breathed in. And for the first time since Uruma's needle, he lifted it.

The door in his chest swung wide.

The world went thin and bright and huge. He could feel the river below the bridge, old and cold and full of a thousand years of everything. He could feel the cherry trees, the sap rising in them. He could feel every person on the bridge like a lamp in fog.

"Now don't read what happened to them," said Mio. "Don't read their past. That's what you've been doing. Objects. Rooms. Histories. That's level three. Look underneath. Look at what's *moving* them. Right now. The river under each one."

He did not know how. He tried. He looked at the businessman with the sandwich, and saw his morning. A meeting. A boss. A missed train. That was the past.

"Underneath," said Mio patiently. "Not what. Why."

He looked again. Past the meeting. Past the boss. Past the sandwich.

And there it was.

A colour. A weight. A direction. Like the current under the surface of the Kamo, which you cannot see but which moves everything. The businessman was moving toward something. All of him. Every choice, every morning, every sandwich eaten fast on a bridge so he could get back to his desk. Toward one thing.

*Shame.*

He was ashamed. Deep down. Of something a long time ago. Of his father, maybe. Of being poor once. Of something he had never told anyone. And everything he did, every day, the suit, the job, the hours, was running away from it.

He did not know it. He thought he was ambitious. He thought he liked his work.

Rei looked at the schoolgirl on the bicycle. *Joy.* Bright as a bell. Hoshino would have liked her. He looked at the mother with the pushchair. *Fear.* Not of anything. Of everything. Of the world being too big for her child. He looked at the two old men in hats. One was *Longing*, all the way down, for a wife, a house, a younger body, a country that was gone. The other was *Courage*, quietly, like a stone in a wall.

He looked at the young couple taking photographs. The man was *Love*. The woman was *Hunger*. Not for food. For more. More places, more photographs, more life, more of everything, never enough. They did not know it about each other. Rei could see, very faintly, like a crack in a cup, where it would one day break them.

"Oh," he said.

"Yes," said Mio quietly. "Oh."

"They don't know. None of them know."

"Almost nobody knows. Ever." She turned her cup in her hands. "They think they're tired, or sad, or angry, or in love. They think it's about money, or work, or the person next to them. And underneath there's one river, and it's carrying them, and they never once look down." She looked at the bridge. "And they're all so different. Fear isn't one thing. It's a mother with a pushchair. It's a soldier. It's a child at night. Hunger isn't for food. It's for love, for money, for a better life, for attention, for one more photograph of the cherry trees. Every feeling is a hundred feelings, wearing different coats." She was quiet for a moment. "That's what we hold. The Thirteen. Not thirteen feelings. Thirteen rivers. With everyone in the world in them."

[break What he was]

"Now," said Mio. "Look at me."

He turned from the window and looked at her. With the door wide open.

He saw grief. Of course. Vast and grey and gentle, like the sea in rain. Every ending in half of Japan, flowing through a small woman in a barista's apron. But under it, her own river, the one she was before the seat found her. It was the same. Grief, all the way down. She had always been grief. He did not look any deeper. It was hers.

"I'm going to look at you now," said Mio. "Hold still."

She looked at him.

It was not like Choi Hana's quick hard hands in his pockets. It was not like Sōun's cold water. It was like being looked at by the river. By something very old and very patient and completely without judgment, that simply saw what was there.

She looked for a long time. The cherry petals drifted past the window. The schoolgirl on the bicycle was long gone.

Then Asakura Mio closed her eyes, and sat back, and let out a long slow breath.

"Duty," she said.

He had known. Mori had told him. Hoshino had told Gin. The fire had told him. But hearing it from her, in this café by the river, with the door in his chest wide open, was different. It went into him like a key into a lock. He felt something turn.

"Not a little," said Mio. "Not like most people, a bit of this and a bit of that, with one river a bit stronger. You're one river. All the way to the bottom. Like a stone. Like a well." She opened her eyes. "Every choice you've made since August. Going to the platform. Going into the temple. Going into that building alone. Joining the Division. Not because you wanted to. Not because it made you happy. Because somebody had to, and you were the one standing there." She looked at him with her old young eyes. "That's Duty. It's the heaviest river there is. It doesn't ask what you want. It asks what's owed."

"What's owed," Rei repeated. He had heard those words before. He could not remember where.

"Your brother was the same," said Mio. "I met him once. Two years ago. He sat where you're sitting." She held out her hand across the table. "Can I see the key?"

He took the cord from round his neck and gave it to her. She held the cold brass key in her palm and closed her fingers over it and closed her eyes.

Her face changed. Just slightly. The dark circles under her eyes seemed to deepen.

"He's still in there," she said quietly. "In the body. Deep down. Locked in a small room. He made the room himself, before it came. A door, with his own hand on it, from the inside." She opened her eyes. "And he's holding it shut. He's been holding it shut for eight months."

Rei could not speak.

"There's something else in there with him," said Mio. "Something very old. Very polite. Duty, like him. Like you. It's wearing his body like a coat it's very fond of." She gave him back the key. "It hasn't hurt him. Not yet. It's been... careful. I don't understand it. I've never felt a thing like that be careful."

"Can I get him out?"

Mio looked out of the window at the river.

"Not from outside," she said. "Not yet. Not as you are." She stood up and took his empty cup. "Go home, Uedera. Practise what I showed you. Look at the rivers under people. Every day. On the train. At work. At your friends." She went behind the counter. "It'll help you. It'll also break your heart. Most of the time you'll see people's rivers and they'll never see their own, and you won't be able to tell them." She turned on the coffee machine. "Welcome to the Thirteen. That's what it feels like."

On the train home he looked at the rivers under every person in the carriage, the whole way to Tokyo. A tired salaryman, *Duty* like a thin thread. A grandmother, *Longing*. A small boy asleep on his mother's lap, *Wonder*, bright as a firework, all the way down.

He did not look at his own reflection in the dark window.

He did not need to. He knew what it would show.
