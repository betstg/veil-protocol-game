#title The Street

Rei felt it go wrong at the bottom of the yard.

The lights were going home. The long bright stream of them was pouring down the grey beach past the woman's table into the fog, and he could feel it through the place where his door had been, steady and strong, like a river that has found its bed. Itsuki was holding the painted door on the freight car with his white wet hands. Haruto was shouting on his concrete block. It was working.

And the woman at the table stopped holding out her hands to the dead.

She turned them instead. Palms out. Toward the living.

The hollow ones stopped walking toward the river.

All of them. Thousands. At once. They stood still in the weeds and on the rails with their white flowers in their hands, and then, slowly, as one, they turned round. Away from the beach. Toward the fence. Toward the gate and the road and the dark streets beyond, where on every doorstep in Tabata and Ōji and Higashi-Jūjō there was a little clay dish of grey ash still warm from the sending fire, and a family behind every door, asleep.

*If they won't stay,* said the voice at the table, soft and everywhere, *then nobody will.*

"They're going into the streets," said Kato. He had come up beside Rei without Rei seeing him. His face was the colour of paper. "Rei. They're going to the houses. All of them. She's telling them where." He pressed his knuckles to his forehead. "She's telling them which doors."

The hollow ones began to walk.

Not slowly now. Not like a funeral. Like a crowd leaving a stadium. Over the fence. Through the gaps. Up the old rails into the dark streets, hundreds and hundreds, with their flowers held out in front of them.

Shimizu was already shouting. Okada's field teams were already moving. The Grid was already running along the fence with their trolleys of salt. Mori went through the gate like a fire going through dry grass. There were too many of them. There were far too many.

A hand closed on Rei's wrist. Hard. Small. Cold fingers.

"Come on," said Hana.

The yard folded.

[break Tabata]

He came out on a narrow street of old two-storey houses at the top of the hill above Tabata station, in the dark, and nearly fell. The ground came up at him wrong, like stepping off an escalator. Hana caught his arm.

"Sorry," she said. "I don't usually take passengers." She was breathing hard. "Kato said this one. Third building. He said there's a woman on the walkway who won't go in."

It was a small block of flats, four floors, old, with open walkways along the front and potted plants and washing. On the third floor, in front of every door, there was a little clay dish with grey ash in it, and the smell of burnt hemp stalks hung in the air. At the far end, by her own door, an old woman was sitting on a folding stool in a cotton nightdress beside her dish, with a photograph in a frame on her knees. A man in a hat, smiling. She had not gone in. She was not ready to go in yet. She was sitting with the last of the smoke.

And coming along the walkway toward her, slowly, two people.

A man and a woman. Middle-aged. Ordinary. The man in a short-sleeved office shirt with a lanyard still round his neck, as if he had walked straight out of work. The woman in a cardigan and slippers, as if she had walked straight out of her kitchen. Each of them with a white chrysanthemum in one hand. And their eyes black, all of them, whites and all.

They were not hurrying. They went from door to door, slowly, the way you go from light switch to light switch before bed, and at each door the man tried the handle, and the woman bent down and blew on the grey ash in the dish, very gently, as if blowing out a candle that had already gone out.

The old woman on her stool had seen them. She was holding the photograph against her chest with both hands. She did not get up. She did not scream. She only watched them come.

Rei went up the stairs three at a time.

[break What Rei saw]

Rei stood at the other end of the walkway with blood running into his mouth from the jump and everything coming in.

He could see them. Both of them. Right down to the bottom.

The man in the office shirt. Under the black, under the tea and the flower and the borrowed patience, there was a man. Forty-four. Kaneda. A name on the lanyard, the letters too small to read from here, but Rei did not need to read them. He could feel it. An accounts department in a building in Ikebukuro. Twenty years at the same desk. A son who had stopped calling. A wife who had died in March. A Sunday afternoon in April when he had seen a flyer on a noticeboard at the ward office, *you do not have to be strong*, and thought, *I don't. I don't have to be. Thank God.*

Under all of it, small and grey and worn very thin, a man who had done what was expected of him every day of his life and had run out.

The woman in the cardigan. Sixty-one. A name Rei could feel and not say. A kitchen in Akabane. A daughter in Osaka. A grandson she had only seen on a phone screen. A husband in the next room, alive, watching television, who had not spoken to her properly in nine years. A Sunday in June, a white room, a hand on her knee, the first time in nine years anybody had touched her as if she were there.

Under all of it, so deep it had worn a hole right through her, wanting. Only wanting somebody to see her.

And over both of them, through both of them, the thread. Grey. Running away north, down the hill, toward the river, toward a woman at a low white table.

Behind him, on the stairs, Hana had stopped. She stood with her back to the wall and her hands in her pockets and watched him, and did not come any closer.

[break The two]

The man and the woman turned round.

They looked at Rei. Both of them. At the same moment. With the same patient, kindly, borrowed face.

*Oh,* said the voice in them. Not out loud. In the air of the walkway. In the smoke. *Oh, it's you. You came all this way. For a few little fires.* A pause, very gentle. *You can't put them all back, you know. There are thousands of doorsteps in this city, and I have thousands of hands. You can't be on every one.*

Rei nodded, slowly, as if agreeing about the weather. "No. I can't."

He stepped between them and the old woman on her stool.

He walked toward them.

He did not hurry. He walked as Tanizaki used to walk to the gate when somebody knocked. One step and then another. Not afraid. Not in a hurry. With nothing in his hands.

The man in the office shirt put out his arms. Wide. Welcoming. *Come and sit down.*

Rei did not stop. He walked right up to him, inside the reach of his arms, close enough to smell the office on him, coffee and photocopier toner and sweat, and he looked up at the black eyes, and he did not look at the black at all.

He looked past it. Down. Into the river. The grey, worn, thin river of a man who had done what was expected of him every day of his life.

He kept his voice very low, as you talk to somebody who has just woken up. "Kaneda-san."

The arms stopped.

"You did everything right," said Rei. Very quietly. Not to the thing. To the man. "Every day. For twenty years. You went to work and you did the accounts and you came home and you did it again. Nobody ever said thank you. Nobody ever told you that you could stop." He put his hand flat on the man's chest, over his heart, over the white flower in his buttonhole. "You can stop. Not like this. Not by the river. You can go home and sit down in your own chair and ring your son and tell him you're tired. That's allowed. Carrying isn't only carrying. It's knowing when to put something down so you can pick up something else."

The man's mouth opened.

The black in his eyes moved. Not out. Moved. Like ink in water when somebody puts a finger in it.

"I don't," said the man. His voice cracked. His own voice. Nobody else's. "I don't know how. I don't know how to put it down. I've never..."

"I know," said Rei. "Neither did my brother. Somebody had to teach him." He pressed his hand flat. "Like this."

And he did not take anything out of the man.

He put something in.

He did not know he was going to do it until he did. Everything was coming in through his open door, and so, he found, everything could go out. Not hard. Not pushing. Like warmth going out of a cup into cold hands. He put his hand on Kaneda's chest, and something went out of Rei's own river, the stone at the bottom of the well, the thing Mio had seen in him in Kyoto, the thing that stays in a doorway at three in the morning, and into the thin grey worn-through river of a man who had run out. Just a little. Just enough. Like lending somebody your coat on a cold night.

The thread snapped.

Rei heard it. A small, dry, high sound, like a fishing line breaking. The grey thread running out of Kaneda's chest toward the river parted, and whipped away into the dark, and was gone.

The black went out of his eyes all at once.

He was a tired man in an office shirt on a walkway, with a crushed flower in his buttonhole and his face wet. He looked down at Rei's hand on his chest. Then at Rei.

His shoulders came down. "I'm so tired." His own voice. Very small.

Rei took his hand away. "I know. Go home. Ring your son."

Kaneda sat down on the walkway with his back against the railing, slowly, like a man sitting down in his own chair at the end of a very long day, and put his face in his hands.

[break The woman]

The woman in the cardigan had not moved.

She was standing by the last door, by the little dish she had blown out, looking at Rei with her black eyes. And the thing in her was not kind any more. It was very still. Watching.

*You put something in,* it said. *You didn't take. You put.* A long pause. *Nobody does that.*

Rei did not stop walking. "Isn't it?"

He walked up to her. He did not put his arms out. He did not reach for her. He stopped in front of her and looked past the black into the river. Longing. A hole worn right through.

"Your grandson," he said. "In Osaka. He's three. He calls you on Sundays on his mother's phone and shows you his toys, and you hold the phone up close to your face so you can see him properly, and when he hangs up you sit in the kitchen with the phone in your lap for an hour." He swallowed. "Your husband's in the next room. He hasn't talked to you properly in nine years. You don't know why. Neither does he. I think he forgot how. I think he's been waiting for you to remind him."

The woman's face trembled.

"Nobody touches me," she said. Her own voice. Old and cracked and very small. "Nobody's touched me in nine years. Not even to pass me the salt. She put her hand on my knee." Her mouth twisted. "She put her hand on my knee and I would have walked into the sea for her."

Rei did not put his hand on her chest.

He held it out. Palm up. As Okada had held out her hand on the platform at Ōji, to the side, without looking, like a hand held out to a child at a crossing.

He did not say anything.

The woman looked at his hand for a long time.

Then she reached out, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal, and put her hand in it.

He put something in. Something different this time. Something he did not have a name for. Eight years of a friend standing beside him so he would not have to stand alone. A crow pressing its head against his ear. An old man pushing a cup of tea across a table with two fingers. A brother's hand on the top of his head.

The thread snapped.

The black went out of her eyes. She looked at her own hand in Rei's. She started to cry. Not quietly. Loudly. Like a child, with her whole face, standing on a stranger's walkway in her slippers.

Rei held her hand until she stopped.

[break The scar]

The old woman on the stool had not moved.

She looked at the two of them, the tired man sitting against the railing and the crying woman in slippers, and then she put her photograph down carefully on the stool and got up and went into her flat, and came out again with a box of tissues and a bottle of barley tea and two glasses. She gave the tissues to the woman. She poured the tea. She did not ask anybody anything.

Rei sat down on the top step of the stairs, because his legs had stopped working.

Hana sat down beside him.

She had watched all of it from the stairs with her hands in her pockets. She took one hand out now, the left one, and the sleeve had ridden up, as it always did, and the thick pale scar showed on the inside of her forearm in the light from the lamp. She did not pull the sleeve down.

"You put something in," she said. Her voice was strange. "I watched you. You didn't pull anything out of them. You gave them something."

"I don't know what I did."

"You gave them something." Hana looked at her own arm. "Nobody ever gave me anything when I was like that. When I was fifteen. I was..." She stopped. Her thumb was resting on the edge of the scar. "It wasn't a demon. Nobody did it to me. That's what everybody thinks. Everybody wants it to be a teacher or a stepfather or some thing in a cupboard. It wasn't." She took a breath. "It was me. I was so..."

Rei's head came up.

Not because of her. Because of the place where his door had been. Through it, four kilometres away, at the bottom of everything, he felt the grey beach.

The stream of lights was still going home. Steady and bright. But the woman at the table was not watching it any more. She had turned her head. Not toward the gate, where the hollow ones had gone. Not toward the freight car.

Toward the river end of the yard. Toward someone standing at the very edge of the grey beach, very straight, in a grey jacket with the sleeves pushed up, who had walked down between the rails while everyone else was running the other way.

*There you are,* she said. *I wondered when you'd stop hiding behind the boy.*

Hana had stopped talking. She was looking at Rei's face.

"It's him," she said. It was not a question.

"It's him."

Hana pulled her sleeve down over the scar. She stood up.

"Later," she said. "I'll tell you later. When we're both still alive." She held out her cold small hand. "I can get us there in two jumps. It'll hurt."

Rei took her hand.

Behind them, on the walkway, a tired man in an office shirt and a woman in slippers sat side by side against the railing with glasses of barley tea, and an old woman in a nightdress sat on her stool between them with her husband's photograph on her knees, and none of them said anything, and none of them went inside.
