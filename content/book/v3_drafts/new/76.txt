#title The Street

Rei felt it go wrong at the top of the hill.

He was standing in the yard among the walking hollow ones with his door off and everything coming in, and he felt the stream of lights pouring through Itsuki's painted door start to falter. Not stop. Falter. The way a river falters when somebody drops a stone in it upstream.

He felt for it. He had all six hands now and he did not know how to use most of them, but he knew how to read a structure, and he read this one. The door on the freight car. The thousand doorsteps on the other side of it, all agreeing at once. And one of them, at the top of the hill above Ōji, on the third floor, going dark.

One fire. Then another. Then three more.

Somebody was putting them out.

*Haruto,* thought Rei.

He did not run. There was nowhere to run to. The building was four kilometres away across the river and the railway and half of Kita.

He took one step.

[break The walkway]

He came out on the walkway on the third floor, in front of his own front door, in the orange light of the lamp at the top of the stairs.

He nearly fell. The ground came up at him wrong, like stepping off an escalator. He caught himself on the railing. His nose was bleeding. His ears were ringing. Somewhere very far away, behind him, at the bottom of everything, he could still feel the yard, and the door on the freight car, and Itsuki holding it with his white wet hands.

He looked up.

The walkway was full of smoke.

Half the fires were out. Kicked over. The clay dishes smashed, the frying pans upside down, the hemp stalks scattered and stamped flat and smoking on the concrete. The Yamadas' cucumber horse lay crushed by their door. The aubergine cow had rolled into the gutter.

And at the far end of the walkway, by the stairs, two people were kicking out the rest.

A man and a woman. Middle-aged. Ordinary. The man in a short-sleeved office shirt with a lanyard still round his neck, as if he had walked straight out of work. The woman in a cardigan and slippers, as if she had walked straight out of her kitchen. Each of them with a white chrysanthemum stuck through a buttonhole like a badge. And their eyes black, all of them, whites and all.

They were not hurrying. They went from fire to fire, slowly, methodically, the way you go from light switch to light switch at night before bed. A kick. A stamp. The next one.

And between them and the last fires, at the top of the stairs, with his back to the wall and a lit match in one hand and the box in the other, Haruto.

He was shaking all over. His face was white. He was holding the match out in front of him at arm's length, the way you hold a knife you do not know how to use, as if the tiny flame could keep them back.

"Get *off*," he was saying. His voice was cracked and high. "Get off, get off, that's Grandma Sasaki's, she can't even get down the stairs, get *off it*..."

The man in the office shirt looked at him. Kindly. With his black eyes.

"There's no need," he said, in a voice that was his own and also somebody else's, soft and patient and coming from very far down. "There's no need for any of this. They don't need to come here. It's so much warmer by the river. Put out the match, son. Come and sit down."

The match burned down to Haruto's fingers. He yelped and dropped it. It went out on the concrete.

The woman in the cardigan bent and blew out the last fire in front of the last door. Very gently. The way you blow out a birthday candle.

The walkway went dark except for the lamp.

[break What Rei saw]

Rei stood at the other end of the walkway with blood running into his mouth and everything coming in.

He could see them. Both of them. Right down to the bottom.

The man in the office shirt. Under the black, under the tea and the flower and the borrowed patience, there was a man. Forty-four. Kaneda. A name on the lanyard, the letters too small to read from here, but Rei did not need to read them. He could feel it. An accounts department in a building in Ikebukuro. Twenty years at the same desk. A son who had stopped calling. A wife who had died in March. A Sunday afternoon in April when he had seen a flyer on a noticeboard at the ward office, *you do not have to be strong*, and thought, *I don't. I don't have to be. Thank God.*

*Duty,* said the river under him. Small and grey and worn very thin. A man who had done what was expected of him every day of his life and had run out.

The woman in the cardigan. Sixty-one. A name Rei could feel and not say. A kitchen in Akabane. A daughter in Osaka. A grandson she had only seen on a phone screen. A husband in the next room, alive, watching television, who had not spoken to her properly in nine years. A Sunday in June, a white room, a hand on her knee, the first time in nine years anybody had touched her as if she were there.

*Longing,* said the river under her. So deep it had worn a hole right through her.

And over both of them, through both of them, the thread. Grey. Running away north, toward the river past Tabata, toward a woman at a low white table with her hands closed into fists.

Rei let go of the railing. "Haruto."

Haruto's head snapped round. He saw Rei. His mouth fell open.

"You... how did you... you weren't... you were at the river, you said you were at the..."

Rei wiped the blood off his lip with his thumb. "I was. Now I'm here. Get behind me."

Haruto did not move. He stared at Rei. At the blood on his face. At whatever he could see in Rei's eyes, which Rei did not know and did not want to.

Rei did not take his eyes off the two at the stairs. "*Haruto.*"

Haruto ran. Down the walkway, past the two of them, ducking under the man's arm, and stopped behind Rei, and grabbed the back of his shirt in both fists the way Itsuki had on the train, and held on.

[break The two]

The man and the woman turned round.

They looked at Rei. Both of them. At the same moment. With the same patient, kindly, borrowed face.

*Oh,* said the voice in them. Not out loud. In the air of the walkway. In the smoke. *Oh, it's you. You came all this way. For a few little fires.* A pause, very gentle. *You can't put them all back, you know. There are thousands of doorsteps in this city, and I have thousands of hands. You can't be on every one.*

Rei nodded, slowly, as if agreeing about the weather. "No. I can't."

He walked toward them.

He did not hurry. He walked the way Tanizaki used to walk to the gate when somebody knocked. One step and then another. Not afraid. Not in a hurry. With nothing in his hands.

The man in the office shirt put out his arms. Wide. Welcoming. *Come and sit down.*

Rei did not stop. He walked right up to him, inside the reach of his arms, close enough to smell the office on him, coffee and photocopier toner and sweat, and he looked up at the black eyes, and he did not look at the black at all.

He looked past it. Down. Into the river. The grey, worn, thin river of a man who had done what was expected of him every day of his life.

He kept his voice very low, the way you talk to somebody who has just woken up. "Kaneda-san."

The arms stopped.

"You did everything right," said Rei. Very quietly. Not to the thing. To the man. "Every day. For twenty years. You went to work and you did the accounts and you came home and you did it again. Nobody ever said thank you. Nobody ever told you that you could stop." He put his hand flat on the man's chest, over his heart, over the white flower in his buttonhole. "You can stop. Not like this. Not by the river. You can go home and sit down in your own chair and ring your son and tell him you're tired. That's allowed. That's what Duty's for. It's not just carrying. It's knowing when to put something down so you can pick up something else."

The man's mouth opened.

The black in his eyes moved. Not out. Moved. Like ink in water when somebody puts a finger in it.

"I don't," said the man. His voice cracked. His own voice. Nobody else's. "I don't know how. I don't know how to put it down. I've never..."

"I know," said Rei. "Neither did my brother. Somebody had to teach him." He pressed his hand flat. "Like this."

And he did not take anything out of the man.

He put something in.

He did not know he was going to do it until he did. Everything was coming in through his open door, and so, he found, everything could go out. Not hard. Not pushing. The way warmth goes out of a cup into cold hands. He put his hand on Kaneda's chest, and something went out of Rei's own river, his own Duty, the stone at the bottom of the well, the thing Mio had seen in him in Kyoto, and into the thin grey worn-through Duty of a man who had run out. Just a little. Just enough. Like lending somebody your coat on a cold night.

The thread snapped.

Rei heard it. A small, dry, high sound, like a fishing line breaking. The grey thread running out of Kaneda's chest toward the river parted, and whipped away into the dark, and was gone.

The black went out of his eyes all at once.

He was a tired man in an office shirt on a walkway, with a crushed flower in his buttonhole and his face wet. He looked down at Rei's hand on his chest. Then at Rei.

His shoulders came down. "I'm so tired." His own voice. Very small.

Rei took his hand away. "I know. Go home. Ring your son."

Kaneda sat down on the walkway with his back against the railing, slowly, like a man sitting down in his own chair at the end of a very long day, and put his face in his hands.

[break The woman]

The woman in the cardigan had not moved.

She was standing by the last door, by the little dish she had blown out, looking at Rei with her black eyes. And the thing in her was not kind any more. It was very still. Watching.

*You put something in,* it said. *You didn't take. You put.* A long pause. *That's not what holders do.*

Rei did not stop walking. "Isn't it?"

He walked up to her. He did not put his arms out. He did not reach for her. He stopped in front of her and looked past the black into the river. Longing. A hole worn right through.

"Your grandson," he said. "In Osaka. He's three. He calls you on Sundays on his mother's phone and shows you his toys, and you hold the phone up close to your face so you can see him properly, and when he hangs up you sit in the kitchen with the phone in your lap for an hour." He swallowed. "Your husband's in the next room. He hasn't talked to you properly in nine years. You don't know why. Neither does he. I think he forgot how. I think he's been waiting for you to remind him."

The woman's face trembled.

"Nobody touches me," she said. Her own voice. Old and cracked and very small. "Nobody's touched me in nine years. Not even to pass me the salt. She put her hand on my knee." Her mouth twisted. "She put her hand on my knee and I would have walked into the sea for her."

Rei did not put his hand on her chest.

He held it out. Palm up. The way Okada had held out her hand on the platform at Ōji, to the side, without looking, the way you hold out your hand to a child at a crossing.

He did not say anything.

The woman looked at his hand for a long time.

Then she reached out, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal, and put her hand in it.

He put something in. Not Duty this time. Something else. Something he did not have a name for. Seven years of a friend standing beside him holding a door open a crack so he could breathe. A crow pressing its head against his ear. An old man pushing a cup of tea across a table with two fingers. A brother's hand on the top of his head.

The thread snapped.

The black went out of her eyes. She looked at her own hand in Rei's. She started to cry. Not quietly. Loudly. The way a child cries, with her whole face, standing on a stranger's walkway in her slippers.

Rei held her hand until she stopped.

[break The fires]

Haruto was the one who moved first.

He let go of the back of Rei's shirt. He went down the walkway, picking up the frying pans and turning them the right way up, scraping the hemp stalks back together with his hands, sweeping up the broken clay with his shoes. He set the Yamadas' cucumber horse back on its four toothpick legs. It was crushed on one side. It stood up anyway, crooked. He fished the aubergine cow out of the gutter and wiped it on his school trousers and put it back beside the horse.

Then he took out his box of matches and lit the first fire again.

His hands were shaking so badly that it took him three matches. The fourth one caught.

Kaneda got up off the walkway. He did not say anything. He came and knelt beside Haruto and picked up a frying pan and gathered the scattered stalks into it and held it out. Haruto lit it.

The woman in the cardigan came and knelt on Haruto's other side. She took the matchbox gently out of his shaking hand. She lit the next one. And the next. Her hands were very steady. She had lit Obon fires on a doorstep for sixty years.

Rei stood at the top of the stairs and watched the three of them go down the walkway together, a boy and a tired man and an old woman in slippers, lighting forty fires one by one.

When they reached the last door, he felt it.

Four kilometres away, across the river and the railway and half of Kita, in an old freight yard, on the side of a rusted car, in a painted white door, the stream of lights steadied. Brightened. Ran on, strong and clear and unbroken, home.

And behind it, under it, through the open frame where his own door used to be, Rei felt something else.

At the grey beach. At the low white table. A woman in a soft white dress, standing very still with her hands in fists, turning her head.

Not toward the door on the freight car. Not toward the walkway.

South.

Toward the cordon. Toward the road. Toward someone walking up it at a steady pace, in a white shirt, with a jacket over his arm, whom she had been waiting for, one way or another, for a very long time.

*There you are,* she said.

Haruto looked up from the last fire.

He pointed with a spent match. "You're bleeding. Rei. You're bleeding from your ears."

"I know," said Rei. He wiped his face with his sleeve. "I've got to go back."

Haruto stood up. He did not grab Rei's shirt again. He stood in the orange light of the lamp at the top of the stairs, among forty little fires, with his bleached hair sticking up and smoke on his face, and looked at Rei.

He jerked his chin at the stairs. "Go on, then." His voice cracked. "I've got this. Go on."

Rei took one step.
