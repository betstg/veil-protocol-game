#title The White Light

By the end of June, Watanabe Jun could no longer climb the stairs of the library in Kawaguchi.

Chen Lian found him a room on the ground floor of the Grid Archive instead, behind the dusty office in Ochanomizu, among the books, with a window onto a narrow back street and a chair by it. He sat there all day. He did not read. He held. The thread ran out of his chest and away west across the city, grey and taut, and every day the grey went a little further up his fingers. By the last week of June it had reached his wrists.

Rei went every evening after work. He sat on the floor by the chair and did not say anything, and Watanabe did not say anything, and sometimes, inside Rei's head, very faint, there was a voice like someone speaking in a library. *Hello, Uedera. Thank you. You don't have to stay.*

He stayed.

On the twenty-eighth of June, Watanabe said, *Two weeks. Maybe three.*

On the twenty-ninth, Rei went up to the round room at the back of the Archive, where the thirteen chairs stood round the old dark table, and sat down on the floor beside the one that was pushed back from the others. The one with dust on its seat.

[break The chair]

He had not planned it. He had come up to be alone for a few minutes, because downstairs he could feel the thread pulling and he could not stand it. The round room was empty. The lamps were off. Only a little light came in from the corridor, yellow and soft, and lay across the table and the twelve chairs and the one.

He looked at the empty chair for a long time.

*Holding Duty,* Tanizaki had said. *Something very large. Something very tired. Holding it alone, from underneath, since 2009.*

*The seat has been calling for someone with the right shape for years,* Mori had said. *You can't sit in it. Not yet. If you sat in it now, the current would go through you like a train.*

He did not sit in it.

He put his hand on it.

Just the edge of the seat. His left hand, flat, on the old dark wood, where the dust lay thick and soft and grey. He did not know why he did it. Later he would say it was to see if it was cold. It was not cold. That was the first thing. It was warm. Warm the way a chair is warm when someone has only just stood up from it.

And then it pulled.

[break White]

It was not like the floodgate. At the floodgate there had been a demon wrapped round him like wet cloth and a door torn open and a fall. This was quiet. This was like being picked up very gently by someone much bigger than him, the way Gin used to pick him up when he was four and had fallen asleep on the train, and carry him home without waking him.

He did not fall. He was carried.

And then he was standing in white.

The same white as before. No floor. No ceiling. No horizon. Not bright, not hurting his eyes. Only white, everywhere, soft and endless.

But this time he could see further.

There were lights. Twelve of them. Very far away, in a great ring, so far apart that each one was a star. He knew them. He did not know how, but he knew them, the way you know the voices of your family in the next room. A warm small light like a kitchen fire, that was Hoshino. A hard bright orange coal, that was Mori. A grey gentle light like the sea in rain, that was Mio in Kyoto. A flickering nervous candle that was Nakashima, and a soft pink one that was Sakuma, and one that moved very fast, here and there and there, that was Inoue. And one that was almost out. Grey. Low. With a thin grey thread running out of it, away into the white, toward something he could not see.

Twelve lights. In a ring. With a gap in it.

And in the middle of the ring, very far away, so far that distance meant nothing, the column.

Tall and thin and steady and white, rising out of the white into more white. He had seen it before, from the edge, through a hole, at the floodgate. He had seen it on black paper in white chalk with a single word under it. *Don't.*

He was much closer this time.

Close enough to see that it was not empty. Inside the light, at the very bottom, at the place where the column met whatever the white stood on, there was a shape. Very small, against all that brightness. Very still. Sitting.

A man. Sitting on the ground with his knees drawn up and his arms round them, the way you sit at a bus stop late at night when the last bus has gone and you have decided to wait anyway. His head was bowed. Rei could not see his face.

He was holding something. Rei could not see what. Something heavy. Something that he held the way you hold a sleeping child on your shoulder for a long, long way, because there is no one else to carry it.

[break The gap]

The gap in the ring was beside him.

He had not noticed walking. He was standing at the edge of the great ring of lights, in the gap, where the thirteenth light should have been. And the gap had a shape. It was not empty. It was hollow, the way a mould is hollow, the way the inside of a glove is hollow. It was the shape of a person who should be standing there and was not.

He knew the shape.

He had seen it every day of his life in the mirror. He had seen it in Gin, standing very straight in a doorway at three in the morning saying *no*. He had felt it in the key round his neck. He had felt it on the kerb in Higashi-Jūjō, and in the doorway of Moriya's burning office, and in the library in Kawaguchi saying *I promise* to a man who told him not to.

It was his shape.

He did not step into it. He knew he must not. Mori had said so. Mio had said so. Every part of him that was careful said so. If he stepped into it now, the current would come through him like a train and leave nothing standing.

But he put his hand out. The same hand. His left. With the latch on the wrist.

And touched the edge of it.

[break What he did not ask]

It was like touching a wire with all the electricity in the world in it. And it was like touching the hand of someone you love in a dark room and knowing it is them.

The whole white shook.

Far away, in the middle of the ring, the man sitting at the bottom of the column lifted his head.

Rei could not see his face. Too far. Too bright. But he saw the head lift. He saw the shoulders straighten, very slightly, the way a man's shoulders straighten when he hears his name called across a crowded station and is not sure he heard it.

*One question,* Rei thought. *You get one question. That's the rule.*

He had a hundred. *Where is my brother. How do I get him back. How do I save Watanabe. What are you. Who are you. Why are you so tired.*

He thought of Tanizaki's front room. An hour. Two cups. The newspaper turning. *Come back when you have finished being in a hurry.*

He did not ask anything.

He stood at the edge of the gap, with his hand on the edge of the shape of himself, and looked across the white at the tired man at the bottom of the light. And he did not ask him for anything at all. He only looked. The way you look at someone at a bus stop late at night, and nod, so that they know someone else is awake.

The man at the bottom of the column looked back.

Then, very slowly, he lifted one hand from round his knees. Just a little. Just the fingers. The way you lift your fingers off the steering wheel of a car to say *thank you* to someone who has let you out of a side road.

There was ink on the side of the first finger.

And a voice. Not loud. Not from anywhere. A man's voice, tired and kind and very, very old, like a voice coming out of a well, from somewhere very deep down, that has not spoken in a long time and is surprised to find it still can.

*Not yet,* it said. *Go home.*

And a hand that was not there put itself flat on Rei's chest, very gently, and pushed.

[break Gone]

He woke up on the floor of the round room with his cheek on the old wood and the morning sun coming in through the window.

The morning sun. It was morning.

He sat up. His whole body hurt, the way it hurts after a fever, in every joint. His nose had bled, a lot, onto the floor and the leg of the empty chair and the front of his shirt, and dried brown. His mouth was so dry he could not swallow. His left hand, the one that had touched the chair, was numb from the fingertips to the wrist and covered in grey dust.

The door of the round room was open. Chen Lian was sitting in it, on the floor, with her back against the frame and her knees up and her glasses pushed up on her head, asleep.

"Chen-san," said Rei. His voice cracked.

She woke all at once. She looked at him. Her face did something complicated, and then she was across the room and on her knees in front of him with both hands on his face.

"You're back," she said. "You're back. You stupid, stupid boy. You absolute idiot. You're back." She was crying. "Mori's going to kill you. Then I'm going to kill you. Then Mori's going to kill you again."

Rei looked past her at the window, at the light that was the wrong colour for any hour he remembered. "What day is it?"

"Friday." She sat back on her heels and wiped her face with her sleeve. "You went on Wednesday night. I came up at ten to turn the lamps off and you were sitting by the chair with your hand on it and your eyes open and nobody behind them. I couldn't move you. Nobody could move you. Mori tried. Inoue tried. Your hand was stuck to that chair like it had grown there." She took a shaky breath. "Thirty-four hours, Rei. You lost a whole day. A whole day and a night and a morning. Your phone rang forty-one times. Your friend with the paint came. He sat right there for nine hours. Your partner came, and he stood in the corridor and he said he couldn't hear you, he couldn't hear you at all, it was like you weren't there, and he went white and had to sit down. Your crow broke the window." She pointed. There was a hole in the glass of the round room's one window, and cardboard taped over it, and a black and white feather caught in the tape. "He's been on the roof of the building since Wednesday. He won't come down. He won't eat."

Rei looked at the empty chair. At the dust on its seat. At the clean place where his hand had been, a perfect palm and five fingers, the wood dark and bare.

"I didn't ask it anything," he said.

Chen Lian stared at him.

"I didn't ask. I got there and I could have asked and I didn't. I just looked at him." His voice was shaking. "There's a man in there, Chen-san. At the bottom of the light. Sitting on the ground. Holding something. He's so tired. He's been holding it so long."

Chen Lian did not say anything for a long time. Her face, which was always so full of curiosity, so delighted by everything, had gone very still.

"A man," she said at last. Very quietly.

"He told me to go home. He said *not yet*."

Chen Lian closed her eyes.

"I've read every book in this building," she said. "Every one. Some of them three times. I've read every account anyone ever wrote of going into the white. Every one of them says the same thing. Light. A column. A centre. Something vast and impersonal, like the sun." She opened her eyes. "Not one of them ever said *a man*."

[break The roof]

He went home on Friday afternoon. Itsuki came and got him in a taxi, and did not say anything the whole way, and held his wrist in the back seat so hard that it hurt, and when they got out at the bottom of the hill Itsuki punched him in the shoulder, hard, twice, and then hugged him, and then punched him again.

"*Thirty-four hours*," he said.

Rei kept his eyes on the pavement. "I know."

Itsuki grabbed the front of his shirt and shook it once. "You promised. You promised to tell me first. Every time."

Rei let himself be shaken. "I didn't know I was going."

"*That's not an excuse.*" Itsuki's voice cracked off the concrete. "That's never going to be an excuse. Not ever. Not with you." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Kato's coming over. He's furious. He's going to shout at you. Let him."

Kato came at seven. He stood in the doorway of the flat and looked at Rei for a long time without saying anything. Then he walked in and sat down at the kitchen table in Gin's chair and put his king on the table, and put his face in his hands.

"I couldn't hear you," he said into his hands. "For a day and a half. I've heard you every day since September. Even asleep. Even across the building. You think so loud. And then there was nothing. Like somebody turned off the sea." He lifted his head. His eyes were red. "Don't do that again. Please. I can't do that again."

Rei sat down across from him with his hands flat on the table, because there was nothing else to do with them. "I'm sorry."

"I know you're sorry. I can hear you being sorry." Kato picked up his king and turned it, very fast. "It's horrible. Stop it."

On the walkway outside, just after dark, there was a thump on the railing, and the sound of claws on metal.

Gorō walked along the railing to the open kitchen window, and climbed in over the sill, and dropped onto the floor, and walked across the tatami with his rolling crow's walk, and climbed up onto Rei's knee. He was thin. One of his wing feathers was broken from the window in Ochanomizu. He looked at Rei with the black eye and then with the blue one.

Then he took Rei's ear in his beak, very hard, harder than he ever had, and held it, and did not let go for a long time.

Nobody laughed. Not even Itsuki.
