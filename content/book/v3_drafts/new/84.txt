#title Duty

For a little while, nobody was holding the door.

Tokyo found out all at once.

In Kita, on the walkway outside flat 204, Haruto was crouched over the Yamadas' crooked cucumber horse with his last match when every fire on the walkway leaned sideways, all forty of them, as if a wind had come down the corridor from nowhere. He looked up. An old man in a grey summer kimono was standing at the far end by the stairs, an old man Haruto had never seen in his life, and he was not see-through, and he was not glowing, and he was frowning at the horse.

He pointed at it with his chin. "That's crooked."

Haruto dropped the match. Then he picked it up again, and struck it, and lit the horse's fire properly, with his hand shaking, because the old man was right.

In Takinogawa, the Reverend Egami was halfway up the bell tower steps with his second terrible beer when the bell rang without him. Once. Long and low. And all down the hill, from the temple gate to the river, the dead came walking up the road in the clothes they had died in, carrying nothing, looking about them at the streetlights and the vending machines with the mild interest of people who have been away on a long trip.

In the bath houses, Hara Setsu's water went cold in every tub at once and then, slowly, warm again, and a hundred old men sitting up to their chins in it looked round and saw their brothers and their schoolfriends and their wives sitting beside them in the steam.

In Ni-chōme, in Keiko's bar, every glass on the shelf rang like a struck bowl.

At the cordon on the Tabata road, Kumagai was standing beside Ono with his hands in his pockets when the whole street in front of them went thin as paper, and through it, along the line of the old tracks, came a slow river of people walking north toward the yard. Ono made a small sound. Kumagai took one hand out of his pocket and put it on the young officer's shoulder and left it there, and did not take it away, and did not say anything, and did not write any of it down.

Every door in the city opened.

Not torn. Not broken. Just open. The way a house stands open on a summer night with every window up and the screens off. And in every doorway, on both sides, somebody stood looking out at somebody else.

[break The fall]

In the white, Rei watched the wall come down.

Kuze had turned round. He was standing in front of Rei now in his indigo coat with his hands at his sides and his face very calm, and behind him, the light he had carried for three hundred years hung on nothing.

It did not fall all at once. It leaned. The way a wave leans at the top before it breaks. Enormous. Brighter than anything Rei had ever seen, brighter than the floodgate, brighter than the column in June, and it leaned, slowly, out over the white, out over the ring, out over the twelve roaring fires and the hollow thirteenth with his handprint on its edge.

"It is heavier than it looks," said Kuze. His voice was quiet and very polite, as if he were warning someone about a step. "I am sorry. I should have told you that before I turned."

Rei swallowed. "Would it have changed anything?"

Kuze looked at him. Something wry moved at the corner of his mouth. "No. I don't suppose it would."

The twelve fires had stopped roaring. They were straining now. Every one of them. Rei could feel the people in them leaning into the wind with everything they had. Hoshino with his shoulder against a walking table. Mio in Kyoto on her knees. Chen Lian with her eyes shut and her lips moving, counting something. Mori on the black beach with his boots dug into the sand, his light held up over his head with both arms, shaking.

They could not hold it. Rei could feel that too. Twelve was not enough. Twelve had never been enough. That was why a man had walked into the middle of everything three hundred years ago and sat down.

The wall leaned further.

Rei looked at the gap.

*You can't sit in it,* Mori had said, in a boiler room under a bath house, a whole lifetime ago. *Not yet. If you sat in it now, the current would go through you like a train.*

He looked at his own chest. At the empty frame where his door had been. Where everything came in and nothing stopped it.

*A train goes through a station,* he thought. *That's what stations are for.*

He walked to the hollow thirteenth, and turned round, and sat down.

[break The current]

It went through him like a train.

Mori had been right about that. Mori was usually right. It came in through the top of his head and the soles of his feet and the empty frame in his chest all at once, and it was everything. Not light. Not weight. Everything. Every duty in the city. Every promise anybody had ever made and kept and broken and meant to keep. Every rent paid late and every dog walked in the rain and every old man who went every New Year's morning to a temple for one cup of tea. Every mother who stayed up. Every brother who left his umbrella by the door. Every investigator who went out at dusk with a notebook in his coat because somebody had to.

It was too much. It was so much too much that there was no word for it, and for one second Rei felt himself begin to come apart the way a paper boat comes apart in a river, slowly at the corners and then all at once.

And then it went through.

That was the thing. It went *through.* There was no door for it to break against. No latch for it to tear off. He had taken all that off himself on a black beach an hour ago with his own hands, and now there was nothing in him for it to snag on, and it came in and went through and went on, out the other side, into the ring, into the twelve straining fires, into the lights of the city above, the way a river goes through a town that has finally stopped building walls against it.

He was not holding it.

He was letting it pass.

*The middle,* he thought. *Not holding the line. Walking down it.*

Something put its hand on his left shoulder.

Then something on his right.

He opened his eyes. He did not know when he had closed them. The twelve fires were not straining any more. They had leaned in. All the way in, toward the centre, toward him, the way they had leaned in at the end of June when he stood among them with a broken door, and every one of them had a hand on him. Hoshino's, square and warm and smelling of rice. Mio's, cool as rain. Chen Lian's, small and quick. Mori's, from a beach a long way off, heavy and hard and unbelievably gentle.

And the wall stopped leaning.

It hung over them all. Thirteen lights in a ring, and in the centre, where for three hundred years there had been one man on his own, there was nothing at all now but the thing they were all holding up together. It did not shine any less. It was not any lighter. But it was not on anybody's back.

It was on everybody's.

Rei laughed. He did not mean to. It came out of him wet and cracked and stupid, and he could not stop.

Kuze was standing in front of the chair, looking down at him, with his hands at his sides and a face that Rei would remember for the rest of his life.

"Oh," said Kuze softly. "Oh, that is much better. Why did I never think of that."

Rei wiped his nose on his wrist. "There wasn't anyone else."

"No." Kuze's eyes went round the ring. Slowly. From light to light. As if he were counting them, and could not quite believe the number. "No, there wasn't. Not then." He looked back at Rei. "There is now."

[break The other table]

On the beach, Gin felt the wall come down.

Not with his skin. With the thing beside him, inside him, the old careful thing that had been sitting next to him on a bench in his own skull for a year. It went very still when the veil fell. And then it stood up.

Gin felt it stand. The way you feel somebody next to you on a train get up from their seat.

He lifted his head.

The grey sea was coming back. Not all of it. A strip, out past the old rails, where the fog had thickened again into a wall. And out of the fog, slowly, the ten shapes were walking back toward the shore. The red one. The white thin one. The faceless beautiful one. Seven more that Gin could not have described to anybody and did not want to. They were not attacking. That was the worst of it. They were coming back the way stray dogs come back to a house where somebody used to feed them, looking for the door.

Looking for somebody to wear.

The doors were all open. Every one in the city. And there were ten thousand people in this yard alone with their faces turned toward the river.

Mori stood at the edge of the sand with his light up over his head, shaking. Misaki lifted one small hand and a bubble of nothing bloomed in the air in front of the red shape and swallowed it to the shoulders and then, for the first time all night, wobbled. Kurosawa was not where he had been a second ago. Then he was. Then he was not.

*They have no chair,* said Yushiro.

Gin heard it inside, in the old polite voice. Very quiet.

*She called them to a table. She was going to give them a ring of seats and somebody to answer to. And then she was gone. And now they have the open doors and nobody to keep the account.*

Gin looked at the low white table. At the ring of cushions round it. Every one of them empty. And the gap.

He knew what Yushiro was going to say before it said it. He had been sharing a head with it for a year.

Gin's hands closed on his knees. "Don't you dare," he said out loud.

*Not her chair.*

The voice was very gentle.

*Mine.*

Gin's hands were shaking. He looked down at them. They were his hands. Long. Thin from a year of somebody else forgetting to feed them. They had been his hands the whole time. He had just been letting someone else use them.

Gin dragged his sleeve across his face. "You don't owe anybody. You said. The account's closed."

*It is. That is why I can choose.* A pause. Gin felt it, the way you feel someone look at you. *Somebody has to keep the accounts on that side, Gin. Somebody always has. If nobody sits there, they will go into the open doors, one at a time, and wear people, and there will be a thousand Uedera Gins by the morning. And nobody in them to say no.*

Gin's throat closed.

*Your brother sat down,* said Yushiro. *On the other side. In the light. I felt him do it. He is holding Duty now. The living kind.* There was something in the voice that Gin had never heard in it before. It took him a moment to recognise it. Pride. *So there is no empty seat for me to chase any more. No hollow for me to pour into. I do not need a man to wear to get there.* A pause. *I can just go and sit down.*

"With them?" Gin jerked his chin at the fog. At the ten shapes. "They're monsters."

*So was I.*

Gin had no answer to that.

*I will keep the account,* said Yushiro. *Honestly. What they take, they pay. What they are owed, they get. Nothing more. Nobody's grandmother. Nobody's brother.* The voice went softer. *Somebody taught me that it can be done. Sitting at a table, drinking tea, being told every day what kind of thing I was.*

Gin shut his eyes.

A laugh came out of him, broken in the middle. "That was me. I was mostly just being rude."

*Yes,* said Yushiro. *It worked.*

[break Leaving]

It went out of him the way it had come in.

Gin did not remember the coming in. He had been on the walkway outside the flat, a year ago, with his keys in his hand, and then he had not been. But he felt the going. He felt it all the way. It was like a hand letting go of his, one finger at a time, slowly, so that he would know each finger as it went.

He was on his knees on the grey sand.

He was alone in his head.

It was so quiet. He had forgotten it could be that quiet. Just him. Just his own breath, and his own heart going much too fast, and his own thoughts, which were mostly swearing.

And in front of him, between him and the white table, something stood up off the sand.

He did not know what it looked like. He would try to describe it to Rei later, and fail, and give up. It was shaped like a man the way a shadow is shaped like a man. Tall. Very straight. Very still. It had good manners. That was the only thing Gin could ever say about it with any confidence. Even standing there, it had good manners.

It turned and looked down at him.

Gin looked up at it. His eyes were running. He was not going to do anything about that.

It bent its head toward him. "Thank you for the tea," said Yushiro.

Out loud. Not inside. A voice from outside him, for the first time. Polite. Quiet. Setting the words down one by one like cups.

Gin's mouth twisted. "You don't owe me anything."

The shape stayed bent toward him. "I know. That is why I am saying it."

Then it turned, and walked across the grey sand to the low white table, past the folded dress, past the cracked stone, and stood for a moment at the gap in the ring of cushions, looking down at it.

And sat.

It sat the way Tanizaki used to sit on his step. Without any hurry. As if it had been meaning to for a while.

Out on the grey sea, ten vast shapes stopped walking.

Gin felt them stop. Everybody on the beach felt them stop. Misaki's bubble steadied in the air. Mori's arms, over his head, stopped shaking. The shapes stood at the edge of the fog, and turned, slowly, all of them, toward the table. Toward the thing sitting at it, very straight, with its hands folded in front of it, waiting, the way a clerk waits behind a desk.

And then, one by one, they came.

Not to the beach. Not to the yard. Not to the open doors and the ten thousand faces. To the table. They came out of the fog and shrank as they came, the way a mountain shrinks as you walk away from it, and when they reached the ring of cushions they were no bigger than people, and they sat. The red one. The white thin one. The faceless beautiful one. All ten. Each in its place. Each with its hands in front of it on the low white table.

Eleven of thirteen. And two gaps. The sixth cushion, Despair's, where Kishin would have sat. And Hunger's, where there was nothing left to sit.

Yushiro looked at them both, and left them empty.

Then it lifted the teapot that stood in the middle of the table, the one Fukai Rinka's hands had poured from all night, and looked into it, and set it down again without pouring.

"Not tonight," Gin heard it say. To the table. To all of them. Very politely. "Tonight we are going to sit here and be quiet, and let them go home."

And the fog closed round the table, softly, like a door being pulled to by somebody who did not want to wake the house.

[break Set down]

In the white, Kuze Kaname sat down on the ground.

He did not fall. He did not collapse. He sat, the way a man sits down on the step of his own house after a very long walk, with his knees up and his forearms on them and his inked hands hanging loose between. And he let out a breath that went on for a very long time.

Rei, in the chair, watched him do it.

He leaned forward. "Are you all right?"

"My legs," said Kuze, with great dignity, "have not been asked to do anything for three hundred years, and they are making their feelings known." He tipped his head back and looked up at the ring. At the thirteen lights. At the wall hanging over them all, held, steady, shining, on nobody's back and everybody's. "Somebody has sat down on the other side as well. Do you feel it?"

Rei did. Far off, under everything, at the other end of the current. A stillness. Very straight. With good manners.

"Yeah," said Rei. His voice cracked. "That's the one who kept my brother."

"He will keep them too." Kuze closed his eyes. "Thirteen and thirteen. The light and the shadow of each. Held by people who chose it." He was quiet for a moment. "That is how it was meant to be, I think. Before. Before anybody was frightened enough to put it all on one man."

Rei shook his head against the back of the chair. "You weren't frightened."

"No." Kuze opened one eye and looked at him. "I was stubborn. It is not the same. It is sometimes worse."

Rei laughed again. It hurt.

Then Kuze got up.

It took him two tries. He put one hand flat on the ground of the white and pushed, the way an old man pushes up out of a low chair, and stood, and brushed down his indigo coat with both hands, carefully, the way you brush a coat before you go out somewhere that matters. He touched the inside pocket. The notebook was still there.

He looked round the white once. At the ring. At the wall. At the boy in the chair.

He settled his hands in his sleeves. "I think," said Kuze Kaname, "that I will go home now."

Rei's chest went tight. "Do you know the way?"

"Up the road by the river." Kuze smiled. "It's the meeting hour. The doors are all open. And there is a fire on every doorstep in the city tonight. Somebody lit them." He looked at Rei with his whole tired kind face. "Somebody told them where to light them."

Rei sniffed hard. "A boy called Haruto. And a lot of other people."

"Then thank Haruto for me." Kuze tilted his head. "And thank your brother. Properly. Not in the way the two of you thank each other, which as far as I can tell is mostly by threatening to kill each other later."

Rei's face crumpled. "Yeah. Okay."

Kuze bowed to him.

Not a small bow. A real one. From the waist, slow and low and deep, the bow of a man three hundred years old to a young man in a chair. And held it. And straightened.

Then he turned and walked away across the white.

And as he walked, the white changed under his feet. Rei saw it. It went gold, and long, and orange, the colour of a summer evening when every shadow is three times the length of the thing that casts it. And it was a road. A dirt road along a river, with willows, the leaves hanging down into the water. And Kuze walked along it with his hands in his sleeves and his coat swinging, not fast, not slow, the way a man walks home at the end of a working day when he knows he is late and has decided not to mind.

At the end of the road there was a gate.

Wooden. Old. With a grey tiled roof, and from the beam under the roof a paper lantern hanging, orange, with three black characters on it.

And on the step of the gate, a young man in a plain dark coat was standing with a tray in his hands and a scowl on his face, looking up the road.

Rei saw the young man see him. Saw him go very still. Saw the tray tip, and catch, and tip again.

He did not hear what the young man said. He was too far away. But he saw Kuze laugh, and lift one hand, the inked one, and keep walking. Not faster. Exactly the same pace. All the way up the road, all the way to the gate, all the way to the step.

He took the cup off the tray.

He drank it cold.

Then the gold went out of the road, and the willows went, and the river, and the gate, and the white came back, quiet and empty, and there was nobody standing in it any more but the thirteen lights in their ring, and the wall they were holding, and a boy in a chair with his face wet, listening.

Far away, very faint, up through the current, from a black beach in Kita, he heard his name.

In one voice. Rough and dry and a little too fast.

Only one.
