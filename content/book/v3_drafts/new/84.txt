#title Duty

For a little while, nobody was holding the door.

Tokyo found out all at once.

On the road above the yard, in the back of Shimizu's van, Usami Hinata looked up from the empty onigiri bag in his lap and saw, sitting beside him on the bench seat, a young man he had never seen and had known for two years. Thin. Shy. With his hands folded. Looking at Usami as if he had been waiting a very long time to be looked at.

"Oh," said Usami. "Oh. It's you. You've got a face."

"*So have you,*" said Ren. "*I've only ever seen it in mirrors.*"

In Takinogawa, the Reverend Egami was halfway up the bell tower steps with his second terrible beer when the bell rang without him. Once. Long and low. And all down the hill, from the temple gate to the river, the dead came walking up the road in the clothes they had died in, carrying nothing, looking about them at the streetlights and the vending machines with the mild interest of people who have been away on a long trip.

In the bath houses, Hara Setsu's water went cold in every tub at once and then, slowly, warm again, and a hundred old men sitting up to their chins in it looked round and saw their brothers and their schoolfriends and their wives sitting beside them in the steam.

In Ni-chōme, in Keiko's bar, every glass on the shelf rang like a struck bowl.

At the cordon on the Tabata road, Kumagai was standing beside Ono with his hands in his pockets when the whole street in front of them went thin as paper, and through it, along the line of the old tracks, came a slow river of people walking north toward the yard. Ono made a small sound. Kumagai took one hand out of his pocket and put it on the young officer's shoulder and left it there, and did not take it away, and did not say anything, and did not write any of it down.

Every door in the city opened.

Not torn. Not broken. Just open. Like a house on a summer night with every window up and the screens off. And in every doorway, on both sides, somebody stood looking out at somebody else.

[break The fall]

In the white, Rei watched the wall come down.

Kuze had turned round. He was standing in front of Rei now in his indigo coat with his hands at his sides and his face very calm, and behind him, the light he had carried for three hundred years hung on nothing.

It did not fall all at once. It leaned. Like a wave at the top before it breaks. Enormous. Brighter than anything Rei had ever seen, and it leaned, slowly, out over the white, out over the ring, out over the twelve roaring fires and the hollow thirteenth with his handprint on its edge.

"It is heavier than it looks," said Kuze. His voice was quiet and very polite, as if he were warning someone about a step. "I am sorry. I should have told you that before I turned."

Rei swallowed. "Would it have changed anything?"

Kuze looked at him. Something wry moved at the corner of his mouth. "No. I don't suppose it would."

The twelve fires had stopped roaring. They were straining now. Every one of them. Rei could feel the people in them leaning into the wind with everything they had. Hoshino with his shoulder against a walking table. Mio in Kyoto on her knees. Chen Lian with her eyes shut and her lips moving, counting something. Mori on the black beach with his boots dug into the sand, his light held up over his head with both arms, shaking.

They could not hold it. Rei could feel that too. Twelve was not enough. Twelve had never been enough. That was why a man had walked into the middle of everything three hundred years ago and sat down.

The wall leaned further.

Rei looked at the gap.

*You can't sit in it,* Mori had said, in a boiler room under a bath house, a whole lifetime ago. *Not yet. If you sat in it now, the current would go through you like a train.*

He looked at his own chest. At the empty frame where his door had been. Where everything came in and nothing stopped it.

*A train goes through a station,* he thought. *That's what stations are for.*

He walked to the hollow thirteenth, and turned round, and sat down.

[break The current]

It went through him like a train.

Mori had been right about that. Mori was usually right. It came in through the top of his head and the soles of his feet and the empty frame in his chest all at once, and it was everything. He knew, now, what it was. Kuze had it in him. Everybody had a little. Rei had a great deal. Duty. Not light. Not weight. Everything. Every duty in the city. Every promise anybody had ever made and kept and broken and meant to keep. Every rent paid late and every dog walked in the rain and every old man who went every New Year's morning to a temple for one cup of tea. Every mother who stayed up. Every brother who left his umbrella by the door. Every investigator who went out at dusk with a notebook in his coat because somebody had to.

It was too much. It was so much too much that there was no word for it, and for one second Rei felt himself begin to come apart like a paper boat in a river, slowly at the corners and then all at once.

And then it went through.

That was the thing. It went *through.* There was no door for it to break against. No latch for it to tear off. He had taken all that off himself on a black beach an hour ago with his own hands, and now there was nothing in him for it to snag on, and it came in and went through and went on, out the other side, into the ring, into the twelve straining fires, into the lights of the city above, like a river through a town that has finally stopped building walls against it.

He was not holding it.

He was letting it pass.

*The middle,* he thought. *Not holding the line. Walking down it.*

Something put its hand on his left shoulder.

Then something on his right.

He opened his eyes. He did not know when he had closed them. The twelve fires were not straining any more. They had leaned in. All the way in, toward the centre, toward him, as they had leaned in that afternoon when he stood among them with a broken door, and every one of them had a hand on him. Hoshino's, square and warm and smelling of rice. Mio's, cool as rain. Chen Lian's, small and quick. Mori's, from a beach a long way off, heavy and hard and unbelievably gentle.

And the wall stopped leaning.

It hung over them all. Thirteen lights in a ring, and in the centre, where for three hundred years there had been one man on his own, there was nothing at all now but the thing they were all holding up together. It did not shine any less. It was not any lighter. But it was not on anybody's back.

It was on everybody's.

Rei laughed. He did not mean to. It came out of him wet and cracked and stupid, and he could not stop.

Kuze was standing in front of the chair, looking down at him, with his hands at his sides and a face that Rei would remember for the rest of his life.

"Oh," said Kuze softly. "Oh, that is much better. Why did I never think of that."

Rei wiped his nose on his wrist. "There wasn't anyone else."

"No." Kuze's eyes went round the ring. Slowly. From light to light. As if he were counting them, and could not quite believe the number. "No, there wasn't. Not then." He looked back at Rei. "There is now."

[break The others]

On the beach, Gin felt the wall come down.

Not with his skin. With the thing beside him, inside him, the old careful thing that had been sitting next to him on a bench in his own skull since the night of the old man. It went very still when the veil fell.

Gin lifted his head.

The grey sea was coming back. A strip of it, out past the old rails, where the fog had thickened again into a wall. And out of the fog, slowly, the shapes were coming back. The red one. The big slow ones. Hundreds of small ones. They were not attacking. That was the worst of it. They were coming back like stray dogs to a house where somebody used to feed them, looking for the door.

Every door in the city was open.

Mori stood at the edge of the sand with his light up over his head, shaking. Misaki lifted one small hand and a bubble of nothing bloomed in front of the red thing and swallowed it to the shoulders and then, for the first time all night, wobbled. Kurosawa had his hand flat on the side of a man's neck who had walked out of the weeds with black eyes, holding his heart slow.

Gin looked down at his own hand.

There was a pager in it. Old. Dark metal. Wrapped in tiny brush seals like thread. With an orange light beating under the screen like a coal. Rei had pushed it into his hand on the grey sand before he went down, without a word, and closed Gin's fingers round it.

*Open it,* said the voice beside him. Very quiet. *He gave his word for it. I felt him give it. Open it.*

Gin pressed the button that had been soldered shut. It gave under his thumb as if it had been waiting forty years for somebody to try.

[break Courage]

It came out of the pager like a man coming out of a lift.

Gin would try to describe it to Rei afterwards, and get as far as *it was standing up very straight and it was much too loud*, and give up. It was shaped like a man the way a bonfire is shaped like a man. It stood on the grey sand between the yard and the sea, between ten thousand open doors and the things coming back for them, and it looked at the fog.

Then it turned round and looked at the beach. At Mori with his light over his head. At Misaki with her hand out and the cat on her shoulders. At Kurosawa. At Okada by the gate with her notebooks. At Hana on a rail with her head between her knees. At Itsuki, white to the elbows, kneeling by his sleeping father. At Kato, in the weeds, holding a dead woman's hand. At Sena in his grey suit, writing. At Usami in the van door, with a thin shy young man standing beside him. At the old dog by the wreck, and the girl in the pink hoodie beside her. At an old man in a grey suit in the gateway, with another dog.

*OH,* it said. Not in Gin's head. In everybody's. Loud. *OH. LOOK AT THEM. LOOK AT ALL OF THEM.*

It turned back to the fog.

*NO,* it said. To the shapes. To all of them. *NOT THESE ONES. THESE ONES ARE BRAVE.*

And it walked into the shallows of the grey sea and stood there with its arms out, and the shapes coming in stopped.

[break Two more]

Two more came after that. From the city side. From behind.

Gin felt them before he saw them. One was small and warm and bright and fizzing, like a sparkler held by a child, and it came over the fence of the yard turning cartwheels that were not quite cartwheels, laughing, and wherever it went, the black-eyed people still wandering in the weeds stopped and blinked and looked round as if somebody had told them a very good joke and they had nearly got it.

*Oh, this is FUN,* it said, to nobody. *I came all this way and nobody told me there'd be a PARTY.*

The other came slowly. It was thin and quick and had too many eyes, and it moved along the edge of the sand sniffing, curious, interested in everything. It looked at the shapes in the fog with enormous attention, the way a cat looks at a moth.

*New,* it said. *Never tasted any of those.* And it went into the shallows after the Courage one, and the small sour shapes nearest the shore began, very quickly, to go missing.

*Who are they,* said Gin, inside. *What are they.*

*Two of the five who came through,* said the voice beside him. *Like me. Like her. Like the one in the box.* A pause. *They never cared about this. They came across to look. One for fun. One because it is hungry for things it has not tried.* Another pause. *I think the brave one called them. I think it was lonely.*

[break The thirteen]

And then the fog changed.

It did not thin. It went still. All of it. As if the whole grey sea had held its breath.

And out of it, slowly, from very far off, from the other side of the other side, came the rest.

Gin had no words for them at all. Afterwards he did not even try. There were not many. Nine, perhaps. Ten. Tall and still and very old, older than anything in the yard, older than the river, walking across the grey water toward the beach without hurrying, the way judges walk into a court. The shapes in the fog went out of their way as they came. Not afraid. Simply out of the way, as you step aside for something that belongs where you are standing.

*The others,* said the voice beside him. Very quietly. With something in it Gin had never heard before. *The ones who stayed behind. They have never come. Not once. Not for anything.*

The tall still things came to the low white table on the grey beach and stood round it.

And one by one, without any ceremony, they sat down.

In a ring. Round the table, on the far side of the beach, where the fog began. And the lesser shapes in the fog, the hungry ones, the angry ones, the hundreds of small sour things that had come because somebody said there was room, turned round, all of them, and went back into the grey, quietly, without being told, like children sent home when the grown-ups come back into the room.

There were gaps at the table. Gin counted them. One where the voice beside him should have sat. One where the thing in the pager should have sat, which was standing up to its knees in the shallows with its arms out, still shouting. Two for the sparkler and the many-eyed thing. One for the woman with the teapot, who would never sit anywhere again.

And one more. At the head of the table. Empty. Gin did not know why. He could feel that it had been empty for a very long time, and that the things sitting round it had not forgotten.

*Love,* said the voice beside him. *Nobody has sat there since before the river. That is why it has all been so wrong on the far side. Nobody holding it.*

Gin looked at the empty place for a long time.

*Are you going over,* he said. *To sit with them.*

*Yes,* said the voice. *Not tonight.* A pause. *We made a promise on this beach. Together. In, and out. We have not come out yet. And there is a kitchen table first, with a chair with a wobbly leg, and your brother, and the painter, and a great deal that needs to be sorted out after.* Another pause, and something that might have been dry amusement. *I gave my word about the kitchen table. I find I would like to see it.*

Gin laughed. It came out of him broken in the middle.

On the far side of the beach, at the table, one of the tall still things turned its head and looked across the grey sand at Gin's body. At the two of them. For a long moment.

Then it inclined its head. Very slightly.

The voice beside Gin inclined Gin's head back.

And the fog closed round the table, softly, like a door being pulled to by somebody who did not want to wake the house.

[break Set down]

In the white, Kuze Kaname sat down on the ground.

He did not fall. He did not collapse. He sat, like a man on the step of his own house after a very long walk, with his knees up and his forearms on them and his inked hands hanging loose between. And he let out a breath that went on for a very long time.

Rei, in the chair, watched him do it.

He leaned forward. "Are you all right?"

"My legs," said Kuze, with great dignity, "have not been asked to do anything for three hundred years, and they are making their feelings known." He tipped his head back and looked up at the ring. At the thirteen lights. At the wall hanging over them all, held, steady, shining, on nobody's back and everybody's. "The ones on the other side have come back to their table. Do you feel it?"

Rei did. Far off, under everything, at the other end of the current. A stillness. A ring of very old things, sitting down, at last, where they belonged.

"They never came before," said Kuze. "Not once. Not for me." He closed his eyes. "Thirteen here, and thirteen there. Held by the ones who chose it." He was quiet for a moment. "That is how it was meant to be, I think. Before. Before anybody was frightened enough to put it all on one man."

Rei shook his head against the back of the chair. "You weren't frightened."

"No." Kuze opened one eye and looked at him. "I was stubborn. It is not the same. It is sometimes worse."

Rei laughed again. It hurt.

Then Kuze got up.

It took him two tries. He put one hand flat on the ground of the white and pushed, like an old man getting up out of a low chair, and stood, and brushed down his indigo coat with both hands, carefully, as you brush a coat before you go out somewhere that matters. He touched the inside pocket. The notebook was still there.

He looked round the white once. At the ring. At the wall. At the boy in the chair.

He settled his hands in his sleeves.

"You could go now," said Rei. "Couldn't you. Home. The road by the river. The gate. They're all holding it now. You don't have to."

Kuze looked at him for a long moment.

"There is no gate any more," he said gently. "There has not been for a very long time. The young man who stood on the step with the tea was called Shūhei. He was my brother's son. He waited for me for eleven years, and then he married, and had children, and became the head of the office, and he died at seventy-one, in the winter, in his sleep." He smiled. "I felt him go. I did not know who it was. I know now."

Rei could not say anything.

"Everyone I loved is dead, Uedera Rei. They have been dead for a very long time. If I went up that road now, I would be visiting a gate." Kuze looked up at the ring. At the thirteen lights. At the wall. "And I find I do not want to go. The world has changed so much. I would not know where to buy rice." He was quiet. "I liked my time. I would not like yours. I think I would rather stay here, where I know the work, and protect the people in it. The living and the dead both. Until I die. Whenever that is. It is not running away from the post that I want." He looked back at Rei. "It is the post. I only did not know that, until a boy came down and sat in the chair beside it, and showed me that I was allowed to choose it."

"I didn't show you anything."

"You did. You sat down." Kuze's eyes were very kind. "Twice now, you have come all this way and asked me for nothing. Nobody in three hundred years ever did that. They came to ask questions. You came to keep me company." He tilted his head. "I should like it if you did that again. Not only when the world is ending. On an ordinary day. A Tuesday."

Rei laughed, wet and broken. "A Tuesday."

"I am told they are very busy on the other side," said Kuze gravely. "I would like to hear about them."

He bowed.

Not a small bow. A real one. From the waist, slow and low and deep, the bow of a man three hundred years old to a young man in a chair. He held it. He straightened.

Then he turned round, and walked back across the white to the place under the wall where he had stood for three hundred years, and stood there again. Not facing it this time. Facing outward. Toward the ring. Toward the city. With his hands in his sleeves and his notebook in his pocket, awake, like a man standing at the door of his own house in the evening, watching the street.

The light settled round him. Not on him. Round him. Like a coat he had decided to keep.

Rei sat in the chair and watched him, until the white began to go thin and gold at the edges, and he felt himself being let go of, gently, like somebody being lowered into warm water.

Far away, very faint, up through the current, from a black beach in Kita, he heard his name.

In one voice. Rough and dry and a little too fast.

And under it, very quietly, another. Saying it carefully, as if it mattered that it was spelled right.
