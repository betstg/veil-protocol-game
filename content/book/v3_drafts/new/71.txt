#title The Freight Yards

Hara Setsu was waiting at the bottom of the road when Kato's car came down it, standing under the last working street lamp in her work apron with her sleeves rolled up and her knitting needles stuck through her bun.

She did not say hello. She opened the back door of the car and looked in at Rei.

"It iced," she said. "At four this afternoon. The corner of the women's bath, by Mount Fuji." She held up the old green notebook with the faded cloth cover. "Last year it was ten at night on the twelfth. The night your brother didn't come home." She opened the notebook at the last page and showed him, in her small upright hand, under *2015. August. Ice.*, a new line. *2016. August. Ice again. Four days late and six hours early.* "It's getting closer, child. Whatever it is. I closed the bath and came."

"Setsu-san, you can't..."

"I'm seventy-two years old and I've been keeping an eye on that corner for fifty-one of them." She took her needles out of her hair. "And on you for a year. I'm not stopping tonight because a boy in a bad tie says please."

[break North]

They went in first. Not all of them. Behind them, up the road, the cars were still arriving, Okada's field cars and Shimizu's and a taxi with Mori and Hoshino and Inoue in the back, and Chen Lian on a bicycle. *Look first,* Shimizu had said on the phone, in her flat voice that saw all the ways things could go wrong. *Count. Don't touch anything. Don't go in. Come back and tell me what's there.*

So they looked.

Rei and Hara Setsu. Majima, with the old dog on its lead. Kato, who said he could listen from further away than anyone could see, and was right. Itsuki, with a jar of iron and his sketchbook, who had said nothing at all in the car and kept his hand on the back of Rei's jacket the whole way, as if he thought Rei might be blown away. And in Rei's pocket, heavy and warm against his hip, the old dark pager from the room without a number, with its orange light beating slowly through the cloth like a second heart.

Gorō flew overhead. From lamp post to lamp post. Then from roof to roof. Then, as they came down toward the river through streets that got quieter and older and emptier with every block, he stopped flying from anywhere to anywhere and simply flew, in a slow circle, high up, against the last of the light.

The freight yard was at the end of a road that had stopped being a road.

There had been a fence. There was still a fence, in places. Chain-link, rusted, sagging, with a gate hanging off one hinge and a sign wired to it so old that the words had bleached to nothing. Beyond it, the yard opened out flat and wide toward the river, acres of it, cracked concrete and gravel and weeds as high as a man's chest, and running through all of it, in long slow curves, the old tracks. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Rusted brown, the sleepers rotten, the rails sunk into the weeds, curving away into the dusk toward the place where the yard met the river and the lines ran straight down into the water and disappeared, as if the trains that used to run on them had simply kept going, under the river, to somewhere else.

And on the tracks, the freight cars.

Old ones. Dozens. Rusted red and brown and black, doors hanging open, wheels sunk in the weeds. Some on their sides. Some stacked. Some standing in long patient lines on the curved tracks as if they were still waiting for an engine that would never come.

The old dog stopped at the gate.

It did not growl. It did not pull back. It simply stopped, and sat down, and would not move. Majima looked down at it for a long moment. Then he crouched beside it and put his hand on its back.

"Good girl," he said quietly. "You stay here. You keep watch."

The dog lay down across the gateway with its chin on its paws and its eyes on the yard.

[break What was there]

They went in as far as the first line of freight cars, and stopped behind the nearest one, and looked round the end of it.

There were people in the yard.

Not many. Not yet. Rei counted them. Thirty. Forty. Sitting on the old tracks, on the rails, on the rotten sleepers, in the weeds. Sitting in the open doors of the freight cars with their legs hanging down. Standing in ones and twos among the rusted wheels. Not talking. Not moving. Not looking at each other. Just there. Waiting, like people on a platform for a train that is late.

Each one of them held a white chrysanthemum.

Rei knew some of them, as you know faces from a dream. The woman with the handkerchief. The man in the crumpled work shirt. The university students, still holding hands. The young man in white who had knelt and set a cup in front of him in Asagaya. And others, so many others he had never seen, old and young, in work clothes and school uniforms and summer kimonos and suits, their faces calm, their eyes dark.

"More coming," said Kato, very quietly, behind him. His face was grey. "From every direction. On foot. On trains. On bikes. I can hear them. Like water running downhill. Hundreds." He swallowed. "Thousands, maybe. By midnight."

Majima was very still.

He was looking at a girl sitting in the open door of a freight car twenty metres away. Thin. Seventeen, maybe. Bleached hair growing out dark at the roots. A pink hoodie. Her feet bare and dirty. She was holding her chrysanthemum in both hands, very carefully, the way a child holds something it has been told is precious.

"Mika," said Majima. Not loud. Not to anyone. "That's Mika. From the strip. The one who didn't come back."

He did not move toward her. He stood behind the freight car with his hand inside his jacket, over his heart, and did not take it out.

[break The lights]

The sun went down behind the city.

The last of the orange went out of the sky. The meeting hour ended, as it always ended, all at once, like a door swinging shut. The yard went blue, and then grey, and then dark. There were no lamps. There had been no lamps here for thirty years.

And the lights came.

White. Small. Round. Like the paper lanterns people hang outside their houses at Obon to show the dead the way home. Dozens of them. Then hundreds. Floating at the height of a man's hand, swaying very slightly, like a lantern carried at a walk.

Nobody was carrying them.

They came from every direction. Out of the dark streets behind the yard. Along the old tracks. Up out of the weeds. Down the line of freight cars. They came drifting, slow and gentle and steady, and each one of them, Rei saw, as the nearest came past the end of the freight car close enough to touch, had something inside it. Not a candle. A face. Very small. Very faint. Looking out through the paper with the patient, puzzled, hopeful expression of someone who has come a long way home and is not quite sure this is the right street.

"Oh," said Hara Setsu, very softly, beside him. "Oh, no. Oh, the poor things."

"What are they?" said Itsuki. His voice was strange. "Rei. What are they?"

Rei could not answer at first. He knew. He knew as he had known the old woman outside the watch shop, the delivery rider on the kerb, the office girl in the Hotel Rosa. He knew in the place under his breastbone where the door was still not quite shut.

"They're the dead," he said. "Going back. Like every year. Following the lights." He watched them drift past. Hundreds. A whole city's worth of grandmothers and fathers and children and old friends, following white lights through the dark. "Every family in Tokyo lit a fire on the doorstep tonight to show them the way back. That's what the last night is. You light a fire and they follow it home to the other side." He swallowed. "She's lit false ones. Here. Hundreds of them. Brighter than the doorsteps. They're coming here instead."

"She's stealing them," said Kato. "The dead. She's calling them here instead of home."

"She's going to hold them here," said Rei. "All of them. The dead and the hollow ones together, standing in the doorway, right where the lines go into the river." He looked at the water, black now, flat and still, with the old rails running down into it. "A door full of people going out can't close. And while it can't close, everything on the other side comes in past them. At midnight. When the last fire on the last doorstep goes out."

He put his hand in his pocket on the warm pager.

*A door is always open both ways,* the screen had said.

[break What Majima said]

They went back to the gate.

The old dog was still lying across it. She got up when Majima came and pressed her head against his leg and did not let go, and he stood there for a long time with his hand on her head and his face turned back toward the yard and the white lights drifting over the weeds.

Itsuki and Kato and Hara Setsu went on ahead, up the road toward the cars and the lamps and the voices. Rei stayed.

He leaned on the bent gatepost. "The girl in the hoodie. Mika."

"Sixteen when she went to the tea," said Majima. "I bought her a hamburger once. Told her to stay off that street. She told me to go to hell." He did not look at Rei. "She was right."

Rei did not say anything.

Majima took his hand off the dog's head and put it inside his jacket, over his heart, as he did before every job. He held it there. When he took it out, there was nothing in it. There never had been, Rei realised. It was only the place he put his hand.

"I don't take cases that interest me," said Majima. "I told you that. That's why. Interesting gets you killed." He looked at the yard. "I'm taking this one."

"Majima-san..."

"Her name's Kinako," said Majima. He nodded down at the old dog. "The dog. Nobody ever asks. She's fourteen. She likes the chicken skin off a yakitori stick and she doesn't like thunder." He clicked his tongue, and the old dog got up from the gateway, stiff and slow. "If it goes wrong, somebody feeds her. That's all. Don't make a thing of it."

He walked away up the road toward the lamps with the dog at his heel.

Rei stood at the broken gate for a long time, watching the dead come to the wrong house.

Above him, in the dark, Gorō was still circling. Round and round. High up. Over the yard. Over the lights. Over the river with the rails running down into it. He did not land. He did not call. He circled, slow and black against the city's glow, like a crow keeping count of something, and Rei did not call him down.

Up the road, under the last lamp, somebody was standing very straight by a wall, a little apart from everyone else, in a grey jacket with the sleeves pushed up. Nobody else seemed to have noticed him. He was looking at the yard. When Rei looked at him, he did not look back.

Rei did not go up the road to him. He stayed at the broken gate with his hands in his pockets, and the tall man stayed by the wall, and between them the dead went on coming down the hill toward the wrong house with their small white lights.
