#title The Freight Yards

On Friday the twelfth of August, one year to the day, Hara Setsu rang Rei at a quarter past four in the afternoon and said, without hello, "It's iced."

He went straight from work. He did not change out of his suit. He ran from Ōji station all the way to Tsuru-no-yu with his tie flapping, and went in through the back door into the steam and the smell of wood smoke, and Hara Setsu was standing in the women's bath in her work apron with her sleeves rolled up and her knitting needles stuck through her bun, looking down at the corner by the painting of Mount Fuji.

The water there had a skin on it. Thin. Grey-white. Like the ice on a puddle on a January morning. In a bath at forty-four degrees, with steam coming off the rest of it, in August.

Hara Setsu did not look round.

"Last year," she said, "it was ten at night. The night your brother didn't come home." She bent down, square and solid, and touched the ice with one finger. It did not crack. "This year it's four in the afternoon. A day early. Six hours early." She straightened up. "It's getting closer, child. Whatever it is."

She took a notebook out of her apron pocket. The old green one, with the faded cloth cover. She opened it at the last page and took a pencil from behind her ear and wrote, in her small upright hand, under *2015. August. Ice.*

*2016. August. Ice again. Earlier.*

Then she underlined it twice, and closed the notebook, and put it back in her pocket.

"The old men with the dogs," she said. "By the freight yard. They stopped walking there in July. All of them. Say the dogs won't go past the gate." She looked at him with her flat, steady eyes. "I'm coming with you."

Rei opened his mouth to argue. "Setsu-san..."

"I'm seventy-two years old and I've been keeping an eye on that corner for fifty-one of them." She took her needles out of her hair. "And on you for ten months. I'm not stopping tonight because a boy in a bad tie says please."

[break North]

They went at dusk. Not all of them. A handful. *Look*, Mori had said, in the boiler room on Wednesday, with a map of the river spread out on an upturned crate and the fire roaring behind him. *Just look. Count. Don't touch anything. Don't go in. Come back and tell me what's there.*

So they looked.

Rei and Hara Setsu. Majima, with the old dog on its lead. Kato, who said he could listen from further away than anyone could see, and was right. Itsuki, with a jar of iron and his chalk and a box of matches, who had said nothing at all on the train and kept his hand on the back of Rei's jacket the whole way, as if he thought Rei might be blown away.

Gorō flew overhead. From lamp post to lamp post. Then from roof to roof. Then, as they came past Tabata and down toward the river through streets that got quieter and older and emptier with every block, he stopped flying from anywhere to anywhere and simply flew, in a slow circle, high up, against the orange sky.

The freight yard was at the end of a road that had stopped being a road.

There had been a fence. There was still a fence, in places. Chain-link, rusted, sagging, with a gate hanging off one hinge and a sign wired to it so old that the words had bleached to nothing. Beyond it, the yard opened out flat and wide toward the river, acres of it, cracked concrete and gravel and weeds as high as a man's chest, and running through all of it, in long slow curves, the old tracks. A dozen of them. Two dozen. Rusted brown, the sleepers rotten, the rails sunk into the weeds, curving away into the dusk toward the place where the yard met the river and the lines ran straight down into the water and disappeared, as if the trains that used to run on them had simply kept going, under the river, to somewhere else.

And on the tracks, the freight cars.

Old ones. Dozens. Rusted red and brown and black, doors hanging open, wheels sunk in the weeds. Some on their sides. Some stacked. Some standing in long patient lines on the curved tracks as if they were still waiting for an engine that would never come.

The old dog stopped at the gate.

It did not growl. It did not pull back. It simply stopped, and sat down, and would not move. Majima looked down at it for a long moment. Then he crouched beside it and put his hand on its back.

"Good girl," he said quietly. "You stay here. You keep watch."

The dog lay down across the gateway with its chin on its paws and its eyes on the yard.

[break What was there]

They went in as far as the first line of freight cars, and stopped behind the nearest one, and looked round the end of it.

There were people in the yard.

Not many. Not yet. Rei counted them. Thirty. Forty. Sitting on the old tracks, on the rails, on the rotten sleepers, in the weeds. Sitting in the open doors of the freight cars with their legs hanging down. Standing in ones and twos among the rusted wheels. Not talking. Not moving. Not looking at each other. Just there. Waiting. The way people wait on a platform for a train that is late.

Each one of them held a white chrysanthemum.

Rei knew some of them. He knew them the way you know faces from a dream. The woman with the handkerchief. The man in the crumpled work shirt. The university students, still holding hands. The young man in white who had knelt and set a cup in front of him in Asagaya. And others, so many others he had never seen, old and young, in work clothes and school uniforms and summer kimonos and suits, their faces calm, their eyes dark.

"More coming," said Kato, very quietly, behind him. His face was grey. "From every direction. On foot. On trains. On bikes. I can hear them. Like water running downhill. Hundreds." He swallowed. "Thousands, maybe. By tomorrow."

Majima was very still.

He was looking at a girl sitting in the open door of a freight car twenty metres away. Thin. Seventeen, maybe. Bleached hair growing out dark at the roots. A pink hoodie. Her feet bare and dirty. She was holding her chrysanthemum in both hands, very carefully, the way a child holds something it has been told is precious.

"Mika," said Majima. Not loud. Not to anyone. "That's Mika. From the strip. The one who didn't come back."

He did not move toward her. He stood behind the freight car with his hand inside his jacket, over his heart, and did not take it out.

[break The lights]

The sun went down behind the city.

The orange went out of the sky. The meeting hour ended, the way it always ended, all at once, like a door swinging shut. The yard went blue, and then grey, and then dark. There were no lamps. There had been no lamps here for thirty years.

And the lights came.

White. Small. Round. Like the paper lanterns people hang outside their houses at Obon to show the dead the way home. Dozens of them. Then hundreds. Floating at the height of a man's hand, swaying very slightly, the way a lantern sways when somebody is carrying it at a walk.

Nobody was carrying them.

They came from every direction. Out of the dark streets behind the yard. Along the old tracks. Up out of the weeds. Down the line of freight cars. They came drifting, slow and gentle and steady, and each one of them, Rei saw, as the nearest came past the end of the freight car close enough to touch, had something inside it. Not a candle. A face. Very small. Very faint. Looking out through the paper with the patient, puzzled, hopeful expression of someone who has come a long way home and is not quite sure this is the right street.

"Oh," said Hara Setsu, very softly, beside him. "Oh, no. Oh, the poor things."

"What are they?" said Itsuki. His voice was strange. "Rei. What are they?"

Rei could not answer at first. He knew. He knew the way he had known the old woman outside the watch shop, the delivery rider on the kerb, the office girl in the Hotel Rosa. He knew in the place under his breastbone where the door was still not quite shut.

"They're the dead," he said. "They're coming home for Obon. Like every year. Following the lights." He watched them drift past. Hundreds. A whole city's worth of grandmothers and fathers and children and old friends, following white lights through the dark. "Every family in Tokyo is going to light a fire on their doorstep tomorrow night to show them the way. That's what Obon is. You light a fire and they follow it home." He swallowed. "She's lit false ones. Here. Hundreds of them. Brighter than the doorsteps. Closer to the far shore than anywhere in the city."

"She's stealing them," said Kato. "The dead. She's calling them here instead of home."

"She's going to walk them across," said Rei. "Tomorrow. When it opens. The hollow ones and the dead together. All of them. Through the far shore, down where the lines go into the river." He looked at the water, black now, flat and still, with the old rails running down into it. "Not home. The other way."

[break What Majima said]

They went back to the gate.

The old dog was still lying across it. She got up when Majima came and pressed her head against his leg and did not let go, and he stood there for a long time with his hand on her head and his face turned back toward the yard and the white lights drifting over the weeds.

Itsuki and Kato and Hara Setsu went on ahead, up the road toward the streets with lamps in them. Rei stayed.

Rei leaned on the bent gatepost beside him. "Mika. The girl in the hoodie."

"Sixteen when she went to the tea," said Majima. "I bought her a hamburger once. Told her to stay off that street. She told me to go to hell." He did not look at Rei. "She was right. I should have gone to hell. It would have been closer to where she was going."

Rei did not say anything.

Majima took his hand off the dog's head. He reached inside his jacket. He took out the thing he kept there, the thing he touched before every job, over his heart. He held it out.

It was a photograph. Small. The size of a train ticket. Soft and creased at the corners from being handled. A school photograph, the kind every child in Japan has taken once a year against a blue background. A girl of about twelve, in a navy school uniform, with her hair in two short plaits and a serious face, not smiling, looking straight at the camera as if she were trying very hard to remember something.

Majima did not let go of it at once. "My daughter."

Rei looked at the photograph. Then at Majima.

"I've never met her," said Majima. His closed-door face did not change at all. Only his voice, very slightly, at the very bottom. "Her mother left before she was born. Took her north. I knew where. I always knew where. I sent money. Every month. Never a letter. Never went." He looked at the photograph in Rei's hand. "Her mother sends me one of those once a year. School photo. Every spring. No letter. Just that." A pause. "She's fifteen now. That one's three years old. The new one's in my flat. I didn't bring it. I wanted to keep this one. She looks like she's thinking about something in this one."

Rei held the photograph by its edges, very carefully. "Why are you showing me?"

Majima took the photograph back. He looked at it for a long time in the dark.

"Because I've been thinking about going," he said. "Up north. To meet her. For two years I've been thinking about it. Every spring when the photo comes. And every spring I think, not yet. Not with this job. Not with what I do. Not while there's something in this city that could follow me there." He put the photograph back inside his jacket, over his heart, and left his hand there. "I don't take cases that interest me. I told you that. That's why. Interesting gets you killed. And then there's nobody to send the money."

He looked back at the yard. At the white lights. At the girl in the pink hoodie, somewhere out there among them, holding her flower.

His hand stayed flat over the pocket. "I'm taking this one."

Rei took a step toward him. "Majima-san..."

"There's an address on the back of the photo." He did not look at Rei. "In case. You don't tell her what I was. You tell her her father was a man who held up ceilings." He clicked his tongue, and the old dog got up from the gateway, stiff and slow. "That's all. Don't make a thing of it."

He walked away up the road toward the lamps with the dog at his heel.

Rei stood at the broken gate for a long time, watching the dead come home to the wrong house.

Above him, in the dark, Gorō was still circling. Round and round. High up. Over the yard. Over the lights. Over the river with the rails running down into it. He did not land. He did not call. He circled, slow and black against the city's glow, like a crow keeping count of something, and Rei did not call him down.

At midnight, he took out the Capturer. He did not press anything. He held it in both hands and said the name.

"Yushiro."

The light came on.

*i know,* said the screen. *i have seen it. i have been watching the lights come in since the sun went down.*

*tomorrow.*

*eat something tonight. sleep in a bed. he says so. i say so. we are agreed on very little. we are agreed on that.*
