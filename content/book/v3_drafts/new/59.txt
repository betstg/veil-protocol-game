#title Kanna-sensei

Yua stopped answering her phones on the eighth of July.

All three of them. The pink one she used for her friends, the black one she used for work she would not talk about, and the cracked white one with the sticker of a cartoon cat that she said was only for emergencies and only Rei and Itsuki had the number. For five days they rang and rang and went to the same recorded voice, and then on the thirteenth the pink one and the black one stopped ringing at all, and the white one rang once more and was answered.

Nobody spoke. Rei stood in the corridor of the fourth floor of the Division with the phone pressed to his ear and listened to someone breathing on the other end. Quiet breathing. Calm. And very faint, behind it, a sound he knew from Gin's book, from a drawing glued in the back, from a long white room he had seen once through the gap in a blind.

The clink of a teacup set down on a saucer.

Then the line went dead.

[break The flyer]

Itsuki found the flyer. He found it on the noticeboard of a community centre in Kōenji, where he had gone to ask about renting the hall for a show, pinned between a yoga class and a lost tortoise. It was printed on soft cream paper in a gentle round font, with a drawing of a single white chrysanthemum at the top.

*Higan-kai. The Far-Shore Circle. A gathering for those who are carrying a loss. You do not have to speak. You do not have to be strong. Tea will be served. Sundays, 4 pm.*

There was an address in Asagaya. A residential street. No phone number.

Itsuki put it on the kitchen table in front of Rei on Saturday night and sat down across from him and did not let go of it.

"No," he said, and kept two fingers on the corner of the flyer. "I know what you're going to say, and no."

Rei looked at the chrysanthemum. "She's in there."

Itsuki did not look down at it. "You don't know that."

"I heard the cup." Rei pulled the flyer, gently, and Itsuki did not let go. "I heard her breathing and I heard the cup. She went in, Itsu. She went in on her own because of the flat in Nakano, because they wrote my name on that note, because she thinks she owes me something. She's sixteen. She's sixteen and she's sitting in a white room drinking that woman's tea."

Itsuki's jaw worked. He looked at the flyer. He looked at Gorō on the back of Gin's chair, who looked back at him with the blue eye as if he had an opinion and was keeping it to himself.

"Your brother went in there," he said finally, very low. "In July. Last July. And he came out wearing a stranger."

Rei held his eyes. "I know."

Itsuki pressed the flyer flat with his whole hand. "So *no*."

Rei let go of the flyer. He put both hands flat on the table, the way he had at the counter of Kita Residential Services, so they would not do anything.

"I promised you I'd tell you first," he said. "Every time. I'm telling you first."

Itsuki stared at him. Then he put his face in his hands and swore into them, long and low and filthy, every word he knew. When he took his hands away, his eyes were wet and furious.

"Kato drives," he said. "Kato sits outside in the car the whole time and listens to you. If he can't hear you for one minute, *one minute*, Rei, we come in. Me and him and the bird. I don't care what she is. I'll open a gate in her front room and drop the whole house in the river."

Rei looked at him for a long time. "Okay."

Itsuki pointed at the jar of filings on the shelf by the door. "And you take the iron."

Rei nodded at the jar. "Okay."

Itsuki leaned across the table until their foreheads nearly touched. "And you don't drink the tea."

Rei did not say okay to that. Itsuki noticed. He did not say anything about it either.

[break Asagaya]

The house was at the end of a narrow street of old wooden houses and newer concrete ones, ten minutes from the station, past a shrine and a public bath and a bakery that was closing for the day. It was not the white house. Rei had expected the white house, with its square hedge and its blinds. This was smaller. Two storeys, painted a soft pale grey, with a little front garden full of white flowers. Chrysanthemums. Lilies. Something with tiny white stars that smelled of honey. A wooden sign hung by the gate, hand-lettered. *Higan-kai.* Under it, smaller, *Please come in. The door is open.*

Kato parked the car round the corner, by the shrine, with the engine off and the windows down. He sat with his hands on the wheel and his king in his fist.

"I can hear you," he said, without looking at Rei. "Right now. Very loud. You're scared."

Rei opened the car door. "Yes."

"Good." Kato turned the king over once in his fingers. "Stay scared. Scared is loud." He swallowed. "Think at me. The whole time. Anything. Count. Recite train stations. I don't care. Just don't stop."

Rei got out of the car.

He walked up the street with the latch shut. Not all the way. Three quarters. Enough to keep the house from reading everything in him, and not so much that he looked like what he was, a Division trainee with a pocket full of iron coming to fetch somebody back. He wore his own clothes, an old thin grey sweatshirt of Gin's with the sleeves too long. He had not slept properly in a week. He did not have to act very much.

*Ōji,* he thought, at the car behind him. *Kami-Nakazato. Tabata. Nishi-Nippori.*

The door was open, as the sign said.

[break The circle]

Inside, it was white.

Not the white of the Font. A human white. Paper walls and pale wood floors and soft white cushions, and white flowers in white vases, and white curtains moving a little at the open windows. It was very clean and very quiet and smelled of tea and lilies, and the moment Rei stepped over the threshold, something in his chest that had been clenched for eleven months let go, just a little, like a fist uncurling in sleep.

He felt it go, and he was afraid.

*Nishi-Nippori,* he thought, very hard. *Nippori. Uguisudani. Ueno.*

There was a room to the left, long and low, with cushions in a circle on the floor. Eleven people were sitting on them. Old and young. A woman in her sixties with her hands folded round a handkerchief. A man about Gin's age in a crumpled work shirt, staring at nothing. Two university students, a boy and a girl, holding hands. A very thin old man in a wheelchair, who was crying quietly and did not seem to know it.

And at the far end of the circle, with her back to the window, a girl of sixteen with buzzed sides and her hair grown out on top, in a white cotton shirt that was not hers, with a teacup held in both hands.

Yua looked up when he came in.

Her eyes went straight through him. Polite. Empty. The way you look at a stranger who has come into a waiting room.

Then she looked down at her tea.

Rei sat on the nearest cushion before his legs could decide anything else.

The wall behind the circle was covered in photographs.

Hundreds of them. Pinned edge to edge from floor to ceiling, so that the whole wall was faces. Old photographs, black and white, curling at the corners. School photographs. Wedding photographs. Holiday snaps, a man on a beach squinting into the sun, a child on a tricycle, an old woman blowing out candles. Under each one, on a little white card, a name and a date in the same neat hand. Some of the faces were dead. Rei could tell, looking at them, which ones were dead. Some of them were not. Some of them were only missing.

He did not look for it. He did not want to look for it.

He found it anyway. On the left, at eye level, where you would see it from the cushion nearest the door. A photograph he knew, because he had taken it. On the walkway outside their flat, three summers ago, Gin squinting at the camera with a plastic bag of shopping in each hand, about to say *stop it*.

*Uedera Gin. August.*

That was all the card said.

*Ochanomizu,* Rei thought, at nothing, at the car round the corner, at anybody. *Ochanomizu. Ochanomizu. Ochanomizu.*

"You came," said a woman's voice, very softly, behind him. "I'm so glad."

[break Sensei]

She was not frightening. That was the first thing, and it was the worst.

She was a woman of about forty, not tall, in a soft oatmeal-coloured dress with long sleeves and her feet bare on the pale floor. Her black hair was tied back simply, with a few strands of grey left in at the temples. Her face was kind. Ordinary and kind, the face of a primary school teacher or a nurse on a children's ward, someone you would hand your crying baby to without thinking. Round her neck, on a thin cord, she wore a small smooth river stone, grey with a white band through it.

Her eyes were dark. They held his a moment too long. And in that moment, he felt that she understood everything about him. Every night on the sofa with his foot hanging off the end. Every morning looking at Gin's chair. Every *I'm fine* to Itsuki. All of it. Understood and forgiven and set gently down, the way you set down something heavy that someone has been carrying too far.

"Everyone calls me Kanna," she said, and smiled, and knelt on the cushion beside him so that their knees almost touched. "Kanna-sensei, sometimes, but I'm not anybody's teacher. I just make the tea." She tilted her head toward the wall of photographs, toward the one by the door. "He's a beautiful man. Your brother. He sat right where you're sitting. Last summer."

Rei could not speak.

"You don't have to speak," said Kanna-sensei, as if he had said it aloud. "Nobody has to speak here. That's the only rule." She reached out and put her hand on his, on the back of his hand, where it lay on his knee. Her hand was warm and dry and light. "You've been carrying so much. For so long. Haven't you. All by yourself."

*Okachimachi,* Rei thought. *Okachimachi. Akihabara.*

But the stations were going thin. Like a radio losing a signal in a tunnel. He could feel it happening. He could feel the house folding round him, white and soft and quiet, the way the black thing in the Hotel Rosa had folded round him, except that this was not hungry, this was not grabbing, this was only kind. This only wanted him to put it all down.

"You don't have to," she said. "Not today. But you could. Put it down. Just for an hour. And have some tea."

A young man in white came and knelt and set a cup in front of him. White porcelain. Pale green tea. Steam curling off it. It smelled like the first morning of a holiday.

Across the circle, Yua lifted her own cup to her mouth and did not drink. Her eyes, over the rim, met his for half a second. Not empty. Not empty at all.

*Don't.*

Then they went polite again, and slid away.

[break The fist]

He did not know, afterwards, what would have happened if he had been alone.

He thought about it a lot. Lying on the sofa at night. He thought he would probably have drunk it. Not because he believed her. Because he was so tired, and the room was so quiet, and her hand on his was so warm, and nobody had held his hand like that since he was small. He thought that the part of him that had stood in the Hotel Rosa only days before and felt a black thing taste the hollow where Gin should be and thought *yes* would have picked up that cup and drunk it to the bottom.

But he was not alone.

Under the cushion, under the floor, out in the street and round the corner by the shrine, a voice was shouting. Not out loud. Inside his head. Very far away, like someone shouting up a well.

*REI. REI. ANSWER ME. I CAN'T HEAR YOU. I CAN'T HEAR YOU. ITSUKI'S GOT THE GATE OPEN. HE'S GOT IT OPEN IN THE CAR. HE'S GOING TO DO IT. ANSWER ME.*

Kato.

And Rei thought, very clearly, the only thing that came into his head.

*Second cup.*

Not a station. Tanizaki's front room. The newspaper turning. The tea going cold in the cup beside his knee. Two cups and an hour and nobody saying anything, every Saturday since the autumn, and an old man who never once asked him to put anything down. Who only poured, and waited, and let him carry it, because it was his to carry. *Come back when you have finished being in a hurry.*

He took his hand out from under hers.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done. Harder than the Hotel Rosa. Harder than the chair. It was like pulling his hand out of warm water on a winter morning. Everything in him wanted to leave it there.

He took it out, and closed it into a fist on his knee, and with his other hand he pushed the cup of tea, very gently, back across the floor toward her. It did not spill.

"No, thank you," he said. His voice was hoarse. "I've already got someone who makes me tea."

[break What she was]

Kanna-sensei looked at the cup.

For a long moment, nothing in her face changed. She was still kind. Still soft. Still the woman you would hand your baby to. Round the circle, nobody moved. The old man in the wheelchair went on crying. The university students went on holding hands.

Then she looked up at him, and smiled, and it was the same smile, exactly the same, and her eyes were black.

Not dark. Black. All of them. Whites and all. The way Rei had seen them once through the gap of a blind, across a dark garden, eight months ago. Two holes in a kind face, and nothing at all behind them. No one. A room with the lights put out one by one until there was nobody left to switch them on.

*There you are,* said something, from very far down inside the woman, in a voice like the bottom of a lake.

The latch on Rei's wrist went so cold it burned.

"I knew you'd come," said Kanna-sensei, in her own soft voice, the black eyes smiling. "Of course you'd come. You're just like him. He wouldn't drink it either." She picked up the cup he had pushed away, and turned it in her hands, and looked into it. "He sat right there and listened to me for an hour. Do you know what he said, at the end? He said, *thank you, but I've got a little brother at home and he can't cook*." She laughed. A small, delighted laugh. "And then he went out of that door, and something very old that I owe a great deal was waiting for him on the step."

Rei's hand closed on his knee. "Where is he." It was not a question.

"Closer than you think." Kanna-sensei set the cup down. "Further than you can reach. You know that. You've been told." She leaned toward him, very slightly, and the river stone swung on its cord. "But you could reach him, Rei. If you wanted. I could give him back to you. All of him. His own face. His own voice. Sitting at your kitchen table, complaining about the rice cooker. That's not a trick. I can do it. The thing that has him owes me, and I owe it, and debts can be called in." She smiled. "All you'd have to do is sit in a chair."

The room was very quiet.

Rei felt his own left palm go warm, as if it remembered. "Which chair."

Kanna-sensei's black eyes did not blink.

"The one you touched," she said gently. "Two weeks ago. When you lost a day. I felt it from here." She put her head on one side. "It's warm now. Isn't it. Where your hand was."

[break Out]

He did not remember standing up.

He was standing. He was by the door, by the photograph of Gin with the shopping bags, and he did not know how he had got there. His nose was bleeding. The iron filings were in his fist, in his jacket pocket, so tight that they had cut his palm.

He held his free hand out across the room to her. "Yua. Come on. We're going."

Yua did not move. She sat on her cushion with her cup in her hands and looked at him with polite, empty eyes, like a stranger.

"I'm sorry," she said, in a light, pleasant voice that was not her voice at all. "I think you've got me mixed up with someone."

He took one step toward the circle, and every head in it turned to him at once. "*Yua.*"

"She's welcome to go whenever she likes," said Kanna-sensei, still kneeling, not getting up, the cup in her lap. "Anyone can. The door's always open. It says so on the sign." She smiled at Yua. Yua smiled back. "She doesn't want to. Do you, sweetheart. She's found people here who see her. She's never had that. Nobody's ever looked at her properly in her whole life. Have they." The black eyes slid back to Rei. "Not even you."

Yua's smile did not change. But the hand round her teacup, the left one, the one nearest the wall, moved. Just the fingers. Just the little finger. It tapped the side of the cup twice, and then once, and then twice again.

Quick-quick. Slow. Quick-quick.

He did not know what it meant. He knew that it meant something. He knew that it was her.

"Next Sunday," said Kanna-sensei. "Four o'clock. Come back. Bring the boy who paints doors, if you like. There's a cushion for him too." She lifted her cup to him, a small toast. "You'll come back. You're the kind who comes back. That's what's so lovely about you."

Rei went out of the door.

The garden was full of white flowers. The air was hot and wet and smelled of honey. He walked down the path and through the gate and along the narrow street past the bakery and the bath and the shrine, and he did not run, because she would have liked that, and he would not give her anything she liked.

Round the corner, the car was there. The back door was open. There was a hole in the air over the back seat, round and dark and shining at the edges like wet ink, and Itsuki was kneeling on the seat with both hands in it up to the wrists and his face grey with effort and blood running from both nostrils. Gorō was on the roof of the car with his wings half open, screaming.

Kato was out of the driver's seat, standing in the road with his king clenched in his fist so hard his knuckles were white, and when he saw Rei come round the corner he made a sound like a man who has been under water too long.

"I've got you," he said. "I've got you. I can hear you. I've got you." His face crumpled. "Itsuki, *stop*. Close it. Close it, he's here, he's here."

Itsuki pulled his hands out of the dark. The hole closed with a sound like a door shutting in another flat. He fell back against the seat and lay there, breathing.

Rei leaned on the car. His legs would not hold him up any more.

"She's in there," he said. "She's in there and she won't come out. She said she's not her."

Itsuki opened his eyes. "Then she's lying."

"I know." Rei looked down at his own hand, the right one, still in a fist, the iron filings and the blood. "She tapped the cup. On the side. Like this." He tapped the car roof, quick-quick, slow, quick-quick. "I don't know what it means."

Kato went very still.

"I do," he said. He was looking at Rei's hand. "That's not a code. That's a heartbeat." He swallowed. "She's counting. She's counting her own heartbeat so she doesn't fall asleep in there." His voice cracked. "She's keeping herself awake, Rei. On purpose. She's holding on."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Gorō walked down the windscreen of the car, and down the bonnet, and dropped onto Rei's shoulder, and pushed his black head hard against Rei's ear.
