#title The Snake at the Door

The Grid's message point that week was a launderette in Akabane, and Switch was waiting in the doorway of it at two in the morning, with the rain coming down outside.

It was the first proper rain of the season. The tsuyu, the rainy season, comes to Tokyo every year in the middle of June and stays for six weeks, and for six weeks nothing dries. Washing hangs on the balconies for days. Umbrellas collect by every door like flocks of tired birds. The launderette was the only bright thing on the street, a long narrow room of white machines under strip lights, and in it an old woman in a pink apron was asleep on a plastic chair with her mouth open while three dryers went round.

Rei had walked from Jūjō. Thirty minutes, under a clear plastic umbrella from the konbini, along the dark side of the railway. He was soaked to the knees.

"It's not the Circle," said Switch. That was the first thing they said. They did not say hello. Switch never did. "Different people. Different thing. Smaller. Newer." Their square face did not move under the strip light. "It calls itself the Hall of the Bright Sky. A man in Itabashi. Rents a room above a pachinko parlour by Ōyama station. Started a year ago with five people. Now there's forty."

"And?"

"Nine of them haven't been seen outside in a month. Their families come to the door. They're told their children are *in purification*. In the basement."

Rei looked at the old woman asleep on her chair. One of the dryers stopped. She did not wake up.

"Why me?" he said. "Why not the Division?"

"The Division went," said Switch. "In April. Two officers. They came out smiling and filed a report that said *legitimate religious organisation, no veil activity*." They looked at him. "One of them was from your floor. Hamada. You might know him to nod to. He's in Reintegration now. He still smiles. He hasn't stopped." They took a folded piece of paper out of their jacket and held it out. "That's the address. Saturday, eight o'clock, they have a meeting anyone can come to. Hana's going. She wants somebody with her who can see what's on the stage."

Rei took the paper. He put it inside his jacket, where it would stay dry.

"I'm Division now," he said. "You know that."

"I know," said Switch. "So was your brother. He came anyway."

[break Ōyama]

He rang Shimizu on Friday night.

He did not have to. He knew that. The Grid was the Grid and the Division was the Division, and what he did on his own time with people who did not have lanyards was his own business, as long as nobody wrote it down. Gin had worked that way for six years.

He rang her anyway.

She listened to all of it without saying anything. Then there was a long silence on the line, and he could hear a television somewhere behind her, and a kettle.

"I didn't hear this," said Shimizu. "I was asleep. My phone was off." A pause. "Take iron. Take the Capturer. If anything goes wrong, you ring Kato before you ring me, because he'll know before I pick up." Another pause. "And Uedera. Thank you for ringing."

On Saturday evening he went out to Itabashi with Itsuki.

They took the Tōbu Tōjō line from Ikebukuro, which is a slow, local, ordinary line that runs out north-west through the suburbs toward Saitama and that nobody in Tokyo ever takes on purpose. The train was full of people going home with shopping. A man across from them was asleep with a bag of leeks on his knees. Two girls in matching school tracksuits were sharing earphones and taking it in turns to be the one who sang along.

Ōyama station had a level crossing right outside it, with bells that rang every three minutes, and beyond the crossing a long covered shopping arcade called Happy Road, which had been called Happy Road since 1978 and had a little plastic sign at the entrance that said so. It was full of the smell of fried chicken and tea and wet umbrellas. There was a shop that only sold socks, and a shop that only sold rice crackers, and a shop that only sold things for one hundred yen, and an old man with a cart selling sweet potatoes roasted on hot stones, even in June, because he always had.

Hana was waiting under the arcade by the sweet potato man, in a white shirt that was too big for her, with her hood up over it.

She looked at Itsuki. Then at Rei.

"He's not coming in," she said.

"I'm not coming in," said Itsuki. "I'm sitting in the alley. With these." He held up a sketchbook and a fistful of pencils. "I've already started. I did the hall on the train."

He opened the sketchbook and showed her.

The first page was a big room with a low stage and a ceiling painted with clouds. The second page was a staircase going down into the dark. The third was a row of nine mats on a concrete floor, very roughly, and across a narrow doorway at the top of a stair, something long and coiled, drawn three times, darker each time, as if his hand had gone back to it without being asked.

Underneath, in his own small handwriting, it said *don't open up near the door. i don't know why. it's in the drawing.*

Hana looked at it for a long time.

"Fine," she said. "Alley." She held out a second white shirt to Rei, folded, smelling of somebody else's washing powder. "Put that on. They like you in white."

[break The hall]

[need ch50_bright_sky | A shabby upstairs hall above a neon pachinko parlour in Itabashi at night, rows of followers in white kneeling on cheap carpet facing a stage where a smiling middle-aged man in white stands under a painted blue sky on the ceiling, a narrow blue door at the back with a brown snake coiled on dark grave earth across its threshold]

The Hall of the Bright Sky was on the second floor of a building in Itabashi, above a pachinko parlour so loud that the floor shook. The noise of ten thousand steel balls came up through the floorboards like rain on a tin roof, and the stairwell smelled of cigarettes and the cheap air freshener they sprayed downstairs every hour.

The hall itself was one big room with cheap blue carpet and folding chairs pushed against the walls. Someone had painted the whole ceiling sky blue, with white clouds, badly. At the front was a low stage. On the stage stood a man.

He was about fifty. Ordinary. A little heavy, with thinning hair and a round, pleasant, smiling face, in a white robe like a bathrobe tied at the waist. He was talking. Forty people in white clothes knelt on the blue carpet in front of him, in rows, looking up at him with their faces shining.

"The sky," he was saying. "Look up. Look up at the sky. Isn't it beautiful? Isn't it the most beautiful thing you've ever seen?"

Forty faces looked up at the badly painted ceiling. Forty mouths opened. Forty pairs of eyes filled with tears.

Rei, at the back of the room in the borrowed white shirt, kneeling beside Hana, lifted the latch on his wrist a finger's width.

He felt it at once. A great wash of something, coming off the man on the stage like heat off a fire. Not fear. Not hunger. Something huge and bright and overwhelming. The feeling you have standing at the edge of a cliff over the sea. Looking at the stars on a clear night in the mountains. Seeing something so big and so beautiful that your mind cannot hold it, and you forget your own name.

Rei did not have a better word for it than *awe*. He would not have known that word for it at all, a year ago.

And the man on the stage was pouring it out. Into them. Forty people kneeling on blue carpet, filled to the brim with awe at a painted ceiling, so full of it that there was no room left in them for anything else. Not their families. Not their jobs. Not food. Not sleep. Not the nine people in the basement. Only the sky.

The man smiled. His pupils were huge. Black. Wet. Shining. Taking in all forty of them at once, like a farmer looking at a field.

"*Something's riding him,*" whispered Hana, beside Rei. She had her sleeves pulled down over her hands. Her eyes were narrow and fast and moving round the room. "*It eats that. Whatever that is. It's not even hiding. It doesn't need to. They'd die for it. Some of them already have.*"

Rei looked at her.

"*There,*" said Hana. "*Back of the room. The door.*"

[break The snake]

At the back of the hall, behind the last row of kneeling people, there was a narrow door. Plain, wooden, painted the same blue as the ceiling. It led down. Rei could feel it. Stairs going down to a basement under the pachinko parlour, under the noise. Nine people down there. Nine small, faint, flickering lights, very weak, very cold.

The sill of the door was dusted with dark earth. Grave earth. And lying across the threshold, coiled on the earth, was a snake.

It was an ordinary snake. An aodaishō, long and brown and olive, as thick as Rei's wrist, the kind that lives in old farmhouses and eats mice. It lay with its head raised a little off its coils and its tongue flickering, and it was not looking at any of them. It was watching the empty air in the doorway, very closely, as if the empty air were the most interesting thing in Tokyo.

Rei knew what it was. He had read about it in Gin's book. *Set a snake at the threshold. It bites what it cannot see.* A snake that feels the veil. Laid on grave earth across a door, it lets ordinary people walk past. A man in socks. A woman with a tray. It does not care about them. It strikes at anything that crosses which the eye cannot see. A spirit. A thing from the far side. A practitioner with the door in his chest open, pushing his sight ahead of him into the dark.

The thing on the stage had not put it there to stop the police. It had put it there to stop people like him.

"*So that's what he drew,*" breathed Hana, beside him. "*If I jump past that, it'll have my leg. Anything that goes through the veil, it bites.*" She looked at Rei. "*And you've got your door open. I can feel it from here. You walk past that thing like that and it'll bite you in the leg, and whatever's on the stage will feel it like a bell.*"

Rei looked at the snake. At its small black ordinary eyes, watching the air.

"Then I'll close it," he said.

[break Blind]

On the stage, the man raised his arms.

"Look up!" he cried. "Look up at the sky!"

Forty people looked up. Forty people wept.

Rei put his right hand on his left wrist and dropped the latch.

Not to a finger's width. All the way. Shut. The door in his chest swung closed and the iron bar fell into the hook, and for the first time since August he was not open to the veil at all. Not even a crack.

It was like going deaf. Like going blind. The hall went flat and ordinary and grey. The great bright wash of awe from the stage was gone. The nine faint lights in the basement were gone. He could not feel Hana beside him or Itsuki behind him or the thing on the stage. He was a boy in a borrowed white shirt in a shabby room above a pachinko parlour, and that was all he was, and he did not know where anything was.

He had not realised how much he had come to rely on it. How frightening the world was without it.

Hana walked first. She went quietly, with her shoulders down, a girl in white going to the toilet. She stepped over the sill. The snake did not move. Its tongue flickered at the air beside her ankle and found nothing it cared about.

Rei followed.

He did not look at the snake. He looked at the back of Hana's head. He kept the latch shut so hard that his wrist ached. He walked like a man walking on ice, one foot, then the other, and he felt his heel come down on the grave earth on the sill, soft and gritty, and he did not breathe.

Something brushed his ankle. Cool and dry. The snake's tongue.

He kept walking. He was through. He was on the stairs. The blue door was behind him.

Behind him, in the hall, the snake lowered its head onto its coils and went on watching the empty air.

[break The basement]

The stairs went down into concrete and cold. At the bottom there was one bare bulb. The noise of the pachinko parlour was very loud down here, pounding through the ceiling. On the floor, in a row, on thin white mats, lay nine people.

Rei lifted the latch.

It came back all at once, like hearing after a loud noise. The cold. The thin place under the floor, worn through, like a hole in a sock. And the nine. Eight small, weak, flickering lights. And one that was not flickering any more.

They were thin. Very thin. Weeks thin. They wore white clothes gone grey with dirt. They lay on their backs with their eyes open, looking up at the concrete ceiling, at nothing. All of them were smiling. Soft, sweet, peaceful smiles. Awe. The demon had filled them with awe and then left them down here in the dark, over the thin place, to be drunk from, slowly, a little more every night.

Hana knelt beside the one who was not breathing. A young man, about twenty, with long hair. She put two fingers on his neck. She stayed there a long time. Then she took her fingers away and pulled her sleeve down over her hand and closed his eyes with it. She did not say anything. Her face did not change.

"Eight," she said. "Get them up."

"How many can you carry?" said Rei.

Hana looked at the high window at the top of the wall, at street level, with bars on it and the wet black alley behind. She looked at the eight.

"One at a time," she said. "Out there. It's twenty metres." She pushed her sleeves up. "Eight times twenty metres. I've never done eight." She took a breath. "Hold them up for me. Sitting. I need to see their faces."

Rei got the first one sitting up, a girl of about seventeen with her hair cut short, her head lolling on his shoulder. Hana put her hand on the girl's wrist and closed her eyes.

The girl was gone. The weight left Rei's arm all at once. Through the bars of the window he heard a small wet sound in the alley, and Itsuki's voice, quite close, saying "I've got her, I've got her," and the scrape of a sketchbook being dropped on wet concrete.

Hana opened her eyes. "Next."

They did it eight times. Rei held them up one by one, the girl and the two brothers and the old woman with the bad hip and the boy who kept smiling, and Hana took each one by the wrist and sent them through the veil and out into the rain, where Itsuki caught them and sat them against the wall under the noodle shop's overhang and put his jacket over the first one and then had nothing else to put over the others. By the fifth Hana was grey. By the sixth there was blood coming out of her nose, dripping off her chin onto her white shirt, and she did not wipe it. By the eighth she was holding the wall.

"Now you," said Rei.

"Can't." She laughed. It came out thin. "Not two more. Not you and me. You go up the stairs. Past the snake. Latch shut. Just walk."

"I'm not leaving you down here."

"Then we both walk," said Hana, and wiped her nose at last, on her sleeve, and looked at the blood on it with something like interest. "Give me a minute."

[break The sky]

Upstairs, the man on the stage was screaming. Rei heard it through the ceiling, over the pachinko noise.

Not in his own voice. In the demon's. A high, wild, joyful shriek, like a child on a rollercoaster. It had felt eight of its herd go out of its reach, one after another, through the veil, like teeth being pulled. And it did what demons do. It did not think. It wanted, and it moved.

It wanted Rei.

It came down the stairs. Rei heard the blue door bang open and the man's heavy body come down the concrete steps three at a time, past the snake, which did not care about a man in a white robe. It came out of the stairwell into the basement in one leap, white robe flying, arms out, mouth open. Rei saw it coming. He saw the black eyes. He felt the awe pour out of it toward him like a wave, huge and bright and overwhelming, *look up, look up, look at the sky, forget everything, forget your brother, forget your name*.

He put his hand on his wrist.

He did not lift the latch. He held it. He felt the wave hit the closed door in his chest and break on it, like the sea on a wall.

And Gorō came through the window.

Between the bars, from the wet alley, folding his wings flat at the last moment, a big black and white crow with one pale blue eye came in at full speed, low over the mats, and hit the man in the face with both feet and his heavy beak, screaming *kaa kaa kaa* like an alarm. The man fell. He hit the concrete hard, on his back, with the crow on his chest, pecking.

Hana came off the wall. She had a handful of iron filings in her pocket, as Grid people always do. She pressed them flat against the man's forehead with her palm and held them there.

The demon came out of him like steam out of a kettle. A shriek. A rush of something bright and enormous and empty, going up, up, through the concrete and the pachinko parlour and the badly painted sky in the hall above, and away.

The man lay on the concrete with a crow on his chest and iron on his forehead and looked up at the bare bulb. His eyes were small and brown and ordinary.

"Oh," he said, in a thin, ordinary, frightened voice. "Oh, no. What have I done. What have I done."

[break After]

The Hall of the Bright Sky ended that night. Not with the Division. With forty people in white clothes standing in a shabby room above a pachinko parlour, looking at each other, looking out of the windows at the eight thin people sitting in the wet alley below, looking at the young man with long hair lying very still on a mat by the stage door. Waking up.

Some of them cried. Some of them walked out without a word and went home. Some of them stayed, and sat with the eight, and held their hands, and called ambulances, and called families.

The man in the white robe sat on the basement stairs with his head in his hands until the police came. He did not try to run. He kept saying the same thing. *I only wanted to show them something beautiful. I only wanted to show them something beautiful.*

The snake was still coiled on its grave earth across the blue door, guarding nothing now. Hana put it in a cardboard box to take to someone in the Grid who kept animals. "It did its job," she said. "It's not its fault the job was stupid."

Outside, in the rain, Hana sat on the kerb with a towel against her nose. Itsuki sat beside her with his wet sketchbook open on his knees. The fourth drawing was on the page, and he had not shown it to anyone. It was Hana, sitting on a kerb, with a towel.

"You drew this before," said Hana, looking at it. "Before we went in."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell me."

"You'd have done it anyway," said Itsuki. "And then you'd have been cross with me as well."

Hana looked at the drawing for a long time. Then she took the cigarette out of her mouth.

"You're good," she said. "For a Kuroda."

"Thank you," said Itsuki. "That's the nicest thing a Grid person has ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

Rei sat on the kerb on Hana's other side. Gorō landed on his shoulder, wet and furious, with one tail feather bent sideways and a smear of the man's blood on his beak, and pressed his head against Rei's ear.

"I know," said Rei. "Thank you."

At the end of the alley, under the arcade, the sweet potato man was packing up his cart. He looked down the alley at the three of them on the kerb, and the eight thin people wrapped in blankets against the wall, and the ambulance lights turning on the wet concrete. Then he took three sweet potatoes out of the warm stones, wrapped them in newspaper, walked down the alley in the rain, and put them on the kerb beside Itsuki without a word, and went back to his cart.

Itsuki unwrapped one and broke it in three. It steamed in the rain. They ate it sitting on the kerb with their hands burning, and nobody said anything, until the ambulances had gone.
