#title Katagiri

Katagiri Noa lived on the floor above an old veterinary clinic in Kita-ku, with more cats than the lease allowed.

There were four of them that summer. A one-eyed tabby someone had left in a box outside the clinic. Two black sisters who had come in through the bathroom window during a typhoon and declined to leave. And a very old white cat called Mugi who had belonged to the clinic's last owner and had simply walked upstairs when the old man died, and lain down on Katagiri's bed, and stayed. They slept where they liked. They ate when she ate. They did not distinguish between her flat and the Cattery and neither did she.

In the spare room there was a pottery wheel and shelves of things she had made on it. Three years of bowls and cups and little dishes. None of them were good. She knew that. She did not care. She liked the feel of the clay going round under her hands, wet and alive, and the moment when it stood up out of nothing and became a shape. She liked that you could not rush it.

On the kitchen counter there were eleven jars of plum jam, labelled in her small square hand with the names of the people she was going to give them to.

One of the labels said *Gin*.

She had made it last June. It had been on the counter for a year. She dusted it on Sundays.

[break Sunday]

She had gone to the circle in Asagaya for the first time on the fifth of July.

She told herself it was work. That was what she told herself on the train, standing by the door with her bag held in front of her in both hands. Rei had come to her desk and said the name Kanna-sensei and the word *chair*, and she was a senior investigator with sixteen years in the field and twelve years of case files, and somebody ought to go and look. Somebody careful. Somebody who knew what they were looking at.

She knew what she was looking at the moment she stepped through the door.

White room. Cushions. Lilies. A wall of faces. She saw his photograph before she saw anything else, at eye level, by the door, where you could not miss it. Gin on a walkway in summer with a bag of shopping in each hand, squinting, about to tell somebody to stop it.

She sat down on the nearest cushion and did not get up for an hour.

Kanna-sensei did not come to her that first Sunday. She only smiled at her from across the room, once, kindly, the way a doctor smiles at a patient in a waiting room who is not yet ready to be seen.

The second Sunday, Kanna-sensei knelt beside her and put a hand on her knee and said nothing at all for twenty minutes, and Katagiri cried for the first time in eleven months. Not for long. Not loudly. She was very disciplined about it, even then. But she cried, with a stranger's hand on her knee, in a white room full of strangers, and nobody looked at her, and it was the first time since August that anything had felt like rest.

The third Sunday, Kanna-sensei said, very softly, so that only she could hear, *You loved him.*

Katagiri had not said it to anyone. Not to Shimizu. Not to her mother. Not to the cats. Not to Gin, in two years of adjacent cases and shared cars and coffee from the machine on the third floor and one night, once, in the rain, on the roof of the Division, when she had very nearly said it and had said instead *you should go home, your brother will be waiting*. And he had looked at her for a long moment with an expression she had never been able to read, and said *yes*, and gone.

*You loved him,* said Kanna-sensei. *You still do. Of course you do. It's the most natural thing in the world.* And then, even more softly, with her warm dry hand on Katagiri's knee, *I could bring him home, you know.*

Katagiri did not drink the tea that Sunday. She was not stupid. She was a Channel with sixteen years in the field and she could feel the far shore under that floor like cold water under ice.

She did not drink it. But she came back.

[break Thursday]

On Thursday the twenty-third of July, Rei came up to the fourth floor at lunchtime and stood by her desk.

He looked better than he had in June. Thinner, still, and tired round the eyes in a way that made her think of Gin at the end, but straighter. As if he had put something heavy down and picked up something else that was heavier but fitted his hands better.

"Katagiri-san," he said, very quietly, and glanced round the office. Nobody was near. "Can I ask you something? Not for a form."

She put down her pen. "Of course."

"Can Kuga watch a house for me? On Saturday night. Not go through. Just watch. From this side." He hesitated. "The cats can see further than we can. Shimizu said."

"They can." She looked at him. "Which house?"

"Tanizaki's." He said it the way he always said it, with something in his voice like a hand on a doorframe. "Behind Ōji. The one with the garden."

She knew the house. Everybody on the Grid knew the house. She had never been inside it. Gin had, for nineteen years.

She kept her hands flat on the desk, either side of the bowl. "Why?"

Rei looked at the kintsugi bowl on the corner of her desk for a long time. She watched him decide. She watched him weigh it, the way Gin used to weigh things, with his whole face very still. And then she watched him decide to trust her, and it went through her like a knife.

"Someone's coming to tea," he said. "Saturday. Late. After dark. Tanizaki said three cups." He swallowed. "It's him. The thing. In Gin. It asked to meet. Properly. Not on a roof. Tanizaki said his house was the only place in the city where nobody would dare start anything. He said he's known it was coming for nineteen years."

Katagiri sat very still at her desk.

"Rei," she said. "That's..." She stopped. She made her voice precise, the way it went when things were bad. "Does anyone else know?"

He counted them off on his fingers without seeming to notice. "Itsuki. Kato. Chen Lian at the Archive. Majima." He paused. "And you, now."

Katagiri heard her own voice go very level. "Not Shimizu?"

"Not yet." He looked at her, and his face was so open, so frightened, so like his brother's on the roof in the rain. "I don't want anyone to come with a team. If anyone comes with a team, it'll go. I just want Kuga to watch the street. In case. In case *she* knows. That's all."

Katagiri picked up the kintsugi bowl. She turned it in her hands, the way she always did, so that the gold lines caught the light from the window.

"Of course," she said. "Of course Kuga will watch. I'll ask him myself."

Rei let out a long breath. "Thank you." He bowed, quickly, the way he did, a little too low. "Thank you, Katagiri-san. Really."

He went away down the corridor.

She sat at her desk with the bowl in her hands for a long time after he had gone.

[break Marunouchi]

The Office of Public Confidence was not in the Division building in Ōji. It was in Marunouchi, on the twenty-sixth floor of a glass tower opposite Tokyo Station, above the Division's public face, a bright ground-floor information centre full of leaflets about disaster preparedness that nobody ever read. The reception desk upstairs was white marble. The receptionist did not look up. The lift went up so fast and so smoothly that Katagiri's ears popped.

Hagiwara Mizuki's office was all glass on two sides. The city lay under it like a map. At five in the afternoon in late July the light came in hard and gold and flat across a long white desk with nothing on it but a closed folder, a glass of water and a fountain pen.

Hagiwara stood at the window with her back to the door. She was a woman of about fifty, in a grey suit cut so exactly that it looked as if it had been poured onto her. Her hair was short and grey and perfect. When she turned round, her face was handsome and tired and very still, the face of a woman who had never once in her life been interrupted, and did not intend to start now.

"Katagiri-san," she said. "Sixteen years in the field. Kita primary for four. The Cattery." She did not smile. "Please sit down. You said on the telephone that you had information about an unregistered Class A entity."

Katagiri sat. She put her bag on her knees and held it with both hands.

"Wearing a registered field officer," she said. "Uedera Gin. Missing since last August. The file says *administrative review*. It isn't. He's possessed. Something very old is wearing him. It's been in Tokyo for eleven months." She made her voice very steady. "It will be at a private house behind Ōji on Saturday night. After dark. For two or three hours. It will not be expecting anyone."

Hagiwara looked at her for a long time. Then she set one fingertip on the closed folder. "And how do you know this?"

Katagiri held her bag a little tighter. "I can't tell you that."

"You can, and you will." Hagiwara picked up the fountain pen. She did not uncap it. She only held it, the way some people hold a cigarette they are not going to smoke. "But not today. Today I'll accept that you know." She turned the pen over. "What do you want, Katagiri-san? People don't come up twenty-six floors to give me things. They come up to ask for something."

Katagiri looked at the city.

"I want him brought in alive," she said. "Contained. Not destroyed. Not exorcised in the street by some trainee with a bucket of salt. Brought in, and held, safely, in the sublevel, until somebody can get the thing out of him properly. Without killing him." Her voice did not shake. She was very proud of that, afterwards, and hated herself for being proud. "He's one of ours. He's a good officer. He deserves to come home."

Hagiwara uncapped the fountain pen.

She opened the folder on the white desk. There was a form in it already. Katagiri could see that, even upside down. Already printed. Already filled in, most of it, in neat black type. A Public Confidence containment order. Three officers and a van. The date was blank. The address was blank.

Hagiwara wrote them in. Slowly. In a beautiful, even hand. Then she signed it, at the bottom, and the signature took a long time, and the nib scratched on the thick paper like a small animal in a wall.

"Alive," she said, and capped the pen. "You have my word. We don't waste resources. A Class A in a registered body is worth a great deal more to the Division contained than destroyed." She looked up. Her eyes were very tired, and very clear. "And the brother?"

Katagiri's hands tightened on her bag.

"He'll be there," she said. "He'll try to stop you. Don't hurt him."

"We'll be careful." Hagiwara closed the folder. "He's a registered nobody who keeps turning up where the data says nobody should be. I've been meaning to have a conversation with him for some time." She stood up. The meeting was over. "Thank you, Katagiri-san. You've done the right thing. People always think the right thing will feel better than it does."

[break The white room]

She went to Asagaya that same evening. It was not Sunday. The door was open anyway. It always was.

Kanna-sensei was alone in the long white room, kneeling by the window, arranging lilies in a vase. She did not turn round.

"You've been very brave today," she said. "I can feel it on you. Like rain."

Katagiri knelt on the nearest cushion, by the door, by the photograph.

"Saturday," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "Tanizaki Seiichi's house. Behind Ōji. After dark. Public Confidence will be there with a van. Three officers. They'll take him alive." She looked at the photograph. "Then you can do it. What you said. Bring him home. Take the thing out and give him back."

Kanna-sensei set the last lily in the vase. She turned it, very slightly, so that its face was toward the light.

"Of course," she said. "Thank you, Noa. You've given me exactly what I needed." She turned round, and smiled, and her eyes were dark and kind and held Katagiri's a beat too long. "By Obon, you'll have him. I promise. Sitting in a chair. I'll make sure you're the first person he sees."

Katagiri looked at her.

Something in her, very deep, the part of her that was a Channel with sixteen years in the field, the part that could feel the far shore like cold water under ice, said one quiet word.

*Chair.*

She did not listen to it. She had been not listening to things for eleven months. She was very good at it.

"Thank you," she said, and stood, and bowed, and went out of the white room and down the path between the white flowers.

[break The bowl]

At home, the cats would not come to her.

Not one. The one-eyed tabby got off the sofa when she came in and went into the bathroom. The black sisters went under the bed. Old Mugi, who had not moved from her pillow in two years for anything less than an earthquake, got up stiffly and walked out of the bedroom past her without looking at her, and lay down in the corridor with his back to the door.

Katagiri stood in the kitchen with her bag still on her shoulder.

On the counter, among the jars of plum jam, sat the jar labelled *Gin*.

She had taken the kintsugi bowl home with her. She did not remember doing it. She found it in her bag, wrapped in a handkerchief, and unwrapped it, and set it on the counter beside the jar.

The light from the street lamp came in through the window and lay across it.

The gold lines were still there. Bright and shining, running across the brown clay like rivers on a map, the way they had for thirteen years. Exactly the same.

But she looked at them, and for the first time, all she could see was where it had broken.

She sat down on the kitchen floor with her back against the cupboard and her bag still on her shoulder, and the cats watched her from the doorways, and none of them came.

On Saturday morning, she went into the Division early, before anyone else, and went up to the Cattery, and stood in front of the highest shelf.

Kuga looked down at her with his heavy head and his still yellow eyes.

"Rei asked if you'd watch a house tonight," she said. "Tanizaki's. Behind Ōji."

Kuga did not move.

"I told him you would." Her voice cracked, very slightly, and she made it stop. "So you'll have to. Please."

Kuga looked at her for a very long time.

Then he turned his back on her, on his high shelf, and lay down facing the wall, and closed his eyes.
