#title Katagiri

The text came at ten past six on the morning of the twelfth of August. Rei was awake. He had been awake since four, sitting at the kitchen table with Gin's book open at the half page of streets and stations, and the calendar by the fridge with its small neat circle, and the fan going round.

*Come in early. Investigation, fourth floor. Bring nothing. Katagiri.*

He was there at seven. The Investigation office was empty except for her. The strip light over her desk was flickering, as it always did. The blinds were up and the morning came in flat and white off the railway lines. She was standing at her desk in the same navy suit, with her reading glasses on the end of her nose and her hair falling over one eye, and on the desk, where the four neat stacks of files usually were, there was a map.

A big paper map of Tokyo, the kind they sold in station bookshops for tourists, opened out across the whole desk and held flat at the corners with a stapler, a tape dispenser, a can of cold coffee and a framed photograph turned face down. There were pins in it. Red map pins with round heads. Rei counted twenty before he stopped counting.

"Sit," said Katagiri. "That chair. Not the broken one."

He sat.

"In August last year," she said, "you rang me. The week he went. I said I'd look." She took her glasses off and let them hang on their cord. "I looked. I found nothing, and I told you so. Two lines. You hated it."

"Yes."

"I didn't stop looking." She sat down on the edge of the desk, on the corner of the map, beside Kita ward. "The official file on Uedera Gin was closed on the second of October. Reason code forty-one. Officer on extended assignment, no further action. You were told he was on a mission. You've been told it in this building every time you've asked." She looked at him. "I've worked here sixteen years. I've seen reason code forty-one four times. It isn't for missions. It means do not search."

Rei said nothing.

"The closure was authorised by Public Confidence," said Katagiri. "Not by Field. Not by the Director. By the glass office upstairs that writes the press releases." She let that sit for a moment. "I put that in a memo to Sena in Internal Affairs on Wednesday. He said thank you and nothing else. With Sena that means he's interested."

[break The pins]

"So I stopped looking for Gin," she said. "And started looking for his money."

She turned round on the desk and pulled a thin folder from under the stapler. Inside were photocopies. Bank statements, with most of the lines blacked out and some of them circled in red.

"His salary still goes in every month," she said. "You know that. You can't touch it. Nobody's frozen it. Haruki made sure." She ran a finger down a page. "And somebody's been taking it out. Small amounts. Three thousand. Five thousand. Once, twelve thousand, in March. From cash machines. Forty-seven times in a year."

Rei looked at the pins.

"The first one," said Katagiri, and touched a pin by Jūjō station with one fingertip, "was at three twelve in the morning on the thirteenth of August. The konbini by the north exit. Four minutes' walk from your flat. Five thousand yen." She touched another. "Fourteenth. Same machine." Another. "Sixteenth. Ōji." Another. "Then nothing for nine days. Then Akabane."

She went on. Rei watched her finger go from pin to pin across the paper city. Ōji. Akabane. Tabata. Higashi-Jūjō. Ueno. A cluster round Nippori in the winter. One in Ogikubo. One, in May, at a station Rei did not know in the far west, past Tachikawa, by itself.

"He doesn't go near your flat after September," she said. "Not once. He doesn't go near this building. He doesn't go near Asagaya at all. Never." She tapped a blank space on the map, a long way west. "He goes everywhere else. He walks a great deal. He takes out enough for a few days, and he buys food with it. I've got eleven of the konbini receipts back from the machines that print them. Rice balls. Bread. Barley tea. Once a whole box of fried chicken." She looked at Rei over the top of the folder. "And in March, at a supermarket in Ōji, a sack of rice and a rice cooker. The cheap kind. Three thousand eight hundred yen."

Rei found that he had put his hand over his mouth.

"He's living somewhere," said Katagiri. "He has a kitchen. He's cooking rice." She put the folder down. "Whatever's in him is keeping the body fed."

[break The last one]

"Since the start of July," she said, "it's been all north. Along the river."

She took her finger to the top of the map. The pins there were close together. Akabane. Ukima. Shimo. The long curve of the Arakawa, where Kita ward stopped and Saitama began.

"Nine times in five weeks," she said. "All within a kilometre of the river." She picked up the framed photograph that was holding down the western corner of the map and turned it over and set it down in front of him.

It was not a photograph. It was a still from a security camera, printed on ordinary paper and slipped into a cheap frame so it would lie flat. Grey and grainy. The top of a cash machine, the edge of a konbini counter, a bright fridge full of drinks. And in front of the machine, side on, a tall man in a grey jacket, with long black hair tied back loosely at his neck. He had a rice ball in one hand, still in its plastic, and he was looking down at the screen of the machine with an expression of great concentration, as if it were a hard puzzle he had only lately learned to solve.

The time stamp at the bottom said *08/11 23:58*.

Last night. Two minutes before midnight.

Rei looked at his brother's face.

He had seen it across a shrine yard in February, and under a tree in July, and both times it had been gone before he could look properly. Here it stood still. Thinner than he remembered. The cheekbones sharp. A small cut, healing, on the jaw, where something had caught it. Gin had never in his life let his hair grow that long. He was standing very straight, the way he always had, an officer's back, as if somebody might inspect him.

"Where," said Rei. His voice did not work properly.

"Akabane. The Family Mart on the river side of the station. Under the railway bridge." Katagiri did not look at the photograph. She looked at Rei. "Twelve hours ago. I've rung the shop. The night boy remembers him. He says the tall man comes in late, two or three times a week, buys rice balls and tea, and once asked him what the difference was between the salmon and the salted salmon, and listened very carefully to the answer." She was quiet for a moment. "He said he's very polite. He said he always says thank you, and always looks a little surprised when he says it."

Rei put his hands flat on the map, either side of the photograph, so they would not do anything.

"I don't know what's in him," said Katagiri. "I'm not going to ask you. I don't need to know to do my job." She stood up off the desk and began, carefully, to take the pins out of the map, one at a time, and put them back in their little plastic box. "I've been an investigator for sixteen years. I find people. That's what I do. I found him." She took out the pin at Jūjō station last, the first one, and held it between her fingers for a moment before she put it in the box. "What you do now is not my department."

"Why?"

She stopped with the box in her hand.

"Why did you keep looking?" said Rei. "After they closed it. Nobody asked you to."

Katagiri looked at the map with the little holes all over it where the pins had been.

"Because he was a good officer," she said. "Because the file was closed by people who don't do field work, for a reason that isn't a reason. Because you came to my desk every week for a month in September and stood there and didn't ask, and I could see you not asking." She snapped the lid on the box. "And because nobody else in this building was going to do it properly. That's the only reason I ever do anything."

She folded up the map, along its creases, and put it in an envelope with the bank statements and the security still, and sealed it, and wrote on the front in her small square hand. *Uedera. Personal. Not for file.* She held it out to him.

He took it.

"Don't go alone," she said. "That's the only thing I'll say that isn't my business. Take the telepath. Take the boy who draws. Take Moriya if he can walk." She sat down behind her desk and picked up her can of coffee and found it empty and put it down again. "And go now. It's the twelfth."

He looked at her.

"I can count," said Katagiri. "I read his file. I read the Director's file on the east corridor. I've known what day it was for a year." She pulled the first of the four stacks of files back into the middle of the desk where the map had been, and opened it, and put her glasses on. "Go on."

[break The stairs]

He did not take the lift. He went down the stairs at a run, four floors, with the envelope held against his chest, and at the bottom of the stairwell he stopped with his hand on the bar of the fire door and could not push it.

He opened the envelope again in the stairwell, standing under the green exit sign, and took out the photograph in its cheap frame, and looked at it.

A tall man in a grey jacket with a rice ball in his hand, looking at a cash machine as if it were a puzzle.

*He always says thank you,* the night boy had said. *And always looks a little surprised.*

Gin had never been surprised to say thank you in his life. He said it to bus drivers. He said it to vending machines when they gave him the right change.

Whatever was standing in front of that machine was learning to say it.

Rei put the photograph back in the envelope. He took out his phone. His hands were shaking so much that he dialled Itsuki twice by mistake before he got Kato, and then he had them both, one after the other, and said the same thing to each of them.

"Akabane. The river. I know where he's been."

Itsuki said he was coming. Kato said he already knew, he'd heard it from the street, he was in a taxi on the Meiji-dōri overpass, and would Rei please stop thinking so loudly in capitals, he had a headache.

Rei pushed the bar of the fire door, and went out into the white heat of the morning on the twelfth of August.
