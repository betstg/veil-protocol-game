#title The Temple

At five o'clock Shimizu took them down to the Preparation Room.

Rei had walked past its door a hundred times. It was on the first floor, at the end of the corridor beyond Records, a plain sliding door of pale wood with no plate on it, only a small paper charm pinned at eye level, the kind you buy at shrines for safe travel. He had thought it was a cupboard.

It was a small square room with tatami on the floor and nothing on the walls. A low shelf ran round three sides of it, and on the shelf were things. Not Division things. People's things. A cheap plastic lucky cat with its paw up. A row of smooth river stones. A child's drawing of a dog, curling at the edges. Boxes of matches. A small bronze bell. An old photograph of a woman laughing on a beach, propped against the wall. Somebody's rosary of dark wooden beads. A tin of fruit drops. A pair of reading glasses that nobody had come back for.

"Before you go out," said Shimizu, "you come in here. Everybody does. You do whatever it is you do." She knelt down at one end of the room, by the window, and put her hands on her knees and closed her eyes. "Nobody asks what it is. Nobody laughs. Five minutes."

That was all she said. She did not explain anything else.

Aoi knelt by the shelf and took off her bracelet and counted the ceramic beads, one by one, with her lips moving, and put it back on. Kato took his white king out of his pocket and set it on the shelf, upright, facing the door, and looked at it for a long time, and then put it back in his pocket. Tendo sat cross-legged in the middle of the floor, unwrapped a cream bun, broke it exactly in half, and put one half on the shelf beside the photograph of the laughing woman. He ate the other half with his eyes closed. Nobody asked. Out in the corridor, Kuma lay across the doorway with his chin on his paws, and Shimizu, without opening her eyes, reached out one hand and touched the red and white cord round his neck.

Rei did not know what he did. He had never done anything. He knelt down by the shelf and looked at all the things people had left there, the stones and the bell and the reading glasses, and wondered whose they were, and whether those people had come back.

Then he took the key out from under his shirt, on its cord, and held it in his hand. Cold. Steady. He did not say anything to it. He just held it, for the length of four breaths in and four held and six out, the way he had held it every night for six months at three in the morning on the sofa. Then he put it back under his shirt.

On the shelf, at the very end, half hidden behind the tin of fruit drops, there was a small grey stone with a hole worn through the middle. And tied through the hole, a length of red cotton thread. The cheap kind. The kind you buy in a little sewing shop through a half-lowered shutter.

He did not touch it. He looked at it for a long time.

"Time," said Shimizu, and opened her eyes.

[break Takinogawa]

Takinogawa is a quiet part of Kita, west of Ōji, full of old houses and small temples and narrow streets that go up and down over the hills. At half past six on a February evening it was almost dark. The air was cold and very still and smelled of woodsmoke and frost.

They came in two cars. Shimizu drove the first, with Kuma upright on the back seat. Tendo drove the second, a long black car of his own that was much too expensive for a Division field officer, with Aoi in the front and Kato and Rei in the back. Tendo played jazz on the car stereo and sang along to it, badly, the whole way. Aoi laughed at him. Kato looked out of the window and said nothing.

Jōkō-ji was at the end of a lane, behind a low wall and a wooden gate. It was small and old and very plain. A main hall with a heavy tiled roof that curved up at the corners. A little graveyard behind it with stone markers crowded together under bare trees. And to the left of the gate, a wooden bell tower, open on all four sides, with a great bronze bell hanging inside it from a thick beam. The bell was dark green with age. A heavy wooden log hung beside it on ropes, for striking.

An old man was standing at the gate.

He was about sixty, in the dark robes of a Buddhist priest, with a shaved head and a long, lined, gentle face. He was holding a can of beer. Not drinking it. Just holding it in both hands, the way you hold a cup of tea to warm your fingers.

"You're the Division," he said. "Thank you for coming. I am Egami." He bowed. "I'm sorry about the beer. I hold one in the evenings. It helps me think. I never finish it." He looked at the five of them, and at the dog. "You're very young. Most of you."

"Reverend Egami," said Shimizu. "You were told to leave."

"I was. I didn't." He smiled, a tired, kind smile. "This is my temple. These are my people. Three of them have died this winter after praying here, under my roof, in front of my altar. I am not going to leave them alone with whatever is doing it." He turned and looked at the bell tower. "It will ring soon. It rings at dusk. Every day. The hour between day and night." He looked back at them. "Would you like tea first? Or would you like to watch it ring?"

[break Dusk]

They watched it ring.

They stood in the cold yard in a half circle round the bell tower, the five of them and the dog, while the sky went from grey to dark blue to a strange deep orange at the edges, the colour of a coal in a fire. Rei had read about this hour in Gin's book. *Ōma-ga-toki*, the old people called it. The meeting hour. The hour when the day and the night overlap and you cannot tell which one you are in, and in the old stories, the hour when you were most likely to meet a demon on the road.

At four minutes to six the bell rang.

Nobody touched it. The wooden log hung still on its ropes. But the bell rang anyway, one long, deep, booming note, so loud and so low that Rei felt it in his chest and his teeth and the bones of his feet. The sound rolled out over the graveyard and the roofs of Takinogawa. It went on, and on, much longer than a bell should ring, fading so slowly that he could not tell when it stopped.

Kuma growled.

Rei's wrist was so cold it hurt.

He opened the door in his chest a finger's width. He did not need any more. The whole temple yard was full of it. Grief. Old grief and new grief, thick and grey and heavy, like wet ash. The grief of a hundred years of people coming here to pray for their dead. Mothers for children. Children for parents. Husbands for wives. All of it had soaked into this ground, this wood, this bronze, the way the bath water at Tsuru-no-yu had soaked up the tiredness of the neighbourhood. And something had found it and was feeding on it. Rei could feel it feeding. A slow, steady pull, like a mouth at a straw.

And the feeding was not coming from the bell.

He turned round.

Reverend Egami was standing behind them, by the main hall, with his can of beer in his hands. He was looking at the bell. His face was calm. Peaceful, almost. Gently sad.

And his pupils were enormous.

[break The priest]

Rei saw it. And in the same second, beside him, he heard Kato breathe in very sharply.

"Shimizu-san," said Kato very quietly. "Two."

Shimizu did not turn round. She had seen it too. Rei saw her shoulders go still. He saw the small line between her eyebrows deepen as she chose.

"Reverend," she said pleasantly, still looking at the bell. "That was very impressive. Could we have that tea now?"

"Of course," said Egami. His voice was perfectly normal. Gentle, a little tired, an old priest's voice. "Come in. It's warmer in the hall."

He turned and walked toward the main hall. He walked normally. He held his can of beer.

Kato's lips were moving without sound. He was listening. He caught Rei's eye and shook his head once, very slightly, and held up two fingers, close to his chest where only Rei could see.

Two layers. Two voices. Like the man with the cats.

But different. Rei felt it, with the door in his chest open its finger's width. In the man with the cats, the thing had been on top, driving, and the man had been underneath, fighting. In Reverend Egami it was the other way round. The priest was on top. Awake. Himself. He did not know. He did not know anything was in him at all.

And underneath him, curled up deep inside, quietly, patiently, something was drinking his grief.

[break What Egami carried]

The main hall was dim and cold and smelled of incense and old wood. At the far end was the altar, with a gold statue of the Buddha sitting calmly in the candlelight, and in front of it rows of small black lacquered tablets with names written on them in gold. The names of the dead. Hundreds of them. Egami knelt by a low table at the side and made tea for them on a small charcoal brazier, carefully, with the can of beer standing beside him on the mat.

They sat. Shimizu sat closest to him. Tendo sat by the door, with his legs crossed, smiling. Aoi sat by the altar, turning her bracelet very slowly. Kato sat beside Rei and did not drink his tea.

"It started in October," said Egami, pouring. "The bell. I thought at first it was the wind."

"What happened in October?" said Rei.

Egami looked at him.

"Your colleague is very direct," he said to Shimizu.

"He is," said Shimizu. "Please answer him."

Egami put the teapot down. He looked at the rows of black tablets in front of the altar. His face did not change, but his hands, round the can of beer, tightened.

"A boy died in October," he said. "A parishioner's son. Seventeen. He came to me in September. He sat where you're sitting." He nodded at Rei. "He said he heard voices. He said he was frightened. He said he didn't want to live any more. I told him what I've told a hundred people in forty years. That the voices would pass. That he was loved. That he should pray, and rest, and come back next week." He was quiet for a moment. "I should have called someone. A doctor. The Division. Anyone. I didn't. I thought prayer was enough. I thought I was enough." He looked down at the beer. "He did not come back next week. They found him by the river."

Nobody said anything. In the dark at the end of the hall, the candles in front of the Buddha flickered.

"I've prayed for him every evening since," said Egami. "At dusk. In front of his tablet. Every evening I ask him to forgive me. And every evening I know he can't, because I'm the one who has to." He smiled faintly. "And then the bell rings."

Rei understood.

He understood all of it, all at once, the way he had understood the mug in Lilac. A boy's grief and a priest's guilt, in the same temple, at the same hour, every day for four months. The guilt so heavy, so deep, so endless, that something had smelled it from far away and come to drink. It had crept into the priest the way damp creeps into a wall. And from inside him, it had reached out and drunk from everyone who came to pray. Three old people with their own small griefs, kneeling in front of their own tablets. It had drunk them dry, slowly, gently, while the priest knelt beside them and prayed and did not know.

It had been using his guilt as a door.

"Reverend," said Rei carefully. "There's something I need to tell you."

The candles in front of the altar went out.

All of them at once. Every candle, every lamp, every small flame in the hall, snuffed out together as if someone had blown on them. The hall went black.

In the dark, Rei heard the bell ring outside. Not once. Over and over. Faster and faster. Deep, booming, without stopping, like a heart beating in panic.

And beside him, in the dark, someone began to laugh. It was Egami's laugh. It came from where Egami was kneeling. But it was the wrong shape. Too low. Too long. Without any breath in it at all.

"*He was going to tell you,*" said the voice in the dark. Soft, and slow, and full of a terrible gentle sadness. "*He was going to tell you. And then you would have made me leave. And then who would drink it?*" A pause. "*Who would drink all this grief? Who would carry it, if not me?*"

Kuma was barking now, outside, over and over, wild.

"Lights," said Shimizu's voice in the dark, perfectly calm. "Tendo. Now."

"On it," said Tendo, from the dark by the door, and snapped his fingers.
