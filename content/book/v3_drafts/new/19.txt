#title The Fight

He told Itsuki on Sunday night, in the studio on the third floor at Geidai, among the canvases and the turpentine, with the windows black and the whole building empty except for the two of them and a security guard somewhere downstairs watching television.

He had not meant to tell him like that. He had meant to wait for a good moment. But Itsuki looked up from the canvas once, under the hard white light, with a brush between his teeth, and took it out and said, "You didn't sleep. Why didn't you sleep?" and Rei was too tired to lie. So he told him. The empty pool. The fifty people. Switch on the diving board. The man whose sister was in a clean room with soft toys. Choi Hana and her sleeves. Kanna-sensei.

He watched Itsuki's face change while he talked. It went from curious to still to something else. Something hard and closed, like a shutter coming down over a shop window.

"The Grid," said Itsuki.

"Yes."

"You went to a Grid meeting."

"Setsu-san took me. The lady from the bath house. She vouched for me."

"A lady from a bath house." Itsuki laughed. It was not his normal laugh. It was short and ugly. "Great. That's great. A lady from a bath house." He was not looking at Rei. He was looking at the canvas, at the wet paint, at nothing. "And you're going back. Aren't you."

"Next month. And before that, I need to find this Kanna-sensei. Switch said Gin was asking about her in July..."

"Switch said." Itsuki stood up. He did it too fast, and knocked the jar of brushes off the stool, and did not pick them up. "Switch said. You've known these people for one night, Rei. One night. And you're going to do what they say."

"I'm not doing what they say. I'm following Gin."

"You don't know that! You don't know anything about them!" Itsuki's voice cracked off the high ceiling. Somewhere downstairs the television went quiet, and then came back on. "You don't know who they are, or what they want, or what they'll do with you when they've got what they want. They're not a village, Rei. They're not a nice little club with tea in a flask. They're people who decided the rules don't apply to them. Some of them are good. Most of them are just people who sell things. Secrets. Favours. Other people."

[break Sei]

"How do you know?" said Rei. He was standing too now. "You've never been."

"I didn't have to go." Itsuki's hands were shaking. "My brother went."

Rei stopped.

In seven years of friendship Itsuki had mentioned his brother perhaps three times. Kuroda Sei. Two years older. Rei had never met him. He knew only that Sei did not come home, and that the family did not say his name, and that Itsuki changed the subject very fast whenever it came up.

"Sei went to the Grid when he was eighteen," said Itsuki. His voice was very quiet now. Much worse than the shouting. "He said the family was a prison. He said the CVD was a prison. He said the Grid was free. And he took everything he knew. Everything. Four generations of the family's notes on every thing that's ever moved in this part of the city. He took copies of them in his head and he sold them, one piece at a time, to whoever paid. For favours. For money. For a couch to sleep on." He swallowed. "He sleeps on couches, Rei. He's twenty-two years old and he hasn't slept in the same bed for two weeks in four years. He has no address. My father stopped saying his name. And every few months somebody from the Grid knocks on our gate and offers to sell us back something Sei sold them."

"Itsuki, I'm not Sei."

"I know you're not Sei!" It came out as a shout again. "That's the point! You're not Sei, you're you, and you're going to walk in there and be nice to everyone and believe what they tell you, because you always believe people, because you think everyone is like Gin. And they're going to use you. They're going to take everything you can do, which is more than anyone in that pool can do, I bet, and they're going to sell it."

"And the Division won't?"

Itsuki stopped.

"The Division says my brother's on assignment," said Rei. He was shaking now too. "The Division sent people in nice suits to make a whole station forget a man was killed in front of them. The Division won't tell me one true thing. The Division put a man's sister in a room with teddy bears. Your family wouldn't even help Gin when he came to ask, six years ago. Your father told me so." He heard his own voice getting louder and could not stop it. "So who am I supposed to trust, Itsuki? You tell me. Which one? Because everyone's lying to me except a bunch of strangers in an empty swimming pool."

"Me," said Itsuki. "You're supposed to trust me."

The studio was completely silent. Outside, a train went past on the line behind the park, a long way off.

"I do trust you," said Rei.

"Then don't go."

"I have to go. Gin went."

"Gin is *gone*, Rei!"

It was out before Itsuki could stop it. Rei saw it on his face, the moment he heard himself. The horror. But it was out, and it was in the room, and it could not be put back.

Rei felt it go into him like a cold blade.

Later, much later, he would understand that it had gone into Itsuki first. That Itsuki had been carrying that sentence around inside him for two months, in his studio at four in the morning with a brush between his teeth, painting and not saying it. Gin had fed him every week for eight years. Gin had stood in front of his worst paintings for ten minutes at a time. Gin was his too. A bit. He had said so, on the kitchen floor, with an orange in his hand. Later Rei would understand all of that. Not then.

"Say that again," he said.

"I didn't mean..."

"Say it again."

"Rei..."

Rei shoved him.

He had never hit anyone in his life. It was not even a hit. It was both hands flat on Itsuki's chest, a hard shove, the kind small boys give each other in school corridors. Itsuki stumbled back two steps across the paint-spattered floor. His heel caught the leg of the easel. He went down on one knee, and the canvas came down with him, face first, onto the boards.

He got up fast. His face was white, and then it was red. And he shoved Rei back, much harder, with his whole body behind it. Rei went backward into the wall with a bang that rattled the tall windows. The back of his head hit the plaster. Stars went across his eyes.

They stood there, a metre apart, breathing hard, staring at each other. Rei's head rang. Itsuki's hands were in fists at his sides. There was a red mark on his cheek where his own hand had caught it as he fell.

For eight years they had never once fought. Not properly. They had argued about art, and food, and which of them was more stupid, and Itsuki had thrown orange peel at him and called him an idiot a thousand times. But this was different. This was two people looking at each other and seeing, for the first time, somebody they could hurt.

"Fine," said Itsuki. His voice was very calm. That was the worst thing. "Fine. Go to your Grid. Go to your pool. Find your Kanna-sensei. Get yourself killed in a white room like your brother." He turned away. "I'm done."

"Itsuki."

"I said I'm done." He picked up his hoodie off the floor and walked to the door. At the door he stopped, with his back to Rei. "You know the way out." He did not turn round. "Turn the light off. The guard gets funny about the light."

[break Gone]

He did not answer his phone that day. Or the next.

On Monday Rei went to the training hall at six, because Sōun had said the first morning he did not come he should not come again. He knelt on the soft old boards for two and a half hours and could not find the door in his chest at all. Grandmother Mitsu hit him on the back of the head twice and then, the third time, did not, and looked at him for a while instead.

On Wednesday he went back to Geidai. The studio on the third floor was locked. A girl in the next studio, with clay on her apron, said Itsuki had come on Monday afternoon and taken some things and said he was going away for a while. "Away," she said, puzzled. "I didn't know he went anywhere. I thought he lived here."

His flat near Ueno was dark. The letterbox was full.

On Thursday, after training, Mrs. Fujii walked Rei to the small door in the gate, as she did every morning. She did not open it. She stood with her hand on the latch and looked at the gravel.

"The young master has gone to the house in Kamakura," she said. "By the sea. He asked that nobody be told." She opened the door and bowed. "I have told nobody."

He sent messages. He sent too many. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry about the shove. Can we talk. Itsuki please. I'm outside your flat. Just tell me you're okay.* They all said *Delivered*. None of them said *Read*.

He looked at them for a long time, standing on the platform at Ueno. It was exactly the same as Gin.

On Friday night he sat in the jazz bar in Nezu with a coffee he did not drink and told Haruki all of it. Haruki listened, and made another coffee, and listened more. When Rei finished, Haruki was quiet for a long time.

"He's not wrong about Sei," he said at last. "I know Sei a little. He's not a bad person. He's a very angry one, and a very lonely one, and he sells things he shouldn't. And he's not wrong about the Grid either. There are people in it I'd trust with my life. And there are people in it I wouldn't trust with a bicycle." He wiped the counter that was already clean. "He's scared, Rei. That's all. He's scared because he's already lost one brother to it. He's not going to lose you too."

"So what do I do?"

"Nothing." Haruki put the cloth down and folded it in half, and in half again, very precisely, the way he did everything when he was thinking about something else. "You wait. You go on. You keep your door open for him. People like Itsuki come back when they need to. Not before." He looked at Rei. "When he comes back, don't make him say sorry first. He won't be able to. Just let him in."

Rei went home through the empty Friday streets. At the top of the hill, outside the flat, he stopped on the walkway and looked at the place on the concrete where, two months ago, a cold wet key had lain on its own in the dark.

He took out his phone and wrote one more message. Not *sorry*. Not *please*.

*ok,* he wrote. Lowercase. The way Itsuki wrote. *the door's open.*

*Delivered.*

He went inside and did not lock it.
