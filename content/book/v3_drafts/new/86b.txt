#title Seven Stars

Gin went back to work on the first Monday of October, in his grey jacket, on the 7:42 from Jūjō.

He had not been on a commuter train at that hour for fourteen months, and he stood in the doorway of the carriage, pressed flat between a schoolgirl doing her English homework against his back and a man in a suit asleep on his feet with his forehead on the window, and looked round at all of it with an expression Rei had not seen on him since Rei was small. The white-gloved station staff at Akabane, pushing the last people in so the doors would close. The adverts hung from the ceiling, swaying, for beer and English schools and a magazine about fishing. The silence. Two hundred people in a steel box and nobody saying anything, because in Tokyo you do not talk on the morning train, or eat, or answer your phone, and nobody has to be told. Gin stood in the middle of it with his bag held against his chest and his face very still, and when the train swung round the long curve before Tabata and everyone leaned together like grass in the wind, he leaned with them, and Rei saw him close his eyes.

"He's never done this," Gin said, very low, at Ueno, when half the carriage got off and they could breathe. "Not once. All year. He walked everywhere. He thinks this is the most extraordinary thing he's ever seen." He looked at the schoolgirl, who had gone back to her English homework. "He wants to know why nobody's talking."

"What did you tell him?"

"I said it's polite." Gin thought about it. "He says he approves."

The Healer had gone back across on the third night after the river. Rei had sat at the kitchen table for all three nights and watched it happen, in and out, like the tide going out over sand, a little more each night, and on the last night, at a quarter to four, Gin had put down his cup of tea and sat very still for a moment with his eyes shut and then said, in an ordinary voice, *he says thank you for the tea*, and that was all. But something stayed. Not the voice. Gin was very clear about that. Only a kind of warmth, low down, like a room where somebody has been sitting, and a habit of noticing things he had not noticed before. He had started saying *he approves* about things and then stopping, as if surprised to hear himself.

[break Investigation]

He went to Investigation on the fourth floor on his third day back, at five o'clock, when the office was emptying. Rei went with him, because Gin asked.

The strip light over Katagiri's desk was flickering. The blinds were half down against the low sun coming off the railway lines, and the light came in under them in long yellow bars across the carpet. She was sitting at her desk in the navy suit with her reading glasses on the end of her nose and her hair over one eye, and on the desk there were four neat stacks of files and a can of cold coffee and nothing else. She did not look up when they came in. She finished the line she was reading, and initialled it, and put the file on the second stack, and then she looked up.

"Uedera," she said. And then, past him, "Uedera."

"Katagiri-san." Gin bowed. Properly. From the waist, the formal bow you give a superior or someone you owe something to, and held it. "I wanted to thank you."

Katagiri took her glasses off and let them hang on their cord.

"Sit down," she said. "That chair. Not the broken one."

Gin sat. Rei stood by the window. Down on the tracks a Yamanote train went by, green and silver, full of people going home.

Katagiri opened the drawer of her desk and took out a thin file, and laid it on the desk in front of Gin, and turned it round so that he could read it.

It was his own file. The missing person file. Rei knew it. He had seen the cover a hundred times across a desk in September, the September before, while he stood there not asking. *UEDERA Gin. Field Operations. Reported missing 13/08/2015.* There was a red stamp across the corner now, and below it, on the last page, a single typed paragraph.

*Located 16/08/2016, Kita ward, by officers of this Division. In good physical health. Returned to duty 03/10/2016 at the request of the Director. No further action.*

Signed *Katagiri N., Senior Investigator*. Dated the Friday before.

Gin read it slowly, and then again. He looked up.

"You left out a year," he said.

"I left out what wasn't my department." Katagiri picked up the can of coffee and found it was empty and put it down again. "I'm an investigator. I find people. I found you. Where you were and what you were doing and who was doing it are for Field and Containment and the Director. They know. I don't need to."

"That isn't all you left out."

Katagiri did not say anything for a moment.

"No," she said. "It isn't." She looked at the file, not at him. "I knew where you were on the twentieth of July. The Family Mart at Akabane. The night boy. The rice balls. I knew on the twentieth of July, and I didn't take it to anybody until the twelfth of August, and then I only took it to your brother, in an envelope marked *not for file*." She turned her hand over on the desk. "There's a duty to report the location of a missing officer within twenty-four hours. It's in the regulations. Section nine. I've sat on two disciplinary panels for people who didn't." She looked at him then. "If I'd filed it on the twentieth of July, it would have gone to Public Confidence by the morning, because everything did that year. And by the evening you'd have been in a white room in Asagaya with a seal on the door, and your brother would have been the last to know."

Gin looked at her.

"I've been an investigator for sixteen years," said Katagiri. "I've never done that before. I don't know if it was the right thing. I'd do it again." She put her glasses back on. "That's all. You don't have to thank me. I'd rather you didn't."

Gin sat for a while with his hands on his knees.

Then he got up, and looked up at the strip light over her desk, which was flickering, as it had flickered for as long as Rei had known this office, and as, Rei suspected, it had flickered for as long as Gin had known it. He took the broken chair from beside the filing cabinet, the one with a leg that was shorter than the others, and set it under the light, and climbed up on it in his socks with his shoes on the carpet, and reached up and took the cover off the light and twisted the tube in its socket, a quarter turn, and back, and the flickering stopped.

The light came on steady and white over the desk.

Katagiri looked up at it.

"Sixteen years," she said.

"Six of them I've been meaning to," said Gin, and put the cover back on, and got down.

[break The radio]

On the way out of the building he stopped the lift at B3.

The evidence archive was as cold as ever. Rows of grey shelves going away under the humming lights, and the smell of dust and cardboard and old rubber. Gin walked straight to the third shelf in the row nearest the door without looking at the labels, as a man walks to his own front door in the dark. Between the box of children's shoes and the bag of old keys, the big silver and black radio sat where it had always sat, with its strip of masking tape across the top in his own handwriting. The tin of rice crackers Rei had left in the summer was beside it, still half full. Somebody had put the lid back on.

Gin crouched down in front of it. He did not touch it.

"Hello," he said. "Sorry I'm late."

For a long time there was nothing. Then, very faintly, without anybody touching the switch, the needle in the little window began to move. Static. A crackle. The needle went along the dial, slowly, past a station playing enka and a station selling insurance and a man reading the stock prices in a flat voice, and stopped.

A woman's voice. A weather forecast. Bright and professional.

*"...a long dry spell... now over..."*

The needle moved again. Static. Then a few bars of a children's song Rei half knew, from a programme everybody watched at six in the morning, and a chorus of small voices.

*"...welcome home... welcome home..."*

Gin put his hand over his mouth.

The needle moved once more. A long way this time, almost to the end of the dial, to a station that was mostly hiss, and a man speaking in English, very far away, very faint, about traffic on a bridge somewhere in another country. One word came out of it clear.

*"...brother..."*

Then the needle went slowly back to the middle of the dial and the radio fell quiet.

Gin stayed crouched in front of it for a long time. Then he took the lid off the tin of rice crackers and took one out and ate it, and put the lid back on, and stood up.

"He wants you to come down on Fridays," he said to Rei. "He says you were good company. He says you listen properly. He says I never did." He picked up his bag. "He's right. I was always in a hurry."

[break Ōtemachi]

The families met on the third Thursday of November, as they had every year for longer than anybody could say, on the forty-first floor of a dark glass tower in Ōtemachi.

Rei had been there once before, a year ago almost to the day, in Haruki's borrowed suit, with a tray of tea things, kneeling at the back by the wall while seven old people decided his brother was nobody's business. He had walked out of that building into the cold afterwards so angry that he had not been able to feel his hands.

He went back in the same suit. Haruki had told him to keep it.

The lobby was the same. The fountain. The white marble desk. The granite block outside with the name of the holding company on it, *Shichisei Holdings*, Seven Stars, a name you see on the side of delivery lorries and on the back of bank statements and never think about. The young woman at the desk looked at Haruki's face, and did not ask for anything, and pressed a button, and then she looked at Rei, and her eyes stayed on him for a second longer than they had last year.

The lift went up forty-one floors so smoothly that Rei only knew it had moved from his ears.

The hall was the same. A hundred tatami mats laid inside the top of a glass tower. The paper lamps. The old faded screens with cranes and pines on them, rescued from some house that had burned. The little garden of raked gravel and three stones behind the screens, and beyond it the windows, and beyond them the whole of Tokyo at night, lit to the edge of the bay. The air smelled of new tatami and green tea and, very faintly, of the incense somebody had lit in the corner before anybody arrived.

Seven heads knelt in a circle in the middle of the hall in formal black. Rei knelt at the back by the wall with Haruki and the tea things, and looked at them through his eyelashes, as he had last year.

They were not the same seven.

The tiny Tsukishiro grandmother was there, with her white hair to her waist and her eyes like black glass, in the same place. The broad red-faced Orimoto head. Higashiyama with his soft doctor's hands. Akiyama, who had apparently ended his family war, or paused it, and was sitting with his arms folded looking pleased with himself. The Fujiwara-Kage head with his silver glasses. And behind him, kneeling a little back from the circle as before, his son. Sena. Very neat. Very still. With a thin grey folder on the mat in front of his knees.

The quiet woman from the Nishimura house was not in the circle. In her place knelt a man of about seventy that Rei had never seen, thin and bent, in black, with his hands shaking slightly on his thighs.

And where Kuroda Sōun had knelt last year, very straight, with his face like a stone, knelt his elder son.

Itsuki was in formal black. Proper formal black, the five-crest kimono and the striped hakama, both of them his father's, taken in at the waist by Mrs. Fujii over three evenings with her mouth full of pins and her opinions entirely to herself. He had tied his hair back. He had scrubbed his hands for twenty minutes in the flat in Jūjō that afternoon and there was still a faint line of cobalt blue under the nail of his right thumb, and every so often, when nobody was looking, he looked at it. He knelt with his back straight and his hands on his thighs and his face very calm, and Rei, who had known him since the side of a swimming pool at twelve, could see that he was terrified.

He had not looked at the back of the hall once since he came in. He did not need to. Rei could feel him not looking, through the latch on his wrist, as plain as a hand on his shoulder.

[break The report]

For an hour they talked about money and old insults and a temple in Saitama. It was the same temple. Rei poured tea and his knees went numb.

Then the Tsukishiro grandmother tapped her small fan on the mat in front of her, once, and the hall went quiet.

"The Division," she said.

The screen at the side of the hall slid open.

It was not Hagiwara Mizuki who came in. Hagiwara Mizuki had resigned in September, in a two-line letter that Sena had read aloud to Rei and Kato in the canteen in the flat voice he used for things he found interesting, and had gone to live with her sister in Sendai, and nobody on the top floor had replaced her yet, and the glass doors up there stayed shut.

It was Adachi.

He came in slowly, in his grey suit, with a folder under one arm and no assistant, and knelt in the centre of the circle where she used to kneel, with some difficulty, because his knees were old and did not care for tatami. He set the folder down on the mat in front of him and did not open it.

"Director," said the Tsukishiro grandmother. "We did not expect you in person."

"No," said Adachi. "You have not had me in person since before most of your sons were born. I apologise for that. It was a mistake. The Division has been reporting to this council through an office that I let speak for me because it was easier. That office no longer exists." He folded his hands on the folder. "In August, I asked to meet the people I work for. Not this council. The others. The ones who are not on any register and who I had answered to, through other people, for twenty years without seeing a face. They agreed. There are eleven of them. I am not going to tell you their names, and you are not going to ask me." He looked round the circle, slowly. "They have asked me to say three things to this council, and I have agreed to say them, and then I am going to retire in March, and somebody younger and much less polite than I am will be saying things to you instead."

Nobody moved.

"The first," said Adachi, "is that the Division will report to this council from now on in person, by the Director, every year, and not through anybody else."

He did not wait for an answer.

"The second is that any vote this council takes about an officer of the Division will from now on be read aloud to that officer, in full, with the name of every house and how it voted, within one week." He paused. "That applies to past votes. I have the minutes for the last ten years. I have started reading them."

At the edge of the circle, the Fujiwara-Kage head's silver glasses caught the lamplight.

"And the third?" said the Tsukishiro grandmother.

"The third is not mine to say," said Adachi. "It is in an Auditor's report. And by the rules of this council an Auditor cannot speak here unless a head of house gives him the floor." He looked at the mat in front of him. "I have read the rules too."

There was a silence. Rei felt the whole hall go still round it, like a pond going still when something heavy has gone into it and the rings have not yet reached the edge.

Itsuki lifted his head.

"The Kuroda give the floor to the Auditor," he said.

His voice came out higher than he wanted. Rei felt him hear it, and hate it, and go on.

"If that's how it's done," said Itsuki. "I don't actually know how it's done. I've been doing this for six weeks. My father would know. He's at home in Nezu feeding the carp and he said if I rang him tonight he wouldn't answer." A pause. "So. The Kuroda give the floor to Fujiwara-Kage Sena. Please. If he'd like it."

The Fujiwara-Kage head turned his head, very slightly, toward his son.

Sena picked up the thin grey folder from the mat in front of his knees. He did not look at his father. He stood up, and walked forward into the circle, and knelt down beside Adachi in the centre, in front of all of them, and bowed. A small exact correct bow. Then he opened the folder.

[break What Sena read]

He read for eleven minutes. He did not raise his voice once.

He read the name of a paper mill outside Kyoto that had sold hand-laid mulberry paper to exactly nine old houses for two hundred years. He read the crest that was laid into a strip of that paper found on a civilian's gate in Kita on the twelfth of August. He read the name of the house the crest belonged to. He read the name of a deputy in the Office of Public Confidence who had collected that paper from the mill in person in June on a day he was signed off sick, and whose commuter pass had gone through the gates at Asagaya every second Sunday for fourteen months. He read the deputy's relationship to the head of that house. He read the date on which the deputy had been taken into Containment, the date on which the head of that house had resigned her seat on this council, and the names of the officers who had found what the deputy had in him. He read the name of an old man called Tanizaki Seiichi who had died on his own doorstep in Kita because of a strip of paper with *closed* written on it, and the date.

Then he read the minutes of the meeting of this council held on the third Thursday of November the year before. Item four. The Uedera matter. He read how each house had voted. He read his own father's words. *The matter is delicate. The directors are best placed.*

He closed the folder.

"That's the report," said Sena. "It's on file. Copies are with Internal Affairs and with the Director." He did not look up. "I would like it on the record that the Fujiwara-Kage approved the closure of an officer's file a year ago without reading it, on the word of an office that was being paid in paper by one of the houses in this room. I would like it on the record that I knew that by August, and filed it in August, and did not say it here until tonight because I was not allowed to." He paused. "I've been thinking about whether that's a reason. I've decided it isn't."

He bowed again and went back to his place behind his father and knelt down.

The Fujiwara-Kage head sat very still.

Then he took off his silver glasses, and folded them, and put them inside his sleeve. Without them his face was older, and tireder, and looked, Rei thought, very like his son's.

"The Fujiwara-Kage withdraw the vote of last November on the Uedera matter," he said. His voice was quite smooth. Only his hands, on his thighs, were not. "On the motion of the Auditor." He turned his head, and looked, for the first time all evening, at the back of the hall. At the two young men by the wall with the tea things. "And apologise to the officer's family. Who I see is present."

Rei put the teapot down very carefully on the tray so that it would not rattle.

[break The vote]

The thin old man who knelt in the Nishimura place did not speak through any of it. When it was finished, he bowed to the circle, very low, until his forehead nearly touched the mat, and stayed there, and the Tsukishiro grandmother let him stay there for a long time before she tapped her fan.

"Nishimura," she said, not unkindly. "Your sister has resigned the seat. You are here to hold it until the house names someone. You don't have to vote tonight."

"I'll vote," said the old man, into the mat. "Somebody in my house should vote properly. Once."

They voted on the three things Adachi had said. They went round the circle as they always had, and as they always had the Fujiwara-Kage went first, and then the Orimoto, and the Higashiyama, and the Nishimura, and the Akiyama. Every one of them said *approves*. The Akiyama head added that he wished to note that this was the first time in eleven years anybody had asked his house about anything sensible.

The Tsukishiro grandmother turned her black glass eyes across the circle.

"Kuroda?"

Itsuki did not answer at once.

He sat with his hands on his thighs and the line of cobalt under his thumbnail, and looked at the little garden of raked gravel and three stones through the gap in the screens, as his father had looked at it a year ago. Rei felt him think. He felt him go back to a basement corridor and a white room and ninety days of nobody coming, and to the word his family had for people like him, when they thought he had no gift and sent him to live on his own in a flat in Ueno with a monthly allowance. *Bones.* The ones who do not make it. The ones the house is very sad about and does not visit.

"The Kuroda approve," said Itsuki. "And the Kuroda would like to add something. If that's allowed. I'm not sure that's allowed either."

"It's allowed," said the grandmother, "if you're quick."

"I'll be quick." Itsuki took a breath. "From tonight the Kuroda don't buy paper anywhere that isn't on our books. And our books are going to the Division every year with everybody else's. Every yen. I've told the house. They're furious. My aunt cried." He swallowed. "And nobody in the Kuroda house is ever going to call anybody *bones of the trade* again. Not ours and not anybody else's. If one of ours hasn't got the gift, they're still ours, and if they get hurt we go and sit outside their door every day until they come out. My father said that last part. In September. I'm just repeating it so it's on the record. Everyone in here seems to like things on the record."

He stopped. His face had gone red to the ears.

"That's all," he said. "The Kuroda approve."

There was a short silence.

Then the Tsukishiro grandmother, very slowly, made a small sound in her throat that it took Rei a moment to recognise as a laugh.

"Your father," she said, "never once in forty years said anything in this room that he had not said in this room before." She tapped her fan on the mat. "Tsukishiro approves. And records the Kuroda addition. Seven and none." She looked round the circle with her black glass eyes. "Next."

[break The garden]

Afterwards they stood by the windows behind the screens, in front of the little garden of raked gravel and three stones, with the whole lit city under their feet. Haruki on one side of Rei with his hands in his pockets. Itsuki on the other, in his father's taken-in kimono, with his hands shaking so badly now that it was over that he had put them in his sleeves.

"I said *furious*," said Itsuki. "In there. About my own aunt. In front of the Tsukishiro."

"You did," said Haruki.

"I said *my aunt cried*."

"You did."

Itsuki put his forehead against the cold glass of the window and shut his eyes. Forty-one floors below, the taxis were going along the wide empty streets of Ōtemachi with their red lights on, and the Imperial Palace lay in its moat to the west, a great square of dark trees in the middle of all that light, with not one window lit in it.

Behind them, somebody coughed.

The Tsukishiro grandmother was standing at the edge of the screens. She was even smaller standing up. She came barely to Rei's chest. She had a cane of black lacquered bamboo and she leaned on it and looked up at the three of them with her black glass eyes, one after another, and stopped on Haruki.

"You," she said. "With the teapot. You come every year."

Haruki went very still.

"Every year for nine years," said the old woman. "With somebody's tray. Kneeling at the back. Pouring very good tea." She tilted her head. "I know whose boy you are. I knew your grandmother. You have her ears." She looked at him for a moment longer. "Next year, come in the front. Bring your own tea."

She turned round, leaning on the bamboo cane, and went away across the hundred tatami mats toward the lifts, slowly, alone.

Haruki stood looking after her with his mouth slightly open.

Itsuki took his forehead off the window. Rei took the teapot back to the tray by the wall, where they had left it, and emptied the cold tea into the slop bowl, and wrapped the pot in its cloth, and the three of them went down in the lift without saying anything, and out past the fountain and the white marble desk into the November night, where it had just begun to rain.
