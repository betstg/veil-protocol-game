#title Call Everyone

By half past nine the road above the freight yard was full of cars.

Okada's field cars, three of them, parked nose to tail along the broken kerb with their lights off. Shimizu's grey van. A taxi that had not left, because the driver had seen the white lights in the yard below and decided he would rather sit with his doors locked among people with lanyards than drive back through the dark alone. Chen Lian's bicycle against a lamp post. And people. Standing in twos and threes under the one working street lamp, talking in low voices, not looking at the yard.

Rei stood at the broken gate with his phone in his hand and Gorō on his shoulder and did not know who to ring first.

So he rang Kita police station, and asked for Inspector Kumagai, and said it was the boy whose brother was probably at a love hotel.

[break Kumagai]

It took four minutes. Kumagai was not at his desk. He was at home. They put Rei through anyway.

"Uedera," said Kumagai. His voice was exactly the same as a year ago. Tired. Flat. A little rough at the edges, like a man who has drunk too much canned coffee for thirty years. There was a television on behind him, and a woman's voice asking who it was. "You took your time."

"You said to come and tell you what you got wrong."

"I did." A pause. A door closing, and the television going quiet. "Go on, then."

Rei told him. Not everything. Enough. That his brother had not been at a love hotel. That something had taken him. That there was an old freight yard past Tabata, by the river, where tonight a great many people had gathered who should not be there, and that some of them were not in their right minds, and that before midnight things would happen in that yard that nobody in a police uniform should see.

Kumagai did not say anything for a long time.

"I've been in this job thirty-one years," he said at last. "You know how many reports I've got in my drawer that say *gas leak*? Or *heat stroke*? When I was there and I saw it and it wasn't any of those things?" A pause. "A drawer full. I keep them. Don't know why." Another pause. "What do you want, Uedera?"

"The roads," said Rei. "Into the yard. From Tabata, and from the river path. Closed. For a gas leak. Now. Nobody gets through who's just walking home from the graves."

Kumagai breathed out through his nose.

"There's a gas main under that whole stretch," he said slowly. "Old one. Been meaning to have it checked for years. I'd be failing in my duty." Rei heard keys. "Twenty minutes. Ono'll be on the cordon. She'll be thrilled. She's been asking about you." His voice did something that was not quite a laugh. "Uedera. When it's done. Come and tell me the rest of what I got wrong. All of it. I'll buy the coffee."

[break Shimizu]

Shimizu came up the road from the van while he was still holding the phone.

She was in her field clothes, dark trousers and a dark jacket with the sleeves pushed up, and her face was very still. Behind her came Moriya on his crutch, and Kato with his hand in his pocket, and Usami in a cardigan much too warm for August with a paper bag of something in his arms, and the tired Containment officer Nagai a careful thirty metres behind Usami, as the paper said.

Shimizu stopped in front of Rei.

"The Director rang me at eight," she said. "From his office. He told me where to bring my team and why." She did not raise her voice. Shimizu never raised her voice. "He told me about your brother. He told me about the cemetery. He told me about the room on the sixth floor that I didn't know existed." She looked at him. "Kato knew. Itsuki knew. Mori knew. A man with a dog knew. The Director knew. Your training officer heard it from the Director."

"I didn't want you to have to choose," said Rei. "Between the rules and me. I thought if you didn't know, you couldn't get in trouble for it."

Shimizu looked at him for a long moment.

"That," she said, "is the stupidest thing you've ever said to me." She took a breath. "I am your team leader, Uedera. My job is to keep you alive while you do something insane. It is not to be kept safe from what you're doing. You don't get to protect me. That isn't how it works. It has never been how it works."

Rei could not say anything.

"I've looked at the yard," said Shimizu. "I've counted. Four hundred people, give or take, sitting on the tracks with flowers. Some with threads on them, some just lost. A great many dead. Five ways this goes wrong that I can see from the road." The small line between her eyebrows went very deep. "I'm going to need you to tell me your plan. All of it. Then I'm going to tell you which parts of it are going to get people killed. And then we're going to do it anyway, because it's the only plan anybody's got." She turned to the others. "Usami. What's in the bag?"

"Onigiri," said Usami. "Forty. I didn't know how many people. I'm sorry." He held the bag out to Rei, and his eyes came round first and his head after. "Ren says you haven't eaten since the morning. Ren says to eat two."

Rei ate two. Standing at the broken gate, with the dead drifting past below in the dark, he ate two rice balls with umeboshi in them, the small wrinkled red pickles that every grandmother in Japan pushes into the middle of a rice ball, salty and so sour they make your mouth shut, and they were the best thing he had ever eaten in his life.

[break The Grid]

The Grid came on foot, from the river path, after Ono had closed the road.

Switch first, in black, with their square face set and their signet ring turned inward. Then a dozen others Rei half knew from the Gathering, and some he did not. A man with tattoos up both arms who had cried through Tanizaki's sutras. Two old women with shopping trolleys full of salt. And at the back, with her hood up in thirty-two degrees and her sleeves pulled down over her hands, Choi Hana.

She came and stood beside him at the gate and looked down at the yard. At the white lights. At the people on the tracks.

"Saki's down there," she said. "The girl from the alley. The one who wanted to be an astronaut. I can see her from here. Sitting in a freight car." She did not look at him. "I want to see the face of the thing that poured her tea."

"You'll see it," said Rei.

Hana was quiet for a while. Her left sleeve had ridden up, the way it always did when she put her hands in her pockets, and the edge of the thick pale scar showed on the inside of her forearm.

"If this goes badly," she said, "there's something I want to..."

Rei's phone rang.

Loud. Shrill. The cheap default tune. In the quiet at the gate it was like a scream.

Hana stopped. Her face closed. Like a door.

"Answer it," she said. She pulled the sleeve down over the scar. "It's Kato. He's standing three metres away and he's ringing you. It's something."

It was Kato, three metres away, with his phone to his ear and his other hand over his mouth.

"Tsuji's coming," said Kato. "From the post room. With the cats. All five. In carriers. In a taxi." His voice was strange. "He says they can see the far shore better than any of us, and they can cross it and come back, and that's what they're for. He says Kuga got into his carrier on his own."

Rei looked back at Hana.

She was already walking away down the line of the fence, toward the river end, with her hands in her pockets.

"Later," she said, without turning round. "When we're both still alive."

[break Everyone]

By eleven his phone had rung thirty-one times.

Chen Lian, from the round room in Ochanomizu. *We're all in our chairs. Every one of us who can sit. Holding. Every light in the city as bright as we can make it. We'll hold the city. You hold the river.* And then, after a pause, *Mori isn't here. Mori's on his way to you. He said if anybody tried to stop him he'd set fire to their chair.*

Haruki, who did not ring. He sent a photograph. A long wooden practice sword with writing carved down the blade and white paper wound round the handle, lying on the counter of the bar in Nezu next to a flask of coffee. No words. Then, a minute later, *on my way.*

Keiko, who said every bar and café between Ōji and Akabane had its doors shut and its old men inside, and that she had threatened two owners to get it done.

Hara Setsu, who was standing six metres away by Okada's car, rang him anyway, to say that every bath house in Kita had its doors locked till morning and every old man in every bath house had a box of salt in his hand, out of the eleven sacks Rei had sent her in July, and that she had started on the next ward by telephone.

Sena, who said in his quiet measured voice that he was on the road above the yard, observing, as the Director had asked, and that he had filed a form declaring himself present in a personal capacity, and that the form was accurate.

Kurosawa, who Rei had never met, who rang from a number he did not know and said, in a deep, slow, unhurried voice, *The Director asked. I'm forty minutes away. I'm bringing two. Don't start without us,* and hung up.

Majima, who said *I'm at the gate. I can see you. Eat something,* and hung up.

Itsuki rang last.

"I'm in Nezu," he said. He sounded very calm. Too calm. Rei had heard that calm before, at the Kuroda house, before the threshold. "At home. Outside his door."

Rei sat down slowly on the kerb under the lamp. "Itsu..."

"Sōun can open gates I can't even see," said Itsuki. "Big ones. Old ones. The kind that go through walls and rivers and whole districts. He's the only one in this city who can." A pause. "He hasn't spoken to me since he called the Division on me. Not really. He bows to me. In car parks. Like I'm somebody important." A short laugh, not a real one. "I'm going to go in and ask him for something. I've never asked him for anything in my life."

"Do you want me to come?"

"No." Itsuki was quiet for a moment. "This one I've got to do on my own. Like you and the chair." A pause. "I'll be at the yard before midnight. If he says no, I'll still be there. Just with less."

Rei swallowed. "Itsuki."

"What."

"Thank you," said Rei. "For everything. Since I was twelve. I never said."

There was a very long silence on the line.

"Don't," said Itsuki. His voice cracked straight through the middle. "Don't you dare say goodbye to me on the phone. I'll see you before midnight." And he hung up.

[break The bank]

At half past eleven, Rei went down to the river.

Not through the yard. Along the outside of the fence, through the weeds, to where the old tracks ran down into the water. He went alone, except for Gorō, because there was one more person to ask and he did not think that person would come up to a road full of cars.

The bank there was steep and overgrown, and below it the river ran black and wide and slow, with the lights of Saitama on the far side and the railway bridge upstream like a black ladder laid across it. There were white lights drifting along the near bank, slow and patient, coming down from the yard toward the place where the rails went under.

And on a concrete block at the edge of the water, sitting with his knees up and his chin on them, there was a boy.

About ten. In shorts and a striped T-shirt, both soaked, dripping, though it had not rained for a week. Barefoot. His hair plastered to his head. Beside him, lying on the concrete with its chin on its paws, a small brown dog, also wet, also dripping, with one ear inside out.

Everybody on this stretch of the river knew about Haruto. Rei had passed him a dozen times with Itsuki on the way to somewhere else, and nodded, and Haruto had nodded back. He had jumped in after his dog a long time ago, and they had both drowned, and he had never gone. He sat on the bank and made sure nobody went in after anything else. People walking at night sometimes saw the dog. Nobody minded.

Haruto looked up.

"You're the one with the crow," he said. "You walk past with the painter."

"Yes."

"You're not walking past."

"No." Rei crouched down on the bank, below him, so their faces were level. "There's a lady up in the yard. With tea. She's put lights out. She's going to try and walk everybody into your river at midnight."

Haruto looked up the bank at the white lights coming down. His face did not change. It was a ten-year-old's face, and very old.

"I know," he said. "She's been putting flowers on my bank every morning for a week. White ones. I throw them in. They come back." He scowled. "Nobody goes in my river."

"Not tonight," said Rei.

"Not ever. That's the rule." Haruto put his hand on the dog's wet back. "It's dangerous. People think it's slow. It isn't. It's fast underneath." He looked at Rei. "What do you want?"

"When it starts," said Rei. "When the dead come down this bank. Don't let them go in the water. Send them up the rails instead. Into the yard. Toward her door. All of them. They'll listen to you. You've been here longer than any of them."

Haruto thought about it, with his chin on his knees.

"Then what?"

"Then they go home," said Rei. "Through her door. The right way. All together. So nothing can come in the other way while they're going out."

Haruto looked at him for a long time.

"I could go too," he said. "Through the door. Home."

"Yes. If you wanted."

Haruto looked down at the dog. The dog looked up at him with its inside-out ear.

"Maybe," he said. "After. If Chibi wants." He stood up on the concrete block, dripping, small and thin and absolutely certain. "Nobody goes in my river. Not the dead either. I'll send them up the rails." He looked at Rei. "You owe me a game. After. Tag. The painter too. Nobody's played with me for ages."

"After," said Rei. "I promise."

Haruto nodded, as if that settled it, and sat back down on his block with his hand on the dog, and looked up the bank at the white lights coming.

Rei climbed back up through the weeds.

At the top, by the fence, he looked at his phone. Eleven forty-one.

Up on the road, under the one working lamp, everybody he had rung was standing together in a loose crowd, not quite talking. Shimizu with her arms folded. Moriya on his crutch. Kato with his knight. Usami handing out the last of the rice balls. Okada with her notebooks. Hara Setsu with her needles. The Grid along the fence. Hana a little apart, smoking. Mori arriving, big and hot and silent, with the air shimmering round him. Haruki's taxi pulling up behind the others, and Haruki getting out of it in a sweater in August with a long wooden sword in one hand and a flask in the other.

And a little apart from all of them, by the wall where the light did not reach, standing very straight in a grey jacket, a tall man who was not looking at any of them, and was looking at the yard.

Rei put his hand in his pocket on the old dark pager. The orange light beat against his palm, slow and hot.

*Brave people,* it had said. *I would like to see brave people again.*

He took it out and held it up, so that the screen faced the road.

The screen lit. One word, in the fast jagged hand.

*YES.*
