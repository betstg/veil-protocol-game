#title Call Everyone

He woke on Saturday morning in a bed.

Not the sofa. Gin's bed. In Gin's room, which he had not slept in once in a year, because it was Gin's, and he had kept the door shut and the curtains drawn and the duvet folded exactly the way it had been folded on the morning of the twelfth of August. Last night he had come home from the freight yard at one in the morning and stood in the doorway for a long time and then gone in and lain down on top of the folded duvet in his clothes, with his feet on the mattress and nothing hanging off anything, and slept for six hours without dreaming.

Gorō was on the windowsill. He had pulled the curtain open with his beak. The sun was coming in hard and white across the floor.

Rei lay there and looked at the ceiling for a moment. Gin's ceiling. The crack in the corner Gin had been meaning to fill for five years.

Then he got up, and put the kettle on, and ate two rice balls standing at the counter, and picked up his phone.

[break Shimizu]

He rang Shimizu first. Because she was his training officer. Because she did not know. Because it was wrong that she did not know.

She answered on the first ring. She always did.

Her voice came flat and awake down the line. "Uedera."

"Shimizu-san. I need to tell you something. It's going to take a while." He held the phone very tightly. "And you're going to be angry."

There was a short silence on the line.

"I'm coming to you," said Shimizu. "Put the kettle on. Don't tell me anything on the phone."

She came in forty minutes, in her weekend clothes, which Rei had never seen, a grey T-shirt and dark jeans that made her look younger and more tired. Kuma came with her. The big red Akita limped in through the door on his bad back leg, with his red and white silk cord round his neck, and went straight to Rei and put his head on Rei's knee, the way he had the first day, in January, and left it there.

Shimizu sat at the kitchen table in Gin's chair and listened to all of it. The roof. The name. The garden. The minute on the walkway. Watanabe. The freight yard. The white lights. The Director's phone call.

She did not interrupt once. She sat with her hands flat on the table and her eyes on Rei's face and her face completely still, the way it went when she was reading the structure of something, the way a building's load ran down through its walls.

When he finished, she did not say anything for a long time.

At last she lifted her hands an inch off the table and set them down again. "You didn't tell me."

Rei made himself hold her eyes. "No."

"Kato knew. Itsuki knew. Mori knew. A man with a dog knew. The Director knew." She did not raise her voice. Shimizu never raised her voice. "Your training officer didn't."

"I didn't want you to have to choose," said Rei. "Between the rules and me. I thought, if you didn't know, you couldn't get in trouble for it."

Shimizu looked at him.

"That," she said, "is the stupidest thing you've ever said to me. And you once told me a ghost dog had a better sense of direction than the Mirror Room." She stood up. She went to the sink and filled a glass of water and drank it, standing with her back to him, the way Adachi stood at his window. "I am your training officer, Uedera. My job is to keep you alive while you learn to do something insane. It is not to be kept safe from what you're doing. You don't get to protect me. That's not how it works. That's not how it has ever worked."

She put the glass down.

She did not look at the dog. "Kuma."

The big dog lifted his head off Rei's knee.

"We're going to a freight yard tonight," said Shimizu. She turned round. Her face was still and grey and absolutely furious. "Both of us. And so is Kato, and so is Aoi, who came off her crutches on Tuesday and will not thank you for leaving her out, and so is Moriya, who has been waiting for nine months for an excuse to go somewhere the ninth floor can't see. And so is Fujiwara-Kage, God help us, because if I don't take him he'll write a report about why I didn't." She picked up her bag. "Six o'clock. My car. Bring iron. And wear a different shirt." She stopped at the door. "That one's wrong."

[break Kumagai]

He rang Kita police station at eleven and asked for Inspector Kumagai.

It took twenty minutes. A young officer put him on hold three times and asked his name twice and asked what it was regarding and Rei said *tell him it's the boy whose brother was probably at a love hotel*, and there was a long silence, and then a click.

"Uedera," said Kumagai. His voice was exactly the same. Tired. Flat. A little rough at the edges, like a man who has drunk too much canned coffee for thirty years. "You took your time."

Rei turned to face the window, the phone hot against his ear. "You said to come and tell you what you got wrong."

"I did." A pause. Rei heard a chair creak. "Go on, then."

Rei told him.

Not everything. Not the name. Not the chair. But enough. That his brother had not been at a love hotel. That something had taken him. That there was an old freight yard past Tabata, by the river, where tonight a great many people were going to gather who should not be there, and that some of them would not be in their right minds, and that there would be things happening in that yard that nobody in a police uniform should see.

Kumagai did not say anything for a long time.

"I've been in this job thirty-one years," he said at last. "You know how many reports I've got in my drawer that say *gas leak*? Or *heat stroke*? Or *person under the influence*? When I was there and I saw it and it wasn't any of those things?" Another creak. "A drawer full. A whole drawer. I keep them. Don't know why." A pause. "What do you want, Uedera?"

"The roads," said Rei. "Into the yard. From the south. From Tabata. If they were closed. For a gas leak. From six o'clock. Nobody gets through who's just walking a dog."

Kumagai breathed out through his nose.

"There's a gas main under that whole stretch," he said slowly. "Old one. Been meaning to have it checked for years. Very dangerous. I'd be failing in my duty if I didn't close the roads." A pause. "Six o'clock?"

Rei nodded at nobody. "Six o'clock."

"Ono'll be on the cordon. She'll be thrilled. She's been asking about you." Kumagai's voice did something that was not quite a laugh. He cleared his throat. "Uedera."

Rei waited. "Yes."

"When it's done. Come and tell me the rest of what I got wrong. All of it. I'll buy the coffee."

[break Haruto]

Haruto was waiting at the bottom of the hill when Rei went out at noon. Sitting on the low wall by the vending machines in a sleeveless T-shirt and his school trousers, with his hair bleached at the tips and a can of melon soda in his hand, pretending he had not been waiting.

"Itsuki said you're doing something tonight," he said. He did not look at Rei. "Something big."

Rei leaned on the vending machine beside him. "Itsuki talks too much."

"Itsuki said I'm not coming." Haruto drank his soda. "I'm coming."

Rei did not even look round. "You're not."

"I'm seventeen." Haruto crushed the can in one hand, not very well. "I can see the stuff. You know I can. Since the river. Since that thing in the water. I see it all the time now. You're not the only one."

Rei sat down on the wall beside him.

He thought about what Gin would say. He knew exactly what Gin would say. Gin would say *no* in a voice like a door shutting, and Haruto would go anyway, the way Rei had always gone anyway, and something would happen to him in the dark that nobody could take back.

So he did not say no.

He looked down the hill at the station, at the heat coming up off the road. "You know what Obon is?"

Haruto snorted. "Course."

Rei waited, with his eyes on the station. "Tell me."

"The dead come home. You light a fire on the step so they can find the house. You put out food." Haruto shrugged. "My mum does it. When she's not on nights. Cucumber horse. Aubergine cow. All that."

"Tonight," said Rei, "something's lit a lot of fires in the wrong place. Down by the river. Big ones. Bright ones. The dead are going to follow them instead of the ones on the steps. And if they get there, they don't come home. Ever." He looked at Haruto. "The only thing that'll pull them back is more fires. Real ones. On real steps. More than usual. A lot more. All over Kita. Every building. Every door."

Haruto looked at him. The bravado had gone out of his face, just a little, at the edges.

"In my building," said Rei, "there are forty flats. Most of them old people. Most of them won't bother this year. Too tired. Too hot. Can't get down the stairs." He took a box of matches out of his pocket and held it out. "I need someone to go door to door. All forty. And make them light one. Carry the matches if you have to. Light it for them. And then do the next building. And the next." He paused. "Hoshino's going to do the same on Sakae Street. A woman called Hara Setsu is going to do every bath house in Kita. A woman called Keiko is doing the bars in Ni-chōme. It's the most important job there is tonight. It's not down by the river. It's here. On the steps."

Haruto looked at the matches for a long time.

He took the box and turned it over in his palm and made a face. "That's a shit job."

Rei stood up off the wall. "It's the only one that matters."

Haruto stuffed the box in his pocket. He stood up.

"Forty flats," he said. "Then the next building." He started up the hill. Then he stopped, and turned round, and pointed at Rei with the crushed can. "You come back. Yeah? You come back, or I'm telling Itsuki you lied."

Rei lifted a hand to him. "I'll come back."

"You'd better." He went up the hill toward the building, and did not look back, and was already shouting at somebody's grandmother on the second-floor walkway before he reached the stairs.

[break Hana]

Choi Hana was on the roof of the building above the Korean grocer in Shin-Ōkubo, where she lived, sitting on an upturned crate with her hood up in thirty-four degrees, smoking.

She did not get up when Rei came out of the stairwell. She looked at him from under the hood. Her left sleeve had ridden up, the way it always did when she lifted the cigarette, and the edge of the thick pale scar showed on the inside of her forearm.

"Inoue told me," she said. "Not the Hunger one. The other one. Misaki. She said something's coming down at the old freight yard. She said she's going." She blew smoke. "Said to tell you she doesn't take orders from the Division, and she doesn't take orders from you, and she's going because she wants to see what a far shore looks like close up."

Rei stayed by the stairwell door. "Are you coming?"

Hana looked at the city. At the rooftops going away grey and white in the heat, and the water tanks, and the washing on the balconies.

"You know why I don't do Division work," she said. "Never have."

Rei shook his head. "No."

"No. You don't." She looked down at her arm. At the scar. She did not pull the sleeve down. "When I was fourteen. There was a man. A teacher. I told you that. Back in the spring. I never told you the rest." She took a long breath, and her fingers found the edge of the scar and rested on it. "The rest is..."

Rei's phone rang.

Loud. Shrill. The cheap default tune. In the silence on the roof it was like a scream.

Hana stopped. Her face closed. Just like that. Like a door.

Rei grabbed the phone to kill it. It was Kato. He looked at the name on the screen. He looked at Hana.

"Answer it," said Hana. She pulled the sleeve down over the scar. "It's Kato. He wouldn't ring if it wasn't something."

Rei held the ringing phone away from him. "Hana..."

"Later." She stood up and ground out the cigarette on the crate. "When we're both still alive. I'll tell you then." She walked past him to the stairwell. At the door, she stopped. "I'm coming. Not for the Division. Not for you." She did not look round. "For the girl in the alley in the spring. The one who wanted to be an astronaut. Saki. She drank that tea too." A pause. "I want to see the face of the thing that poured it."

She went down the stairs.

Rei answered the phone.

"Katagiri," said Kato. His voice was strange. "She's at the Division. In the Cattery. She's got all five of them in their carriers. Sera. Itou. Mochida. Hara. Kuga." A pause. "She says she's bringing them. To the yard. She says the cats can see the far shore better than any of us, and they can cross it, and come back, and that's what they're for." Another pause. "She says she knows you won't want her there. She says she's coming anyway. She says to tell you Kuga turned round this morning."

Rei closed his eyes.

He opened his eyes on the bright roof. "Tell her. Tell her six o'clock."

[break Everyone]

By five o'clock his phone had rung thirty-one times.

Okada, who said only *I'll be there* and hung up.

Moriya, who said he would bring a pen and a notebook, in case anybody needed anything written down afterwards for the record, and that he had told his children he would be home late.

Aoi, who shouted down the phone for a full minute about being left out and then said *thank you, thank you for calling me, I thought you'd forgotten I existed*, and cried.

Sena, who said in his quiet measured voice that he would be attending in a personal capacity and that he had already filed a form declaring himself on leave, and that the form was accurate.

Chen Lian, who said the Thirteen would be in the round room. All of them. In their chairs. Holding. Every light in the city as bright as they could make it. *We'll hold the city,* she said. *You hold the river.* And Mori would not be in the round room. Mori would be at the yard. Mori had said, very loudly, in front of everyone, that if anybody tried to stop him he would set fire to their chair.

Haruki, who did not ring. He sent one message. A photograph of a long wooden sword with carved writing down the blade and white paper wound round the handle, lying on the counter of the bar in Nezu next to a flask of coffee. No words.

Keiko, who said the bars in Ni-chōme would all have fires on their steps by eight, and that she had already threatened two of the owners.

Hara Setsu, who said every bath house in Kita would have a fire at its door by dusk, and every old man in every bath house would have a box of matches in his hand, and that she had started on the next ward.

Majima, who said *seven o'clock, the gate*, and nothing else, and hung up, and then rang back ten seconds later and said *eat something*, and hung up again.

Itsuki, who rang last.

"I'm going home," he said. He sounded very calm. Too calm. Rei had heard that calm before, at the Kuroda house, in December, before the threshold. "To Nezu. To my father."

Rei sat down slowly on the kitchen floor. "Itsu..."

"Sōun can open gates I can't even see," said Itsuki. "Big ones. Old ones. The kind that go through walls and rivers and whole districts. He's the only one in this city who can." A pause. "He hasn't spoken to me since I left the hospital. Not really. He bows to me. In car parks. Like I'm somebody important." A short laugh, not a real one. "I'm going to go and ask him for something. I've never asked him for anything in my life."

Rei pressed his free hand flat against the cupboard door. "Do you want me to come?"

"No." Itsuki was quiet for a moment. "This one I've got to do on my own. Like you and the chair. Like Gin and the thing." A pause. "I'll see you at the yard. Seven. If he says no, I'll still be there. Just with less."

Rei swallowed. "Itsuki."

A breath on the line, impatient and frightened. "What."

Rei looked at the kitchen. At the hook by the door. At the window frame with its layers of grey pencil. At Gin's chair, and Shimizu's empty water glass on the table, and Gorō on the back of the chair with his feathers flat and his blue eye on the window.

"Thank you," he said. "For everything. Since I was twelve. I never said."

There was a very long silence on the line.

"Don't," said Itsuki. His voice cracked straight through the middle. "Don't you dare say goodbye to me on the phone, you absolute bastard. I'll see you at seven." And he hung up.

[break Six o'clock]

At a quarter to six, Rei stood in the genkan with his shoes on and a jar of iron filings in each pocket of his jacket and Gin's key round his neck on its cord, under a clean shirt.

He looked at the brass hook by the door. Empty. The way it had been empty for a year. *Come home*, said the faint grey mark on the wall underneath it.

He did not hang the key on it. Gin had said not to.

He put his hand flat on the wall, over the mark, for a moment.

Then he opened the door.

On the walkway outside, all along the third floor, in front of every door, in little clay dishes and old frying pans and a cake tin and an ashtray, someone had set out small piles of hemp stalks for the evening fires. Forty doors. Forty little piles, ready to be lit at dusk. On the step of the Yamadas', two doors down, there was a cucumber with four toothpick legs, and an aubergine with four more, standing side by side, a fast horse to bring the dead home and a slow cow to take them back.

At the top of the stairs, sitting on the top step with a box of matches in his lap and his bleached hair sticking up, Haruto looked up at him.

"Forty," he said. "Done. Starting on the next building at seven." He stuck his chin out. "Told you."

Rei went down the stairs past him. At the bottom, he looked back up.

Haruto was watching him. The bravado had gone completely. He was seventeen, and sitting on a step with a box of matches, and frightened.

Haruto gripped the matchbox in both hands. "Come back. Yeah?"

Rei looked up at him for a long moment, the way Gin used to look up from the corner at the bottom of the hill, at a boy on a walkway, waiting.

He lifted his hand, the one without the key, and held it up to the boy on the stairs. "Light the fires. I'll follow them home."
