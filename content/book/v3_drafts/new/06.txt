#title The Warm Side of the Line

On the last night of Obon the lanterns went down the river without them.

Rei did not go. He sat on the balcony with the cold key in his hand and watched the sky over the rooftops go orange and then purple and then black, and somewhere down there by the Sumida thousands of small paper lights were being set on the water to carry the dead back where they came from, and his brother had promised.

At nine o'clock his phone buzzed. It was Haruto. No words. Just a photograph, taken from the river bank, a little blurred, of the dark water covered in floating lanterns, hundreds of them, warm and yellow, drifting away under a bridge.

A minute later, a second message.

*went anyway. its ok. next year.*

Rei looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote back, *Next year. I promise.* And then he sat there feeling sick, because *promise* was Gin's word, and Gin had used it, and look.

[break A week]

For a week, Rei did what Tanizaki had told him to do. He slept. He ate. It was much harder than it sounded.

He went back to work on Monday. Nobody at the police station mentioned the front desk. Ono said *Morning, kid!* exactly as cheerfully as always and slid a box of files across the counter to him, and only the tips of his ears went a little pink. Kumagai did not call him in. Arai passed him once on the stairs and looked at his hands and said nothing. Rei sorted files and made copies and carried boxes, and took the long way to work, round by the river, so that he did not have to stand on the platform at Ōji.

He cooked rice and ate it standing up at the counter. He read Gin's book at night, a chapter at a time, in order, the way Gin had asked. He did not call the CVD again. He kept Okada's card in his wallet, behind his train pass, with her own number facing in.

Itsuki came round three times that week, and each time he brought food and a new drawing and did not ask anything until Rei was ready, which was never, and then he asked anyway. Once he fell asleep on the sofa with his shoes on, and Rei took them off for him and put a blanket over him, and stood there for a moment in the dark holding the blanket's corner, because that was what Gin did, and he had not known until that second that he had learned it.

[break Thursday]

On Thursday morning he woke at six without an alarm and knew where he was going before he was properly awake.

It was the twentieth of August. On the page in Gin's book for the bakery in Jūjō, there were only three words. *Go on Thursdays.*

Jūjō is two stops north of Ōji on the Saikyō line. Rei rode up with a carriage full of schoolchildren in summer club uniforms, carrying baseball bats in long canvas bags. The Saikyō line trains have a green stripe and are newer than the Keihin-Tōhoku ones, and the seats are softer. A girl next to him had fallen asleep against her friend's shoulder with her mouth open. Her friend was reading a manga about a volleyball team and mouthing the words silently, with tremendous concentration.

[need ch6_jujo_morning | Jūjō Ginza covered shopping arcade at 7am, shutters rolling up, fishmonger hosing the street, grandmothers in sun hats, a small bakery with a faded blue awning]

Jūjō Ginza is a shopping street. Lots of old neighbourhoods in Tokyo have a street called *something Ginza*, after the famous expensive shopping district in the middle of the city. It is a small joke. The real Ginza has jewellers and department stores and handbags that cost more than a car. The Ginza in Jūjō has a fishmonger, three greengrocers, a shop that sells only socks, a pharmacy, a tea shop, a shop that sells only brooms and buckets, and about forty shops that sell fried food. It is a long narrow street with a glass roof over it to keep the rain off. It is famous all over Tokyo for being cheap. Old women come from three wards away to buy croquettes for eighty yen.

At seven in the morning the arcade was just waking up. Shutters rolled up one by one with a rattle. A man was hosing down the pavement in front of the fish shop, and the water ran in a silver line down the middle of the street. The smell of frying oil had already started. Two grandmothers in sun hats walked arm in arm, very slowly, examining the vegetables as if they were judging a competition. A tabby cat sat on a stack of plastic crates and washed its face with one paw.

The bakery was halfway down on the left. It had a blue awning with KONDŌ BAKERY on it in white letters faded almost to grey. The window was full of trays. Melon bread with crackly sugar crusts. Curry bread in fat golden pillows. And anpan. Round, soft, brown buns, each with a single black sesame seed pressed into the top, full of sweet red bean paste.

A man in his sixties stood behind the counter with flour up to his elbows. He had a round face and an easy smile and a white towel tied round his head.

"Anpan?" he said, before Rei had opened his mouth. "Thursday. Everybody wants anpan on a Thursday. I don't know why. Nobody knows why. Forty years and nobody's ever told me."

"Two, please," said Rei.

Mr. Kondō put them in a paper bag and folded the top over twice with his thumbs, very neatly, and talked the whole time. About the weather, which was going to be hot again. About the man on the television who said it was the hottest August in ten years. About his wife, who wanted an air conditioner for the bedroom, and him, who thought the fan was perfectly fine. "A fan is a fan," he said, and handed over the bag as if that settled it for all time.

Rei liked him at once.

He paid, and went and stood outside on the street with the warm bag in his hands, and waited.

[break The detour]

At half past seven Mr. Kondō came out of the bakery carrying an empty plastic crate and walked off toward the far end of the arcade, to fetch more flour from a storeroom.

Rei watched him go. And he saw it.

Halfway along, outside a shop with its shutter down and an old sign over it that said OKAMURA WATCHES AND CLOCKS, Mr. Kondō stepped to the right. He did it smoothly, without looking, the way you step round a puddle you have stepped round a thousand times. He walked in a small curve round a patch of the floor about the size of a dinner table, and then stepped back into the middle of the street and carried on.

There was nothing on the floor there. No puddle. No crack. The same grey tiles as everywhere else.

Rei watched the other people. A woman with a pushchair came from the other direction. When she got to the closed watch shop, she steered the pushchair round the same patch of floor, in the same small curve, without looking down. A boy on a bicycle did it. A delivery man with a trolley stacked with cardboard boxes did it. Even the tabby cat, when it jumped down off the crates and trotted up the arcade, swung wide round that one spot with its tail held low, and then went back to the middle.

Nobody stepped on that patch of floor. And nobody knew they were not stepping on it.

Rei walked slowly up to the closed watch shop, stopped at the edge of the patch, and let the door in his chest open. Just a crack.

It was cold.

Not the deep wet cold of the platform. A smaller cold, like the air that comes out when you open the fridge. And with it came a feeling that landed on him all at once, so strongly that he had to put his hand flat against the shutter to stay upright.

It was the feeling of waiting. Of waiting for a very long time for somebody who was late. Checking the time. Checking it again. Telling yourself, they'll be here in a minute. Five more minutes. Ten. Of the clock moving on, and the person not coming, and not coming, and never coming.

He opened the door a little wider.

[need ch6_old_woman | In a morning shopping arcade, the faded pale figure of a small old woman in a wrongly buttoned brown cardigan and flowered apron, empty shopping basket, looking at her bare wrist as if at a watch, in front of a closed watch shop, shoppers unconsciously curving around her]

The arcade went pale and thin, like a photograph left too long in a sunny window. And in the middle of the patch of floor, there was an old woman.

She was small and bent. She had on a brown cardigan buttoned wrong, one button too low, and a flowered apron over a long grey skirt. Her hair was white and thin and pinned up with a single plastic clip. She held a shopping basket over one arm, with nothing in it. She stood facing the far end of the arcade, toward the street outside, and every few seconds she lifted her wrist and looked at it. There was no watch on her wrist. She looked anyway.

She did not look frightening. She looked like any grandmother in any shopping street in Japan. That was what made Rei's throat close up.

"Excuse me," he said quietly. "Grandmother?"

She turned her head. Her eyes were cloudy and pale, and they did not quite find him. They settled on a spot a little to the left of his face.

"He's late," she said. Her voice was very thin and very far away, like a voice on a telephone in another room. "He went to buy cigarettes. He said, *I'll be ten minutes.* He's never late. Never in his life." She looked at her empty wrist. "Have you seen him? He's tall. He wears a grey hat. He always wears the grey hat, even when it's hot, I tell him he'll cook his brains."

Rei did not know what to say. He had read about this in Gin's book, two nights ago. He tried to remember the page. *Spirits held in place by an unfinished expectation. Not dangerous. Not able to leave. They need the expectation answered, or released.*

"What day is it?" he asked her, gently.

She looked at her wrist. "Thursday," she said. "It's Thursday. We always come on a Thursday. For the anpan. He likes the anpan from Kondō's. He eats one walking, before we've even paid, and the boy laughs at him."

Behind Rei, a shutter rattled up. Somebody laughed. A radio started playing in the greengrocer's, a man singing about the sea. The old woman did not hear any of it. She lifted her wrist and looked at it again.

Rei thought of Gin. Of the plate upside down on the counter. Of the phone that said *Delivered* and never *Read*. Of checking it, and checking it again, and telling yourself, just a few more minutes.

He understood her much better than he wanted to.

[break The line]

This is how Gin's book said to do it. Rei had read the page so many times that he could see it with his eyes shut.

You do not force a spirit like this one to go. You do not shout at it or throw salt in its face. You make a line, and you show it which side is which.

He knelt down on the tiles at the edge of the cold patch. His knees cracked. He took a small bag of coarse salt out of his backpack. He had bought it at the supermarket the day before, because the book said to carry salt the way you carry a phone, and he had stood in the aisle for ten minutes reading the backs of the packets like an idiot, as if there might be one that said *for the dead*. He poured a thin line of salt across the floor, between the old woman and the open end of the arcade where the morning light came in. Then, on his own side of the line, the street side, he set down one of the two anpan from Kondō's bag, still warm.

"Grandmother," he said. "He's not coming. I'm sorry. He's not late. He went on ahead."

She looked at the line of salt. Her face did not change.

"It's Thursday," said Rei. "There's anpan. It's on this side. On the warm side." He did not know if he was doing it right. He was making it up as he went, out of a page written by his brother, kneeling on the floor of a street he had never been to before, at half past seven in the morning, in front of a closed watch shop. "He's on the other side. He went ahead to wait for you. You've always been the one waiting. It's your turn to be late now. It's alright to be late."

For a long moment, nothing happened. The cold sat against his face and his hands. His knees ached on the hard tiles. Somewhere behind him a little girl asked her mother why that man was kneeling on the floor talking to the shop, and her mother said *shh, don't stare*, and pulled her away.

Then the old woman lowered her wrist.

She looked at the anpan on the tiles. She looked at the line of salt. And then, for the first time, she looked straight at Rei, and her eyes were not cloudy any more. They were brown, and clear, and very, very tired.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's so late."

She stepped over the line of salt. Not toward Rei. Past him, toward the open end of the arcade and the white morning light. As she went by, she put her hand on his head, lightly, the way grandmothers do with small boys who have been good. He felt it. It was cold, and then it was warm, and then it was nothing at all.

The patch of floor was only a patch of floor.

[break After]

He tried to stand up and could not.

His legs had gone soft, like wet paper. His hands were shaking so hard that the paper bag rattled. The edges of his sight had gone dark and narrow, like looking down a cardboard tube. He could hear his own heart, very loud and very fast, banging in his ears. He felt exactly as if he had run up every stair of a tall building with a sack of rice on his back, and been told at the top to go down and do it again.

Gin's book had a word for this. It was on page twelve, underlined. *Stamina. You have a certain amount. Every time you open, you spend it. When it is gone, it is gone, and the body will collect the rest of the bill.* Rei had read it and thought it was a figure of speech.

He found himself sitting on a plastic crate outside the closed watch shop. He did not remember getting there. He was breathing in big ragged gasps, like a swimmer.

A shadow fell over him. Mr. Kondō, holding his crate of flour.

"You alright, son?" he said. "You've gone white as rice."

"It's the heat," said Rei.

He heard himself say it. It was exactly what the blue suits had told the mother on the platform. It came out of his mouth so easily that it frightened him.

Mr. Kondō looked at him for a moment. Then he looked down at the floor. At the line of salt. At the anpan lying on the tiles. Then he looked at the patch of floor in front of the watch shop, the one he had walked round every single morning for twenty-three years without ever knowing why.

He did not say anything. He frowned a little, like a man trying to remember a word that is right on the tip of his tongue.

Then he put the crate of flour down on the ground, walked straight across that patch of floor, right through the middle of it, to his bakery, and came back out with a cold bottle of water and a curry bread in a paper bag.

"On the house," he said, and pushed them both into Rei's hands. "Eat. You young people don't eat. My grandson's exactly the same. All phone, no rice."

He picked up his crate and walked back down the middle of the arcade, and he did not step round anything at all.

Rei sat on the crate and ate the curry bread, and drank the water, and slowly, very slowly, the dark edges of the world went away. He felt hollowed out and scraped clean, like the inside of a bowl. And he felt, for the first time in eight days, that he had done something that was not waiting.

He took out his phone and, without thinking about it, wrote to Gin.

*I helped someone today. In Jūjō. With salt and anpan. I think I did it right.*

*Delivered.*

He put the phone away before it could not say *Read*.
