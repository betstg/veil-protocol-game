#title Chen Lian

On Thursday, at seven in the evening, Rei stood by the statue of the man with the dog in Ueno Park in a new pair of black running shoes that had cost him six thousand yen he did not have.

Majima came up the steps from the station at seven exactly, with the old brindled dog on its lead and the convenience-store bag in his other hand. He looked at the shoes. He did not say anything about them. He handed Rei a can of hot coffee and kept walking, and Rei fell in beside him, and the dog walked between them with its torn ear flopping.

They walked for three hours.

Majima had not lied. It was mostly boring. They walked down through the park past the museums, past the zoo gates, past the pond with its lotus leaves as big as umbrellas, and Majima stopped every so often for no reason Rei could see and stood still with his weight forward and his eyes doing their endless work, and then walked on. Twice he crouched and put his palm flat on the ground. Once he stopped beside a bench where an old man in a cardigan was feeding pigeons, and sat down at the other end of the bench, and did not say a word to him, and after five minutes the old man said, without looking round, *the one by the shrine is getting worse, Majima-san*, and Majima said, *thanks, Tetsu*, and got up and walked on.

The one by the shrine was a thin spot in the corner of a small Inari shrine behind the pond, where the stone foxes stood with red bibs round their necks. Rei felt it from twenty metres away. Cold, and soft, and wrong, like stepping onto a rotten floorboard.

And on the stone step in front of it, someone had left a white chrysanthemum.

Rei stopped.

Majima stopped too. He looked at the flower for a long time. Then he looked at Rei.

"That's the third one this week," he said. "Asakusa had one on Monday. Okachimachi had one on Tuesday. Always fresh. Always on a spot that's going bad." He crouched and looked at the chrysanthemum without touching it. "Somebody's marking them."

Rei looked at the flower and felt the latch go cold. "I know who."

Majima did not ask. He waited, crouched by the shrine, with his dark eyes on Rei's face and the old dog sitting patiently at his heel, and the evening traffic going past on the far side of the trees.

"There's a woman called Kanna-sensei," Rei said. "In Asagaya. She runs a circle for people who've lost someone. She's not a woman. She's something wearing one." He swallowed. "My brother went to see her. Last July. Right before."

Majima stood up slowly. His knees cracked.

"The Far Shore," he said. "I've heard the name." He looked at the chrysanthemum. "Three people I keep an eye on along this strip went to her tea. Two came back quiet. One didn't come back." He put his hand inside his jacket, over his heart, and held it there for a second, and took it away. "I don't take cases that interest me anymore."

Rei kept his eyes on the chrysanthemum. "I know."

"This one interests me." Majima clicked his tongue, and the dog got up. "Which is going to be a pain in the arse."

He walked on, toward the lights of Okachimachi. After a moment, Rei followed.

[break The Archive]

He went to the Grid Archive on Friday after work with his notes from the Capturer written out on four sheets of paper in pencil, word for word, as near as he could remember them.

He did not go to Mori. He had thought about it all week, on the sofa, with his foot off the end. Mori held Rage. Mori would hear the words *the thing in Gin came and asked for help* and the boiler room would burn down with Rei in it. Not out of cruelty. Because Rage was *no, this is wrong*, and an alliance with a demon was the most wrong thing in the world.

He went to Chen Lian. Because she was the only one who had ever looked at the white and said *a man*.

She was in the long reading room on the second floor, the one with the green-shaded lamps and the ladders on rails, sitting cross-legged on top of a library table with three books open round her and her plait pinned up on her head with a pencil. She looked up when he came in. She looked at his face.

Then she closed all three books, carefully, one after the other.

"Something's happened," she said, and took the pencil out of her hair, and her plait fell down her back. "Sit. No. Not there, that chair's haunted, it pinches. There." She pointed. "Tell me."

He told her.

All of it. The Capturer. The roof. *Please.* Yua and the heartbeat. Kanna-sensei's black eyes and the offer of the chair. The terms. *Names are debts.* He gave her the four pencil sheets and she read them twice, very fast, her lips moving, and then once more very slowly, and then she put them down on the table and put both her hands flat on top of them, as if they might blow away.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, Rei. Oh, you idiot. You brave, stupid, wonderful idiot."

Rei watched her hands on the paper. "Are you going to tell Mori?"

Chen Lian looked at him over the top of her glasses for a long time.

"No," she said. "Not yet." She took off her glasses and rubbed her eyes. "Mori would be right. That's the problem. Mori would be absolutely right, and he would stop you, and then she'd win." She put the glasses back on. "Wonder is the one that doesn't know yet. That's what I hold. Not knowing, and wanting to. I've never been more not-knowing in my life." She stood up on the table. "Come on. Ladder. Fourth gallery. I need to find something and I need someone to hold the bottom."

[break The fourth gallery]

The Grid Archive did not make sense on the inside. Rei had known that since the first night, when he had walked up a narrow staircase above a guitar shop and come out into a library that went on further than the building could possibly contain. He had stopped trying to make it make sense. You went up a ladder in the long reading room and came out on a gallery that ran round the inside of a tower you had never seen from the street, with books on every side going up into the dark, and a cello playing somewhere far below.

The fourth gallery was very old. The books there were not books. They were bundles of paper tied with string, and scrolls in wooden boxes, and ledgers bound in cloth gone soft as felt, and boxes of index cards in handwriting that changed every few decades, from brush to fountain pen to biro.

Chen Lian went along the shelves with one finger out, touching spines, not reading them. Rei held the ladder and the lamp.

"The Thirteen are holders," she said, as she went. "You know that. One person for each feeling, holding it up, so that it runs the right way through the city. Like a dam. Like a sluice. Joy and Rage and Fear and Love and all the rest." She pulled out a box, looked into it, put it back. "But a holder seat has two sides. That's the thing nobody likes to say out loud. Every feeling has a light way and a dark way. The light way is what we hold. The dark way..."

She stopped. Pulled out a cloth-bound ledger. Blew dust off it. Opened it.

"The dark way has a holder too," she said. "Not a person. Something else. One for each seat. Each feeling in its worst form, given a shape. Thirteen of them. The old books call them the Shells. The husks. Somebody very clever in Kyoto two hundred years ago drew them as a tree, upside down, with its roots in the sky." She turned the ledger round so Rei could see.

It was a drawing. Ink on old yellow paper. Thirteen circles joined by lines, in the shape of a tree hanging from the top of the page like a bat. Beside each circle, in tiny brush characters, the name of a feeling.

Most of the circles were empty.

Two of them had names written inside them, in a different ink. Newer. Red.

Beside *Despair*, inside the circle, two characters. *Demon.* *God.*

*鬼神.*

"Kishin," said Chen Lian softly. "That's her. That's what the thing in Asagaya is. The Shell of Despair. It's been loose for longer than anybody here has kept records. It changes faces." She touched the circle with one fingertip. "It's eating Watanabe. You know that. Not just the thread. It's eating him because he's its holder. The light side of its own seat. When he's gone, it'll be sitting on both sides of the same chair at once."

Rei could not look away from the other red name. At the bottom of the tree. At the very last circle, where the roots went up into the sky.

*Duty.*

Inside it there had been a name, in the same red ink. Someone had struck it through with a soft pencil, again and again, so hard that the old paper had torn, so that nothing was left of it but a black scar. Beside it, smaller, in a fountain pen that had nearly run dry, someone had written a note in the margin, slanting, in a hand Rei knew better than his own.

*Not a Shell. Not only. It pays. G.*

[break The tree]

Rei sat down on the floor of the fourth gallery with the lamp in his lap.

His voice cracked. "He knew. Gin. He knew what it was. Before."

Chen Lian came down the ladder and sat down beside him with the ledger on her knees.

"He was here a lot," she said. "The year before. More than anybody. He'd sit up here for hours." She turned the page back and forth very gently. "I didn't know what he was looking for. I asked him once. He said he was trying to find out if anything in the world ever paid back more than it took."

Rei put his hand over his mouth.

"She doesn't want you in the chair," said Chen Lian slowly. She was looking at the tree. Her face had the look it got when she was a long way ahead of everyone else and could see something nobody else could see yet, and was delighted, and was terrified. "That's what I couldn't make work. Why would she want *you*? A human. A holder. You'd be on the light side. You'd be in her way." She tapped the bottom circle. "But she doesn't want you. Not really. She wants the chair filled with something she owns. That's all. She owns the Duty debt. She's owned it for hundreds of years. So she offered you the chair, because if you sat in it on her word, she'd own you too. And if you didn't..."

She looked at Rei.

"Then she calls in the debt," said Rei. His voice came out very strange. "And the thing that owes her has to sit in it instead. The Duty Shell. In the Duty chair." He swallowed. "In Gin's body."

Chen Lian nodded slowly.

"Two chairs with two sides each," she said. "Despair, both sides, hers. Duty, both sides, the thing she owns. And the other eleven seats with their Shells loose and hungry and waiting, and four or five holders who are tired or frightened or old." She closed the ledger. Very gently. "And the veil's been damaged since the earthquake. Since March 2011. It never healed. Every thin spot that opened that year is still open. You've seen them. Majima's been watching them go bad."

Rei looked up from the floor. "With chrysanthemums on them."

Chen Lian went very still.

Her hand stopped on the ledger. "On them?"

Rei nodded. "On the step. Fresh. Three this week. Majima found them."

Chen Lian closed her eyes. When she opened them, the delight was gone. There was only the terror.

"She's marking doors," she whispered. "She's going round the city marking every door she wants open. For Obon. For the night the dead come home and the veil's as thin as paper and everybody in the city is lighting fires on their doorsteps to show the dead the way in." She gripped the ledger. "She's going to open the far shore on Obon, with two chairs under her and every thin spot in Tokyo marked with a white flower, and put the Shells in the seats. All of them. Thirteen demons in thirteen chairs, and the current running backwards through the whole city. Not a few things coming across." She looked at him. "Everything."

[break Downstairs]

Rei went down to the ground floor before he left. To the little room at the back, among the books, with the window onto the narrow street.

Watanabe was sitting in his chair by the window. He did not turn his head. He could not, really, any more. The grey had gone past his wrists now and up into his forearms, and his face had gone the colour of old paper, and his eyes were open and fixed on the window, on the west, where the thread ran away across the city.

Rei sat down on the floor beside the chair, the way he did every evening.

*Hello, Uedera,* said the voice in his head. Very faint now. Like someone speaking in a library three rooms away. *You've been upstairs. In the tree.*

Rei drew his knees up. "Yes."

*Then you know what she is.*

Rei looked at the grey on the man's arms. "Yes."

*Good.* A long pause. The thread hummed. *I knew, you know. Since February. That it was mine. My own dark side. Coming up through the floor.* Another pause. *It's very strange. To be eaten by your own shadow. You'd think it would hurt more. It doesn't. It's only tired.*

Rei put his hand on the arm of the chair. Not on Watanabe's hand. Watanabe had asked him not to. He said it was like being touched through a wet blanket and it made him sad.

"The thing in my brother came to see me," he said quietly. "It asked for help. It wants to stop her."

The room was quiet for so long that Rei thought Watanabe had gone somewhere further away, the way he sometimes did now.

Then, very faint, it came.

*Does it.*

Rei nodded, though Watanabe could not see him. "Yes."

*Then help it.* The voice was so tired. *Help it, Uedera. And when it's my time, keep your promise. Stand between me and the thread. Don't let her have the last of me. If she gets the last of me, she gets the seat. Whole.* A pause. *I'm not afraid of dying. I'm afraid of what I'll be holding when I do.*

Rei sat on the floor by the chair in the evening light until the window went dark.

He put his forehead against the cool wood of the chair arm. "I promise," he said again, at last.

*I know,* said Watanabe. *That's why I asked you.* And then, very faintly, almost too faint to hear, something that might have been a laugh. *Promises are for demons. I told you. Now look. You're making one with a demon. You'll be all right. You've had practice.*
