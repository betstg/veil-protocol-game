#title Chen Lian

On Thursday, at seven in the evening, three days after the east corridor, Rei stood by the statue of the man with the dog in Ueno Park in a new pair of black running shoes that had cost him six thousand yen he did not have.

Majima came up the steps from the station at seven exactly, with the old brindled dog on its lead and the convenience-store bag in his other hand. He looked at the shoes. He did not say anything about them. He handed Rei a can of hot coffee and kept walking, and Rei fell in beside him, and the dog walked between them with its torn ear flopping.

They walked for three hours. Rei had not wanted to come. It was the tenth of August, and the letter from Kita Residential Services was folded in his jacket pocket, unanswered, and there were two days left on the calendar by the fridge. But Kato was listening for Gin across the whole city, and Itsuki was drawing him over and over at the kitchen table, and Majima walked the places where things went bad. Rei thought that if Gin was anywhere, he might be near one of them.

Majima had not lied. It was mostly boring. They walked down through the park past the museums, past the zoo gates, past the pond with its lotus leaves as big as umbrellas, and Majima stopped every so often for no reason Rei could see and stood still with his weight forward and his eyes doing their endless work, and then walked on. Twice he crouched and put his palm flat on the ground. Once he stopped beside a bench where an old man in a cardigan was feeding pigeons, and sat down at the other end of the bench, and did not say a word to him, and after five minutes the old man said, without looking round, *the one by the shrine is getting worse, Majima-san*, and Majima said, *thanks, Tetsu*, and got up and walked on.

The one by the shrine was a thin spot in the corner of a small Inari shrine behind the pond, where the stone foxes stood with red bibs round their necks. Rei felt it from twenty metres away. Cold, and soft, and wrong, like stepping onto a rotten floorboard.

And on the stone step in front of it, someone had left a white chrysanthemum.

Rei stopped.

Majima stopped too. He looked at the flower for a long time. Then he looked at Rei.

"That's the third one this week," he said. "Asakusa had one on Monday. Okachimachi had one on Tuesday. Always fresh. Always on a spot that's going bad." He crouched and looked at the chrysanthemum without touching it. "Somebody's marking them."

Rei looked at the flower and felt the latch go cold. "I know who."

Majima did not ask. He waited, crouched by the shrine, with his dark eyes on Rei's face and the old dog sitting patiently at his heel, and the evening traffic going past on the far side of the trees.

"There's a woman called Kanna-sensei," Rei said. "In Asagaya. She runs a circle for people who've lost someone. She's not a woman. She's something wearing one." He swallowed. "My brother went to see her. Last July. Right before."

Majima stood up slowly. His knees cracked.

"The Far Shore," he said. "I've heard the name." He looked at the chrysanthemum. "Three people I keep an eye on along this strip went to her tea. Two came back quiet. One didn't come back." He put his hand inside his jacket, over his heart, and held it there for a second, and took it away. "I don't take cases that interest me anymore."

Rei kept his eyes on the chrysanthemum. "I know."

"This one interests me." Majima clicked his tongue, and the dog got up. "Which is going to be a pain in the arse."

He walked on, toward the lights of Okachimachi. After a moment, Rei followed.

[break The Archive]

He went to the Grid Archive the next evening, straight from the Division, with the flowers written down on a sheet of paper in pencil. Asakusa, Monday. Okachimachi, Tuesday. Ueno, the Inari shrine behind the pond, Thursday. And under them, everything Kanna-sensei had said to him in the white room, as near as he could remember it, word for word.

He did not go to Mori. Mori held Rage, and Rage was *no, this is wrong, stop it now*, and Mori would hear the words *Asagaya* and *Kanna* and go there himself that night with every one of the Thirteen who could still stand, and walk into thirty hollow people in their own house.

He went to Chen Lian. She had sat on the floor in the door of the round room for thirty-four hours while his hand was stuck to the chair, and she had not once told him he should not have touched it.

She was in the long reading room on the second floor, the one with the green-shaded lamps and the ladders on rails, sitting cross-legged on top of a library table with three books open round her and two paintbrushes stuck through her hair to hold it up. There was blue paint on her fingers and a smear of it on her glasses. She looked up when he came in. She looked at his face.

Then she closed all three books, carefully, one after the other.

"Something's happened," she said, and pulled one of the brushes out of her hair, and the hair came half down. "Sit. No. Not there, that chair pinches, nobody knows why. There." She pointed. "Tell me."

He told her. The flowers. The white room. The black eyes. *Stay with us. When it comes close again, we'll hold it still for you.* The heartbeat on the side of Yua's cup. He gave her the sheet of paper and she read it fast, her lips moving, and then again slowly, and then she put both hands flat on top of it as if it might blow away.

"White chrysanthemums," she said. "On thin places. Fresh every day." She looked up. "Rei. Who puts flowers on a grave?"

"Family. At Obon."

"At Obon," said Chen Lian. "To show the dead the way home." She stood up on the table. "Come on. Ladder. Fourth gallery. I need to find something and I need someone to hold the bottom."

[break The fourth gallery]

The Grid Archive did not make sense on the inside. Rei had stopped trying to make it. You went up a ladder in the long reading room and came out on a gallery that ran round the inside of a tower you had never seen from the street, with books on every side going up into the dark, and a cello playing somewhere far below.

The fourth gallery was very old. The books there were not books. They were bundles of paper tied with string, and scrolls in wooden boxes, and ledgers bound in cloth gone soft as felt, in handwriting that changed every few decades, from brush to fountain pen to biro.

Chen Lian went along the shelves with one blue finger out, touching spines, not reading them. Rei held the ladder and the lamp.

"There's a record," she said, as she went. "Old. Older than your Division. From the people who did your job before there was a Division, when this city was Edo and the river had no concrete on it. Kasumi Shin's family kept it. She lent it to us when she took the Awe seat. Most of it is dull. Who was paid what. Which bridge had a drowned woman under it. Which inn had a fox in the kitchen." She pulled out a wooden box, looked into it, put it back. "But I read it all. I read everything. I remember a page about Bon."

She found it on the third box. A bundle of thin yellow paper folded concertina-fashion, tied with a faded purple cord. She sat down on the gallery floor and untied it and unfolded it across her knees, page after page, running a finger down columns of tiny brush characters. Rei sat beside her with the lamp.

"Here," she said.

She read it aloud, slowly, putting the old words into new ones as she went.

*In the seventh month, in the days before Bon, white flowers were found in the morning at the thin places of the city. At the foot of the Ryōgoku bridge. At the well behind the Kanda shrine. At the gate of an empty house in Yanaka. Eleven places in all. Fresh every morning. Nobody was seen to put them there.*

*Those who looked into the matter found that a woman who kept a house for the grieving in Yotsuya had been giving tea to the lonely for some years, and that many of those who drank her tea did not go home.*

*On the first night of Bon, when the fires were lit at the gates to bring the dead home, every place that had been marked with a white flower opened at once.*

Chen Lian stopped.

"Go on," said Rei.

"That's the end of the page." She turned it over. The back was blank. She unfolded the next one. It began with a list of payments for repairing a bridge. "There's a page missing." She held the bundle up to the lamp. Between the two sheets, close to the fold, there was a narrow ragged edge of yellow paper, where one had been torn out. "Somebody took it."

"Who?"

"I don't know." She ran her thumb along the torn edge. "Old tear. Very old. The paper's gone brown at the edge." She was quiet for a while. "Whatever happened that night, somebody didn't want anyone reading how it ended."

Rei looked at the three words she had read last. *Opened at once.*

"She's done it before," he said.

"Somebody has." Chen Lian folded the pages back up, very carefully, along their old folds. "A woman who keeps a house for the grieving. Tea. White flowers on the thin places before Bon." She tied the purple cord. "I don't believe in coincidences. I'm the Wonder seat. I believe in everything except coincidences."

She sat with the bundle on her knees.

"Obon starts the day after tomorrow," she said. "The thirteenth. The fires go out on the doorsteps on the thirteenth at dusk." She looked at him. "Every thin place she's marked. All at once. With thirty people in that house who'll do whatever she says, and the city full of people who've lit fires on purpose to show the dead the way in." Her voice had gone very quiet. "Not a few things coming across, Rei. Everything that wants to."

Rei sat on the floor of the fourth gallery with the lamp in his lap.

The thirteenth. One day after the twelfth.

"Then we have to tell Mori," he said.

"Yes." Chen Lian stood up. She held out a blue hand and pulled him up after her. "Tonight. All of them. And your Director. And the families. And the Grid. Everyone." She put the bundle back in its box and the box back on the shelf. "And then we find out how many white flowers there are. Because we've found three. And I'd bet my whole seat there are a great many more than three."

[break Downstairs]

Rei went down to the ground floor before he left. To the little room at the back, among the books, with the window onto the narrow street.

Watanabe was sitting in his chair by the window. He did not turn his head. He could not, really, any more. The grey had gone past his wrists now and up into his forearms, and his face had gone the colour of old paper, and his eyes were open and fixed on the window, on the west, where the thread ran away across the city.

Rei sat down on the floor beside the chair, as he did every evening.

*Hello, Uedera,* said the voice in his head. Very faint now. Like someone speaking in a library three rooms away. *You've been upstairs. You've found something.*

Rei drew his knees up. "Yes."

*Then you know when.*

Rei looked at the grey on the man's arms. "The thirteenth."

*Good. Somebody should know.* A long pause. The thread hummed. *I can feel her getting ready. It's like living next to someone who's packing to go on holiday. Drawers opening and shutting all night.* Another pause. *She needs me gone first, I think. She needs the seat empty on this side. Then there's nobody holding the door from here.*

Rei put his hand on the arm of the chair. Not on Watanabe's hand. Watanabe had asked him not to. He said it was like being touched through a wet blanket and it made him sad.

"My brother's got two days," he said. He had not said it aloud to anyone but Itsuki and Kato. "About two days. The Director says a year, without a contract. It'll be a year on the twelfth."

The room was quiet for so long that Rei thought Watanabe had gone somewhere further away, as he sometimes did now.

Then, very faint, it came.

*About,* said Watanabe.

"That's what Moriya said."

*Moriya's right. I was supposed to be dead in May.* Something that might have been a laugh. *Listen, Uedera. When it's my time, keep your promise. Stand between me and the thread. Don't let her have the last of me.* A pause. *And then go and get your brother. Don't sit here with me any longer than you have to. I'm not going anywhere you can follow.*

Rei sat on the floor by the chair in the evening light until the window went dark.

"I promise," he said again, at last, with his forehead on the cool wood of the chair arm.

*I know,* said Watanabe. *That's why I asked you.*
