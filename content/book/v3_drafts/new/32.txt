#title The Brother

*The Healer.*

In February the shrine on the hill above Ōji station held a kite market, and the Healer went, because it had never seen a kite.

It had learned by now that the body liked to walk, and that a walking body drew less attention than a standing one, and that the city was full of things it had no names for. So it walked, most days, wherever the streets went. And on a cold bright Sunday the streets went up a stone stair between old trees to a shrine with red gates, and the whole of the shrine yard was full of stalls, and every stall was full of kites.

Small square paper kites painted with the faces of old warriors, red and black and fierce. Long thin kites shaped like birds. A kite like a fish, a kite like a dragon with a tail three metres long, a tiny kite no bigger than a hand with a cartoon cat on it. They hung from the awnings in rows and turned slowly in the wind. People pushed between the stalls in thick coats, eating grilled squid on sticks and sweet potato from paper bags. Children held up kites to their parents and said *this one, this one*. An old man with a microphone was selling charms against fire in a voice like a gravel road.

[need d2_kite_market | A crowded shrine yard on a cold February day, stalls hung with painted paper kites of warriors and birds and fish, red shrine gates and old trees, a tall man with long black hair in a grey jacket standing very still in the crowd looking up at the kites]

The Healer stood in the middle of it all and looked up.

It did not know what the kites were for. It had asked, the week before, in the small yellow kitchen, and the man had said *flying* and gone quiet, and it had not been able to get anything more out of him. Now it watched a boy of about six run along the edge of the yard with a paper kite on a string, and the wind caught it, and it went up, wobbling, over the heads of the crowd, and the boy shrieked with joy, and his father ran after him shouting *let the string out, let it out*.

*That is what they are for,* the Healer thought. *They are for that.*

It found it did not mind standing here. It found it would have liked to stay.

Then the body went still all at once, like a dog that has heard its name.

[break The face]

The younger one was on the other side of the yard.

The Healer knew him at once. Not because it had ever seen him. Because the body had. The body had seen that face every day for twenty years. In a cot. At a kitchen table. Asleep on a sofa with one foot off the end. The face was in every corner of the body's memory, more than any other face, more than its own. The Healer had walked through those memories for months as you walk through rooms in a stranger's house, and in every room there was this face.

He was standing by a stall that sold kites shaped like crows, in a dark coat with the collar up, with a black and white crow on his shoulder. A real crow, piebald, with one pale blue eye, pecking at the edge of his collar. Beside him stood a tall young man in a long dark overcoat and a tie, holding a paper cup, saying something. The younger one was laughing.

He did not look like the cot or the sofa. He looked thinner. Older. There was a mark on the inside of his left wrist, where his sleeve had slipped back. The Healer could feel it from across the yard. A small cold closed thing. A latch.

And the body moved.

It was not the Healer. The Healer had not told it to. The body took one step forward, toward the far side of the yard, and the Healer stopped it, and it took another. From the very bottom of the body, from under all the dark the Healer had pressed down on him, the man was coming up. Not slowly, as he had in the kitchen. All at once. Like a man drowning who has seen a hand.

The mouth opened.

"Rei," it said. Out loud. In Gin's voice, rough and cracked from not being used. "*Rei.* My brother. That's my brother."

A woman beside the Healer turned and looked at it.

On the other side of the yard, the younger one stopped laughing. He turned his head.

He looked across the crowd, across the stalls and the kites and the people with their squid on sticks, and he looked straight at the Healer.

[break What it did]

The Healer had seconds. It knew that.

In a second the younger one would cross the yard. In two, the tall one with the tie, who was listening to everything, would hear the wrong thing in the Healer's head and turn round too. The man in the body was still coming up, pushing, shouting now, *Rei, Rei, I'm here, Rei*, and the body's feet were trying to walk, and the Healer was holding them, and it could not hold them and do anything else at the same time for long.

It did not want the younger one. It had no use for him. It wanted him to look somewhere else.

It reached out with the man's own sight. The man could feel things in places. He had spent his life doing it. Every corner of this shrine, every stone, every old tree had something in it, and the man's sight found them all at once, the way his hand found a light switch in the dark. Most of it was nothing. Old prayers. Old fire. A little grief by the wishing boards.

And under the stone stair, in the cold, a bad thing. Small and sour and angry. Something that had been waiting under those steps for a long time for somebody to trip.

The Healer took it.

It used the other thing the man could do. The thing that moved things from one place to another without crossing what lay between. The man had used it on himself, mostly, and on objects. He had never used it like this. The Healer did not care how he had used it. It took the small sour thing from under the stair and put it, all at once, into an old woman standing at the crow-kite stall, a metre from the younger one's elbow.

Inside the body, the man stopped shouting.

For one second there was nothing from him at all. Then a sound came up from the bottom of the dark that the Healer had not heard before. Not a word. Something like a man watching his own hand do a thing he would never do.

The old woman at the stall dropped her bag of sweet potato. She turned round. Her face went slack, and then tight. She picked up a pair of scissors from the stall, the long ones the stall-keeper used for cutting kite string, and she went for the boy with the kite.

[break The younger one]

The Healer watched it happen, because it needed to see that it had worked.

The younger one turned away. He had to. He turned away from the Healer and toward the old woman and the boy and the scissors, and he went. He did not think. The Healer could see him not think. He was across the gap in one step and he had the old woman's wrist in both hands and the scissors were pointing at the sky and the boy's father was screaming and the kite was tangling in the branches of a tree.

The old woman was very strong. The sour thing in her was making her strong. She twisted. The scissors came down. The younger one put his own arm in the way, and the blade went into his coat sleeve and through it, and Rei made a sound, and did not let go.

He was trying to do something. The Healer could feel it, faintly, from across the yard. He was opening the door in his chest, lifting the latch on it, trying to reach the thing inside the old woman and talk to it, or pull it, or hold it. He did not have enough. He was much too new, and the thing was small but very angry, and it had a body now, and it did not want to come out.

The tall young man with the tie was beside him. He had put down his paper cup. He was holding the old woman's other arm, and his face was very calm, and the Healer felt him go into her head, quick and precise, like a hand going into a drawer, and find the thing, and pin it. Not pull it out. Only pin it, so that it could not move her arms.

The crow on the younger one's shoulder was screaming. People were shouting. Somebody was calling an ambulance.

The younger one looked up. Over the old woman's shoulder, through the crowd, across the yard.

At the place where the Healer had been standing.

The Healer was not there any more. It had used the man's other gift once more, for itself this time, and it was standing at the bottom of the stone stair, in the cold, under the old trees, where the sour thing had been. Nobody had seen it go.

It walked down the hill toward the station with its hands in its coat pockets.

Inside the body, very deep down, the man was not shouting. He was not fighting. He was lying very still at the bottom of the dark, and the Healer could feel what he was feeling, and it was not anger. It was worse than anger. It was the feeling of a man who has just watched his brother bleed for a stranger, and knows whose hands did it.

*You used me,* said the man. Very quietly. *You used what I can do. On him.*

*I used it near him,* said the Healer. *Not on him. He was not hurt much.*

*He was bleeding.*

*Yes.* The Healer considered this. *He went to the old woman. He did not come to you. You should be glad. He is the kind of man who goes to the one who is in danger.*

The man did not answer that.

The Healer walked on down the hill. At the bottom, by the station, there was a stall that had come down from the shrine to catch the people on their way home. It sold small paper kites for children, the cheap kind, with a cartoon cat on them. The Healer stopped and looked at them for a moment.

Then it walked on. It did not buy one. It did not know who it would have bought it for.
