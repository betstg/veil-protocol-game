#title The Six Paths

At six in the morning the training hall was cold, even in September.

It was a long, high room in the oldest part of the Kuroda house, with a floor of wide dark boards worn smooth by four hundred years of bare feet. Itsuki had not been exaggerating. In places the wood had gone soft and dipped, like the middle of an old stone step. The walls were plain cedar. At the far end hung one scroll with one character brushed on it in thick black ink. 門. *Gate.* The tall windows along one side stood open, and the morning came in grey and damp, with the smell of the wet garden and the slap of a carp in the pond.

[need ch11_training_hall | A long high old wooden training hall at dawn, wide dark floorboards worn into soft dips, tall open windows onto a wet garden, a single scroll with the kanji 門 at the end, young people in plain grey training clothes kneeling in a row]

There were six people in the hall when they arrived. Four were young, cousins of Itsuki's, in plain grey training clothes, kneeling in a row like birds on a wire. One was an old woman with her white hair in a bun, kneeling by the wall with a teapot and a small wooden box beside her. And one was Kuroda Sōun, standing in the middle of the floor in a plain dark kimono, hands behind his back.

The cousins looked at Itsuki the way people look at a dog that has walked into a restaurant.

"Morning," said Itsuki, bright as a game show host. He had come in the same grey hoodie he painted in, and had clearly not slept. "I'm back. Did everyone miss me? Don't answer that."

Nobody answered it.

Rei's stomach growled into the silence, loud as a door. He had not eaten breakfast. Nobody had told him that not eating breakfast would be this loud.

[break What there is]

"Before you learn anything," said Sōun, "you will learn what there is to learn."

He walked slowly down the hall as he spoke. His voice was quiet and it filled the room without trying, the way water fills a bowl.

"There are six paths. Six ways a person can touch the veil. Most who can touch it at all have one path open. Some have two. Very few have more. Each path is a different kind of hand. You do not choose your hands. You are born with them, and then you spend the rest of your life finding out what they are for."

He stopped by the first cousin. A tall young man with a shaved head and a jaw like a brick.

"Telekinesis. The hand that moves things."

The young man did not move. On the boards in front of him lay six flat floor cushions in a row. The first one lifted off the floor, slowly, as if somebody invisible were picking it up by two corners. It rose to the height of his chest and hung there. The second rose and laid itself on top of the first. Then the third. Then all six hung in the air, stacked neat as folded laundry, and the young man had not lifted a finger. His face was perfectly calm. Only one small vein stood out at his temple and pulsed.

"Down, Takuya," said Sōun. The cushions came down one at a time, like snow deciding where to land.

He moved to a girl of about sixteen with a long ponytail and very straight shoulders.

"Psychokinesis. The hand that makes heat, and cold, and lightning."

The girl held up her right hand, palm open. On it stood a short white candle, unlit. She looked at it. The wick went black, smoked, and caught. A small yellow flame stood up, steady. Then it went blue. Then it went out, and the candle was furred all over with frost, white and glittering, and the girl's breath came out of her mouth as a cloud in the September air.

"It is the bluntest of the six," said Sōun. "It does not talk to the dead. It burns them. It burns the bodies they are wearing, too." He looked at Rei. "Remember that. One day it will matter."

The old woman by the wall did not wait to be introduced. She crooked one finger at Itsuki. He went, the way a cat goes when it has been called and wants it known that it was going anyway, and knelt in front of her. She took his right hand in both of hers and turned it over. Across the knuckles was a fresh scrape, a stupid little wound from some corner of his studio, that Rei had not even noticed.

"Psychometabolism," said Sōun. "The hand that reads the body. Living or dead. And mends it."

The old woman held Itsuki's hand and closed her eyes. Rei watched the scrape. Slowly, while he watched, the angry red edges went pink. The pink went pale. The skin drew together from both sides like a zip pulled shut. In less than a minute there was nothing on Itsuki's knuckles but a thin white line.

"You don't sleep," the old woman said, eyes still closed. "You don't eat breakfast. You drink coffee out of cans like a taxi driver. You smoke. Your heart is fine." She opened her eyes. "Your liver is annoyed."

"I'll think about it, Grandmother Mitsu."

"You won't." She patted his cheek. It was a pat the way a door slamming is a draught. "Go away."

He went away rubbing his face, and on his way back past Rei he muttered, "She's the only one in this house I like," and Rei believed him.

[break The other three]

"Telepathy," said Sōun. "The hand that touches minds."

He did not demonstrate it with a cousin. He looked at Rei.

*You are frightened,* said a voice inside Rei's head.

It was Sōun's voice. Low and dry and careful, exactly the same as the voice that came out of his mouth, except that it did not come out of his mouth. It was inside, behind Rei's eyes, close as his own thinking. Rei jerked back so hard he nearly went over. Two of the cousins laughed. Sōun did not.

*You are frightened,* the voice went on, unhurried, *and you are angry, and under the anger you are very tired. And you keep a door in your chest, and you believe it was given to you so that you could sleep.* A pause, as if it were checking something. *It was not.*

Then it was gone, and Rei's head was his own again, and he was breathing like he had run up the hill. He felt as if someone had opened the bathroom door on him and then, worse, apologised.

"That was rude," Sōun said aloud. "I did it so that you would know exactly how rude it is. A telepath can read the surface of a person without asking. Fear. Anger. What they want. What they are hiding. It is the easiest of the six to abuse and the hardest to be trusted with." He looked at the cousins who had laughed until they stopped. "A good telepath spends his whole life asking permission. Most of them are very lonely people."

Rei would remember that sentence for a long time. Later, he would know exactly who it had been about.

"Clairsentience," said Sōun. "The hand that reads what has happened. Rooms. Objects. Places. The structure of a thing. Who has power over whom, and where it will break." He looked at Rei again. "That one, I think, has been reading you for years."

"And the sixth?" said Rei.

Sōun turned to the far end of the hall, where the scroll hung.

"Teleportation. The hand that opens doors. You saw it last night. To step from one place into another without crossing what lies between. It is the rarest of the six, and it is the Kuroda path. Every person in this family has carried it in the blood for four hundred years." He did not look at his son. "Some of them use it."

Itsuki was looking at the floor. Very hard. As if there were something written on it.

"That is the wheel," said Sōun. "Six hands. And every practitioner has a rank, which is how far the hands can reach. A Null cannot reach at all. A Sensitive feels the veil and nothing more. Then Postulate. Adept. Ritualist. Arbiter. Vanguard. Guardian. Each a little further. Beyond Guardian there is a rank called Singularity, and I have met two people in my life who held it, and I hope never to meet a third." He let that sit. "And every practitioner also has a stability, which is how much of themselves is left at the end of the day. Rank is how far you can reach. Stability is how much you have left to reach with. They are not the same thing. Fools mix them up." A small pause. "Fools die."

He came and stood in front of Rei and looked down at him for a while, the way a man looks at a clock that is running but showing the wrong time.

"I cannot tell your rank," he said at last. "There is something in you that makes a noise when I look. Like a radio between two stations. I do not know whether the noise is you, or something you have done to yourself." His mouth did something very small. "Your brother had the same noise. It annoyed me then, as well."

Rei's heart turned over.

"What I can see," Sōun went on, "is that you have been reading the dead for years with no training and no protection, opening and closing that door in your chest like a child slamming a gate in a temper. That costs. Your stability is very low. You are spending money you do not have." He turned away. "So we will begin with the door."

[break The door]

For the first week they did nothing but the door.

Rei knelt on the old boards from six until half past eight every morning, then ran for the train in damp clothes and arrived at Kita Ward Police HQ smelling faintly of incense, so that Ono at the front desk started calling him *the monk*. He made tea for detectives. He filed. He fetched. In the evenings he fell asleep on the sofa with Gin's book open on his chest and woke at three, always at three, and lay there with the key cold against his breastbone until the birds started.

And every morning he opened and closed the door in his chest, and was told he was doing it wrong.

He slammed it, Sōun said. He opened it all at once, like flinging a bucket of water, and shut it all at once, like slamming the boot of a car. Every time he did that, something in him tore a little. That was why the arcade in Jūjō had dropped him on the pavement. That was why the cup in Lilac had left him shaking and wet in the face.

"Slowly," said Sōun, walking behind him, his feet making no sound on the boards. "A crack. A finger's width. Look through it. Then close it, a finger's width at a time. Never slam. A slammed door is a signal. It tells everything on the other side that someone is here, and is afraid."

On the third day Grandmother Mitsu set five plain clay bowls of water in a row in front of him and tied a strip of white cloth over his eyes, quite tight, tighter than was strictly necessary.

"One of these bowls," she said, "was filled this morning from the well at a house where somebody is laid out for their funeral. The rest came out of my kitchen tap. Find it. Open slowly." He felt her knuckle tap the back of his skull, once. "If you slam, I hit you."

Rei breathed. Four in. Four held. Six out. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

The dark behind the blindfold changed. It was hard to say how. The bowls stopped being things. They became five small weights in the room, and four of them weighed nothing at all, and one of them, the fourth from the left, sat in him like a stone in his stomach. It smelled very faintly of chrysanthemums and turned earth and cheap incense, and somewhere under that, of somebody's best kimono taken out of a drawer for the last time.

"Fourth," he said. "Fourth from the left."

There was a silence in the hall.

When he pulled the blindfold down, the four cousins were all looking at him. Not the way they looked at Itsuki. Another way. The girl with the ponytail had frost on her fingertips and had not noticed.

"Three seconds," said Grandmother Mitsu, and for the first time she smiled, and it took ten years off her and put them on everyone else. "It took Takuya three months."

"It did not take me three months," said Takuya.

"It took you three months and you cried," said Grandmother Mitsu.

Takuya opened his mouth, shut it, and found something very interesting to look at in the garden.

[break Itsuki's door]

Itsuki could not open his door at all.

Every morning, while Rei practised, Itsuki stood in front of the black lacquered doorframe at the far end of the hall. The training gate. The same as the one in his father's room, two posts and a beam with a rope of white paper across the top, standing alone on the boards. A Kuroda opened it by laying a hand on the post and finding the door inside themselves. On the first morning his father showed him once, without a word. For a moment, through the frame, there was the pond outside, carp and all, as if somebody had cut a hole in the hall. Then his father took his hand away and walked off.

Itsuki put his hand on the post. Nothing.

He stood there three hours. Nothing. He stood there the next day, and the next. Nothing. The cousins stopped watching. His father never said a word to him, good or bad, which was worse than anything he could have said. By the end of the week Itsuki had a purple bruise on his forehead from leaning his head on the black post, he was smoking twice as much as before, and Grandmother Mitsu had started hitting him on the back of the head every time she smelled it on him, which was every time she came within two metres.

On Friday morning Rei sat down next to him by the gate while the others were at breakfast. Real breakfast, now. You could smell the miso soup down the corridor.

"It's not going to open," said Itsuki. He was staring at the plaster through the empty frame. "There's nothing there. I don't have one. My father's been wrong for twenty years and he's too proud to say so. That's it. That's the whole story." He laughed. "Kuroda Itsuki. The door that's a wall."

"You told me once you see what you paint."

"So?"

"So paint it."

Itsuki turned his head and looked at him.

"I'm serious." Rei pulled his knees up. "You don't open things by staring at them. You open them by knowing what's behind them. You've been standing there trying to open *a door*. Any door. Just to show him." He shrugged. "I don't know anything. I've been here a week. But you never paint *anything*. You paint one thing at a time. You paint it till it's real."

"Rei."

"Pick somewhere. Somewhere you'd actually want to go. Paint it in your head. Then open the door to that."

Itsuki stared at him for a long moment.

"That's the stupidest thing anybody has ever said to me," he said. "That is so stupid. That is so stupid it's actually annoying."

"Okay."

"Shut up. I'm thinking."

He put his hand back on the black post. He closed his eyes. Rei watched his face, and watched it change, slowly. The frown went out of it. His mouth moved a little, the way it moved when he was at the easel trying to get a colour exactly right, mixing and mixing on the palette with his tongue between his teeth. His hand on the post went still. The paint-blue in his knuckles went still.

The plaster wall through the frame wavered.

Not much. It went soft for a moment, like a road in the heat. And for half a second Rei saw, through the frame, something dark and wet and moving. Water. A dark river at night, wide and slow, and on it a single light.

Then Itsuki opened his eyes and the wall was a wall, and he snatched his hand off the post as if it were a stove, and stared at his palm.

"Did you," he said. "Did you see..."

"Yeah."

"Don't tell my father."

"Why not?"

"Because I want to do it properly first." His voice was shaking, and he was grinning like an absolute idiot, all his teeth. "I want to do it all the way, all the way through, and I want to see his stupid face. Okay? Promise me."

"Promise."

"Swear on something."

"I swear on the konbini."

Itsuki laughed so hard he had to put his forehead on the floor.

At the far end of the hall, in the dim corridor where neither of them was looking, Kuroda Sōun stood and watched his son laugh with his face on the boards in front of a doorframe. He stood there a long time. Then he turned and walked away down the old corridor, very quietly, on the soft worn boards, so that nobody would hear him go.
