#title The Six Paths

At six in the morning the training hall was cold, even in September.

It was a long, high room in the oldest part of the Kuroda house, with a floor of wide dark boards worn smooth by four hundred years of bare feet. Itsuki had not been exaggerating. In places the wood had gone soft and dipped, like the middle of an old stone step. The walls were plain cedar. At the far end hung one scroll with one character brushed on it in thick black ink. 門. *Gate.* The tall windows along one side stood open, and the morning came in grey and damp, with the smell of the wet garden and the slap of a carp in the pond.

[need ch11_training_hall | A long high old wooden training hall at dawn, wide dark floorboards worn into soft dips, tall open windows onto a wet garden, a single scroll with the kanji 門 at the end, young people in plain grey training clothes kneeling in a row]

Mrs. Fujii brought him in through the small door in the gate at a quarter to six and walked him across the wet garden without a word. He had come alone. Itsuki had walked him as far as the corner of the lane, in the dark, in his painting hoodie, with two cans of coffee, and given him one, and stopped there.

"This is as far as I go," he had said. "Don't let them make you cry. If they make you cry, cry in the toilet."

There were six people in the hall. Four were young, cousins of Itsuki's, in plain grey training clothes, kneeling in a row like birds on a wire. One was an old woman with her white hair in a bun, kneeling by the wall with a teapot and a small wooden box beside her. And one was Kuroda Sōun, standing in the middle of the floor in a plain dark kimono, hands behind his back.

The cousins looked at Rei as if a stray dog had walked into a restaurant, sat down, and asked for the menu.

Rei's stomach growled into the silence, loud as a door. He had not eaten breakfast. Nobody had told him that not eating breakfast would be this loud.

[break What there is]

"Before you learn anything," said Sōun, "you will learn what there is to learn."

He walked slowly down the hall as he spoke. His voice was quiet and it filled the room without trying.

"There are six paths. Six ways a person can touch the veil. Most who can touch it at all have one path open. Some have two. Very few have more. Each path is a different kind of hand. You do not choose your hands. You are born with them, and then you spend the rest of your life finding out what they are for."

He stopped by the first cousin. A tall young man with a shaved head and a jaw like a brick.

"Telekinesis. The hand that moves things."

The young man did not move. On the boards in front of him lay six flat floor cushions in a row. The first one lifted off the floor, slowly, as if somebody invisible were picking it up by two corners. It rose to the height of his chest and hung there. The second rose and laid itself on top of the first. Then the third. Then all six hung in the air, stacked neat as folded laundry, and the young man had not lifted a finger. His face was perfectly calm. Only one small vein stood out at his temple and pulsed.

"Down, Takuya," said Sōun. The cushions came down one at a time, like snow deciding where to land.

He moved to a girl of about sixteen with a long ponytail and very straight shoulders.

"Psychokinesis. The hand that makes heat, and cold, and lightning."

The girl held up her right hand, palm open. On it stood a short white candle, unlit. She looked at it. The wick went black, smoked, and caught. A small yellow flame stood up, steady. Then it went blue. Then it went out, and the candle was furred all over with frost, white and glittering, and the girl's breath came out of her mouth as a cloud in the September air.

"It is the bluntest of the six," said Sōun. "It does not talk to the dead. It burns them. It burns the bodies they are wearing, too." He looked at Rei. "Remember that. One day it will matter."

The old woman by the wall did not wait to be introduced. She crooked one finger at Rei. He went, and knelt in front of her. She took his right hand in both of hers and turned it over. On the side of his thumb was a paper cut, a stupid little thing from turning the pages of Gin's book too fast at three in the morning, that he had stopped noticing.

"Psychometabolism," said Sōun. "The hand that reads the body. Living or dead. And mends it."

The old woman held his hand and closed her eyes. Rei watched the cut. Slowly, while he watched, the angry red edges went pink. The pink went pale. The skin drew together from both sides like a zip pulled shut. In less than a minute there was nothing on his thumb but a thin white line.

"You don't sleep," the old woman said, eyes still closed. "You wake up at the same hour every night and lie there. You drink coffee out of cans like a taxi driver. You smoke. Your heart is fine." She opened her eyes. "Your stomach is very angry with you."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Grandmother Mitsu. Everybody calls me that, even people I don't like." She patted his cheek. It was more of a slap that had changed its mind halfway. "Go away. Eat lunch."

He went back to his place rubbing his face. One of the cousins, the tall one with the shaved head, was trying very hard not to smile.

[break The other three]

"Telepathy," said Sōun. "The hand that touches minds."

He did not demonstrate it with a cousin. He looked at Rei.

*You are frightened,* said a voice inside Rei's head.

It was Sōun's voice. Low and dry and careful, exactly the same as the voice that came out of his mouth, except that it did not come out of his mouth. It was inside, behind Rei's eyes, close as his own thinking. Rei jerked back so hard he nearly went over. Two of the cousins laughed. Sōun did not.

*You are frightened,* the voice went on, unhurried, *and you are angry, and under the anger you are very tired. And you keep a door in your chest, and you believe it was given to you so that you could sleep.* A pause, as if it were checking something. *It was not.*

Then it was gone, and Rei's head was his own again, and he was breathing like he had run up the hill. He felt as if someone had opened the bathroom door on him and then, worse, apologised.

"That was rude," Sōun said aloud. "I did it so that you would know exactly how rude it is. A telepath can read the surface of a person without asking. Fear. Anger. What they want. What they are hiding. It is the easiest of the six to abuse and the hardest to be trusted with." He looked at the cousins who had laughed until they stopped. "A good telepath spends his whole life asking permission. Most of them are very lonely people."

Rei would remember that sentence for a long time. Later, he would know exactly who it had been about.

"Clairsentience," said Sōun. "The hand that reads what has happened. Rooms. Objects. Places. The structure of a thing. Who has power over whom, and where it will break." He looked at Rei again. "That one, I think, has been reading you for years."

"And the sixth?" said Rei.

Sōun turned to the far end of the hall, where the scroll hung.

"Teleportation. The hand that opens doors. You saw it. To step from one place into another without crossing what lies between. It is the rarest of the six, and it is the Kuroda path. Every person in this family has carried it in the blood for four hundred years." A very small pause. "Nearly every person."

The cousins all looked at the floor at the same moment, as if they had practised it.

"That is the wheel," said Sōun. "Six hands. Some people can reach further with theirs than others. The Division writes it down and gives it names. Sensitive at the bottom, Vanguard and Guardian at the top, and a ladder in between. Paper is often wrong." He let that sit. "And every practitioner also has a stability, which is how much of themselves is left at the end of the day. How far you reach is one thing. How much you have left to reach with is another. Fools mix them up." A small pause. "Fools die."

He came and stood in front of Rei and looked down at him for a while, like a man looking at a clock that is running but showing the wrong time.

"I cannot tell your rank," he said at last. "There is something in you that makes a noise when I look. Like a radio between two stations. I do not know whether the noise is you, or something you have done to yourself." His mouth did something very small. "Your brother had the same noise. It annoyed me then, as well."

Rei's heart turned over.

"What I can see," Sōun went on, "is that you have been reading the dead for years with no training and no protection, opening and closing that door in your chest like a child slamming a gate in a temper. That costs. Your stability is very low. You are spending money you do not have." He turned away. "So we will begin with the door."

[break The door]

For the first week they did nothing but the door.

Rei knelt on the old boards from six until half past eight every morning. Then he walked out through the gate into Nezu with his knees humming and ate the biggest thing the konbini on the corner would sell him, standing at the window. Then he texted Itsuki, because he had promised. *Alive. Knees dead. Your grandmother slapped me.* Itsuki always answered inside a minute, at an hour when Itsuki was never awake. *she does that when she likes you. when she doesnt like you she doesnt touch you at all. ask me how i know.*

The afternoons were for Gin. He walked the streets around Café Asahi. He rode the Keihin-Tōhoku up and down and watched the platforms. He read the book. On Tuesday he sat at the counter and Matsuda Keiko made the coffee for the empty stool and gave it to him, and neither of them said anything about the window. In the evenings he fell asleep on the sofa with Gin's book open on his chest and woke at three, always at three, and lay there with the key cold against his breastbone until the birds started.

And every morning he opened and closed the door in his chest, and was told he was doing it wrong.

He slammed it, Sōun said. He opened it all at once, like flinging a bucket of water, and shut it all at once, like slamming the boot of a car. Every time he did that, something in him tore a little. That was why the arcade in Jūjō had dropped him on the pavement. That was why the cup in Lilac had left him shaking and wet in the face.

"Slowly," said Sōun, walking behind him, his feet making no sound on the boards. "A crack. A finger's width. Look through it. Then close it, a finger's width at a time. Never slam. A slammed door is a signal. It tells everything on the other side that someone is here, and is afraid."

On the third day Grandmother Mitsu set five plain clay bowls of water in a row in front of him and tied a strip of white cloth over his eyes, quite tight, tighter than was strictly necessary.

"One of these bowls," she said, "was filled this morning from the well at a house where somebody is laid out for their funeral. The rest came out of my kitchen tap. Find it. Open slowly." He felt her knuckle tap the back of his skull, once. "If you slam, I hit you."

Rei breathed. Four in. Four held. Six out. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

The dark behind the blindfold changed. It was hard to say how. The bowls stopped being things. They became five small weights in the room, and four of them weighed nothing at all, and one of them, the fourth from the left, sat in him like a stone in his stomach. It smelled very faintly of chrysanthemums and turned earth and cheap incense, and somewhere under that, of somebody's best kimono taken out of a drawer for the last time.

"Fourth," he said. "Fourth from the left."

There was a silence in the hall.

When he pulled the blindfold down, the four cousins were all looking at him. Not the way they had looked at him on the first morning. Another way. The girl with the ponytail had frost on her fingertips and had not noticed.

"Three seconds," said Grandmother Mitsu, and for the first time she smiled, and it took ten years off her and put them on everyone else. "It took Takuya three months."

"It did not take me three months," said Takuya.

"It took you three months and you cried," said Grandmother Mitsu.

Takuya opened his mouth, shut it, and found something very interesting to look at in the garden.

[break The question]

On Saturday the cousins went to breakfast, real breakfast now, and the smell of miso soup came down the corridor, and Rei knelt alone at the far end of the hall putting his shoes on with fingers that still did not quite belong to him.

At the far end stood the training gate. Two black lacquered posts and a beam, with a rope of white paper across the top, alone on the boards. All week he had watched the cousins go to it, one at a time, lay a hand on the post, and open a hole in the hall onto the pond outside, carp and all. Takuya could hold it for a minute. The girl with the ponytail could hold it for ten seconds and then had to sit down. Nobody had told Rei to try. Nobody needed to. It was not his door.

Sōun was standing beside it. Rei had not heard him come.

"Does he still paint?" said Sōun.

He did not say who. He was looking at the plaster through the empty frame.

"Every day," said Rei.

"What?"

Rei thought of the studio on the third floor at Geidai. The laundromat. The fish market. The girl in the yellow raincoat. A crowd under umbrellas that would not finish. And a small wet canvas of a walkway at night with a key lying on the concrete, finished at midnight, four hours before the key was there.

He kept that one. It was not his to give.

"People," he said. "On trains. In laundromats. Rivers. He paints rivers a lot. He paints the thing about people that they don't know is showing."

Sōun was quiet for a long time.

"His mother paints," he said at last. "In the evenings. When the training is done." Then, as if he had dropped something and was picking it up again before anyone saw, "The light in this hall is good in the mornings. Tell him that."

He walked away down the old corridor, very quietly, on the soft worn boards, so that nobody would hear him go.

[break The konbini]

Rei told him that afternoon, standing at the konbini window in Ueno with two cans of corn soup between them, because some things you do not say by text.

Itsuki did not say anything for a while. He turned his can round and round in his hands.

"He said that?"

"Word for word."

"He means the light is good," said Itsuki. "That's all he means. He's talking about the windows. He's a man who talks about windows." He drank his soup. It was too hot. He drank it anyway. "That's all he means."

"Okay."

"Don't say okay like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you know something." Itsuki put the can down on the counter and looked out at the street, at the pigeons on the railing of the footbridge and the people going home. His face was doing something he did not want it to do, and he let it, because it was only Rei. "My mother paints," he said. "Watercolours. On Sunday afternoons, after she's spent the whole morning in that hall teaching the little cousins to open doors. In that house you're allowed to paint when the real work's done." He turned the can round once. "He never says it. Twenty years, he talks about her like she only exists in the training hall. And he tells *you*. On a Saturday. By the door I haven't got."

He was quiet for a while.

"I used to sit with her," he said. "Sundays. On the floor of her room, with the screens open, while she painted. It was the only time in the whole week she wasn't a Kuroda. She wasn't teaching anybody anything. She wasn't listening for the dead. She was just my mum with water on the brush, humming." He shrugged. "So when it turned out I didn't have a door, I thought, fine. I'll have that instead. And I went and got into Geidai to learn to do it properly." He picked the can up again. "That's all. That's the whole sad story. Don't make the face."

Rei did not say anything. Some things you also do not answer.

After a while Itsuki picked the can up again.

"Tell me about the bowls," he said. "Tell me about Takuya crying. Slowly. Don't skip anything."
