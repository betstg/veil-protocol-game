#title Bones of the Trade

In December the Kuroda took them across.

"Until now you have opened doors from this side," said Sōun, on the first Monday of the month, standing by the black frame in the cold training hall. "From the living side. Today you will learn to stand on the other side of a door and hold it open for someone coming back." He looked at the line of cousins, and at Itsuki, and at Rei. "This is the threshold. The space between a door and the room behind it. It is not the veil. Not yet. It is the step before the veil. Every Kuroda learns to stand on it. Every Kuroda learns how cold it is."

"How cold is it?" said Itsuki.

His father looked at him. "Cold enough," he said, "that some of us do not come back. That is why we hold the door for each other."

Nobody laughed. Even Itsuki did not make a joke.

[break The threshold]

They went one at a time, in order of age. Takuya first, with the shaved head, stepping through the black frame into what looked like nothing at all, a grey shimmer like heat over a road, and standing there for one minute, and stepping back, pale and smiling. Then the girl with the ponytail. Then the other cousins. Each one went through and stood on the threshold for a minute and came back. Each time, Sōun stood with his hand on the black post, holding the door, his face like stone.

Rei did not go through. He was not a Kuroda. His job was to stand at the side and read. To keep the door in his chest open a finger's width and watch the threshold, and say if anything came near.

Itsuki went last.

He was nervous. Rei could see it in the way he kept wiping his hands on his training trousers, in the way he did not look at his father. He had only had his gate for four weeks. The others had had theirs for years.

"Hold the door yourself," said Sōun. "From the inside. I will hold it from here. If you feel anything pull you, come back at once. Do not be proud."

"I'm never proud," said Itsuki. "Ask anyone."

He stepped through.

For thirty seconds, everything was fine.

Rei watched him through the frame. Itsuki stood in the grey shimmer with his arms folded across his chest against the cold, and he turned round and looked back at them through the door and pulled a face, the face he made when a painting went wrong. Grandmother Mitsu snorted. One of the cousins laughed.

Then Rei's wrist went cold.

Not the clear cold of the latch saying *something is near*. A burning cold, so sudden and so sharp that he gasped and grabbed his wrist with his other hand. Through the door in his chest, through the finger's width, he saw it.

Something was standing behind Itsuki.

It was very tall. It was very thin. It was grey, the same grey as the threshold, so that it was almost invisible against it, like a moth on bark. It had no face. Where its face should have been, there was only a tall, narrow, black gap, like a door standing a little open onto a dark room. It bent over Itsuki from behind, slowly, the way a heron bends over water.

"Behind him!" Rei shouted. "Sōun-sama, behind him!"

Sōun's face changed. Itsuki started to turn.

The tall grey thing put its long arms round him.

[break What came back]

Itsuki did not scream. That was the first thing. He opened his mouth, and no sound came out at all. Rei saw his eyes go wide, and wider, until there was white all round them. He saw Itsuki's hands come up, clawing at nothing, at the arms that held him, which were too thin and too grey to see properly. He saw Itsuki's feet leave the threshold.

Then the tall grey thing folded itself down, and into him, like a long coat being pulled on.

It took less than a second. One moment there were two shapes in the grey shimmer. Then there was only one.

"Itsuki!" Sōun had both hands on the black post now. Rei had never heard him raise his voice. "Itsuki, come back. Come back to the door. *Now.*"

Itsuki turned round.

He walked back toward the frame. But he walked backward. Facing away from them, toward the grey nothing, his heels finding the way, one careful step at a time, the way you walk backward down a ladder. When he came through the black frame into the hall, he was still facing the wrong way. He stopped. He stood with his back to all of them.

Then his head turned.

Not the way Mrs. Tachibana's had, all the way round. Only partly. Only until one side of his face was looking at them over his shoulder. And it was not quite Itsuki's face any more.

The left side of it was smiling. Only the left. The corner of the mouth pulled up, high and tight, showing the side teeth, like a dog showing its teeth. The right side of the face did not move at all. It hung slack and still, as if that side were asleep, or dead. The line between the smiling side and the dead side ran straight down the middle of his face, from the forehead to the chin, and it was perfectly sharp, like a fold in a sheet of paper.

His eyes had changed too. Both pupils had swollen until his eyes were almost entirely black, wet and shining, and they were moving. Not looking at one thing. Taking in everything. The hall, the windows, the gate, every person in the room, the cousins, Grandmother Mitsu, his father, Rei, all at once, with a fierce bright attention that no human face could keep up for more than a moment. It was the look of something that could feel every heartbeat in the room and was already choosing what it wanted.

"Hello, little door," said Itsuki's mouth.

The voice was Itsuki's voice. That was what made Rei's knees go weak. It was his exact voice, his tone, the way he spoke when he was teasing. But the words came out in the wrong order. Not completely. Just enough. As if someone had learned to speak by listening to him through a wall, and got almost everything right.

"Hello door little. Nice so opened you. Twenty years, years twenty, knocking I've been." The left side of the mouth stretched wider. "Thank you, inside let me."

His nose was bleeding. Both nostrils, thick and dark, running over his lips and his chin and dripping onto the soft old boards of the hall. He did not notice. He crouched down, slowly, on the boards, and put one paint-stained finger into the blood on the floor, and began to draw.

He drew a door.

A rectangle, with a small circle for a handle. Then another, beside it. Then another. Fast. Neat. Doors. Doors. Doors. His finger moved over the old wood leaving trails of red, and every door he drew was standing a little open.

Grandmother Mitsu was moving toward him, very slowly, with her hands held out low in front of her, palms open, the way you walk toward a frightened dog.

"Itsuki," she said softly. "Itsuki, child, it's Mitsu. Look at me."

The face turned up toward her. The smiling side. The dead side.

"Old woman," it said. "Old woman bones. Your knees hurt in the cold. Hurt, hurt." It giggled. Itsuki's giggle, exactly. "Hurt more soon."

And it stood up and hit her.

[break Broken]

It moved like Itsuki had never moved in his life. Fast and loose, like a thing made of rope. It swung his arm round in a wide flat circle and caught Grandmother Mitsu across the side of the head and she went down on the boards like a dropped coat. One of the cousins shouted. Takuya ran at it. It turned and looked at him, and Takuya stopped running and flew backward across the hall as if he had been pulled on a wire, and hit the wall, and slid down it, and did not get up.

Then it turned to Rei.

"*You,*" it said. And for the first time, the dead side of the face twitched. "You. The one with all the room. The one he dreams about. Come here, room. Come here, here come."

Rei's wrist was so cold it burned. He did not run. He did not know why he did not run. He stood where he was, and he put his left hand flat on his chest, over the latch, over the key under his shirt, and he did not let the door in his chest open even one more millimetre.

"Give him back," he said.

"No," said Itsuki's mouth, smiling. "Mine. Little door mine. So many doors in him. I'll open all of them. Every single one. And walk through." It came toward him across the boards, leaving red footprints, smiling with half its face. "And then you."

Sōun stepped between them.

He did not hurry. He walked across the hall at an ordinary pace and stood in front of his son with his hands at his sides. He was not looking at the thing. He was looking at Itsuki. Past the smile. Past the huge black hungry eyes. As if he were looking through a window into a room at someone standing very far back.

"Itsuki," he said quietly. "I know you can hear me."

The thing laughed. It raised Itsuki's arm to strike.

Sōun put out his right hand and laid it flat on his son's chest, over the heart. Exactly the way he laid it on the black post of the gate.

And opened a door.

Rei felt it. The whole hall felt it. A wrench in the air, like a building shifting on its foundations. Sōun opened a door in the middle of his own son's body, a door from Itsuki's chest into somewhere else, somewhere very far and very cold, and the tall grey thing was pulled toward it like water toward a drain.

It screamed. Through Itsuki's mouth. Not in Itsuki's voice now. A sound like the hinges of a great iron gate, rusted for a thousand years, being forced open.

It held on.

It held on with everything it had, to everything it could reach. To Itsuki's heart. His lungs. His bones. And Sōun pulled, and the thing held on, and something had to give.

Rei heard Itsuki's ribs break. Three sharp cracks, one after another, like dry sticks. He saw Itsuki's left arm, the one raised to strike, bend backward at the elbow. He saw Itsuki's body go stiff from head to foot, every muscle locked. And then he saw the black shrink out of both eyes at once, back to two small ordinary dots, and Itsuki fell forward into his father's arms like a puppet with its strings cut.

The tall grey thing was not gone. Rei could feel it. Deep inside, curled tight round something, clinging. But it was not driving any more. It was hiding.

And Itsuki was not breathing.

[break Bones of the trade]

Grandmother Mitsu saved him. She came up off the floor with blood running down from her temple and crawled to him on her knees and put both her hands on his chest, over his broken ribs, and pressed. Rei saw her face go grey with the effort. He saw her lips move. He saw, under her hands, Itsuki's chest jerk, once, twice. Then lift. Then fall. Then lift again.

"Breathing," she said. Her voice was a croak. "Breathing. Heart's going. Three ribs. The arm. The thing's still in him. Deep. I can feel it in his spine, like a cold wire." She sat back on her heels and swayed. "I can't get it out. Nobody in this house can get it out. Not without killing him."

Sōun knelt on the boards with his son's head in his lap. He did not say anything. His face had not changed at all. Only his hand, resting on Itsuki's hair, was shaking so hard that Rei could see it from across the hall.

One of the older cousins, a man of about thirty who had come in from the corridor at the noise, looked at the blood on the floor. At the drawn doors. At Takuya against the wall, groaning now. At Grandmother Mitsu. At Itsuki, white and still in his father's lap.

"It happens," the cousin said. He said it quietly, almost kindly, the way you say something everyone already knows. "It's the threshold. It happens one time in fifty. It happened to Uncle Kenzō. It happened to Aunt Hisa's boy." He shook his head. "Bones of the trade. That's all. Bones of the trade."

Rei stared at him.

He thought of Gin, at the kitchen table, every week for seven years, putting a bowl of rice in front of a skinny twelve-year-old with paint on his fingers who never said thank you and always came back. He thought of Gin standing ten minutes in front of the worst paintings. Gin would have heard that sentence, and put down his tea, very slowly, the way he did before he said something he would not take back.

"Bones of the trade," he said.

"That's what we call it." The cousin looked at him, puzzled, as if surprised he needed to be told. "Every family has them. Every family loses some. That's the price of the gate."

Rei looked at Itsuki. At the paint on his fingers, mixed with blood now. At the red doors drawn on the old floor, every one of them standing a little open.

*Bones of the trade.*

Itsuki had stood in front of a black frame for three weeks with a bruise on his forehead, trying to open a door he did not want, because his father asked him to, because Rei needed him to. He had run to Higashi-Jūjō in his slippers and opened a door four kilometres long. He had said *I'm in* under the station lights.

And now he was lying on the floor with his ribs broken and something curled round his spine, and a man was calling it a price.

"No," said Rei.

Everyone looked at him.

"No," he said again. He did not know he was going to say it until he said it. "He's not bones. He's not a price. He's Itsuki." His voice was shaking and he did not care. "You don't get to call him that. None of you. Not ever."

The hall was silent.

On the floor, in his father's lap, Itsuki made a small sound. His eyes did not open. But his right hand, the one that was not broken, moved on the boards. One finger. Through the blood. Slowly.

It drew a small, clumsy shape. Not a door.

A bridge.

Then it went still.

Kuroda Sōun looked down at the bridge. Then he looked up, across the hall, at Rei. And for the first time since Rei had known him, there was something in his face that Rei could read.

It was shame.

"Call the Division," said Sōun, very quietly, to no one in particular. "Tell them the Kuroda need help."

Nobody in the hall moved. Nobody in that family had ever said those words.

"Call them," said Sōun. "*Now.*"
