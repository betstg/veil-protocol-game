#title Bones of the Trade

The job was Itsuki's. He found it himself, and he was very proud of that.

"A gallery in Ginza," he said, on the first Saturday in December, lying on Rei's kitchen floor with his feet up on a chair and his phone held over his face. "Little one. Upstairs, two rooms, very expensive coffee. The owner knows my teacher at Geidai. She's got a painting she bought at an estate sale in Yanaka. Some old painter who died last year with a whole house full of canvases and no family. She hung it in the back room." He turned the phone so Rei could see a photograph. "And now nobody will go in the back room."

The photograph showed an oil painting in a heavy gilt frame. A woman in a dark blue kimono, sitting at a window, sewing. It was very good. Rei could see that even on a small screen. The light on her hands. The thread. The patience of it.

"What's wrong with it?"

"The owner says the woman moves. Not in the painting. In the room. At night, on the security camera, there's somebody sitting at the window in the back room, sewing. Nobody's there in the morning." Itsuki lowered the phone onto his chest. "She asked my teacher if he knew anyone. He said, *I've got a student who paints things that aren't there.*" He grinned at the ceiling. "So she's paying me. Thirty thousand yen. Me. For being weird. I've been weird for free my whole life."

"You can't do anything about it, Itsuki. You don't..."

"I know I don't. That's why you're coming." Itsuki sat up. "You read it. I look at the painting and tell you what's wrong with it. I'm good at that, you know I am. And then you do your thing with your chest, and we split the money, and I buy you dinner for once in your life." He held out his hand, palm up. "It's mine, Rei. I found it. Let me have one."

Rei looked at him. He thought about saying no. He thought about how Itsuki had sat on a kerb in Higashi-Jūjō in his slippers and drawn him a way out of a folded building.

"Okay," he said. "One."

[break Yanaka]

The gallery was in Ginza, but the owner wanted them to see the house first. "So you understand where it came from," she said on the phone, in a bright nervous voice. "I have the keys still. The estate agent hasn't sold it."

The house was in Yanaka, on a lane of old wooden houses behind the cemetery, ten minutes' walk from Nezu. It was a small two-storey house with a tiled roof and a sliding front door with frosted glass, and a dead persimmon tree in a pot by the step. The owner of the gallery met them outside. She was about fifty, very thin, in a camel coat and expensive glasses, and she held the key out at arm's length, as if she did not want to be any closer to the house than her arm.

"I'll wait in the café on the corner," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm not brave about these things."

Inside, the house smelled of turpentine and dust and old oil paint. Rei knew the smell. It was the smell of Itsuki's studio, only older, and colder, and sadder. Every wall was covered in canvases. They were stacked against the walls three and four deep, and hung up the stairs, and leaned in the corners of the kitchen. Hundreds of them. The same woman, again and again. In the dark blue kimono. Sewing at a window. Cooking. Sleeping. Laughing at something out of the frame. Young, then older, then old. Then, on the last canvases, in the room upstairs, the window with nobody at it. Just the empty window, and the light, and a sewing basket on the floor.

[need ch27_yanaka_house | The upstairs room of an old wooden house in Yanaka on a grey December afternoon, the walls covered in oil paintings of the same woman in a dark blue kimono, an empty window with a sewing basket below it, a young man in a grey hoodie standing very still in the middle looking at the paintings]

Itsuki stood in the middle of the upstairs room and did not say anything for a long time.

"He painted her for fifty years," he said at last. "His wife. You can see it. He never got bored of her. Not once. Look at the hands. He did her hands a thousand times and every time it's like he's never seen them before." He looked at the last canvas. The empty window. "And then she died. And he kept painting the window."

Rei opened the door in his chest. A finger's width.

The house was full of grief. Old, soft, thick grief, settled into the walls and the floorboards like the smell of turpentine. A man's grief. It did not want anything. It was only sad. But it was not alone. Somewhere under it, very faint, there was something else. Something thin and cold and patient, that did not belong to the old painter or his wife. Something that had come into this house because a lonely old man had left a door open in himself for fifty years, painting one face, and had never closed it.

"There's something else here," Rei said. "Not him. Not her."

"Where?"

"I don't know. It's thin. It's hiding." He breathed. "Itsuki, don't touch anything."

Itsuki was not listening. He was standing in front of the last canvas, the empty window, with his head on one side. The way he stood in front of his own paintings when something in them was wrong and he could not see what.

"There's somebody in this one," he said. "Look. In the window glass. In the reflection." He leaned closer. "He's painted somebody standing behind the person looking at the painting. Very faint. Tall. Grey. You can only see it if you stand exactly..."

He stood exactly there.

[break What came in]

Rei felt it before he saw anything. The thin cold thing under the grief turned round, fast, the way a fish turns. It had been waiting. Fifty years in a house with one man who left his door open, and now the man was dead, and here was a young man standing in front of a canvas with every door in him unlocked. No training. No tattoo. No ward. Nobody had ever taught Itsuki to shut anything, because his family had decided when he was ten that he had nothing to shut.

"Itsuki, *move*..."

Itsuki turned his head. He opened his mouth.

Something tall and grey and thin, the colour of the dust in the corners, stepped out of the reflection in the painted glass, and bent over him from behind, slowly, like a heron bending over water, and put its long arms round him.

Itsuki did not scream. That was the first thing. He opened his mouth, and no sound came out at all. Rei saw his eyes go wide, and wider, until there was white all round them. He saw Itsuki's hands come up, clawing at nothing, at arms that were too thin and too grey to see properly.

Then the tall grey thing folded itself down, and into him, like a long coat being pulled on.

It took less than a second.

Rei was across the room. He had salt in his hand. He had iron in his pocket. He did not know which to use. He did not know if either would do anything to a thing that was inside his friend. He grabbed Itsuki by both shoulders and turned him round.

It was not quite Itsuki's face any more.

The left side of it was smiling. Only the left. The corner of the mouth pulled up, high and tight, showing the side teeth. The right side of the face did not move at all. It hung slack and still, as if that side were asleep. The line between the smiling side and the still side ran straight down the middle of his face, from the forehead to the chin, sharp as a fold in a sheet of paper.

His pupils had swollen until his eyes were almost entirely black, and they were moving. Taking in everything. The room, the canvases, the window, Rei, all at once.

"Hello," said Itsuki's mouth.

It was Itsuki's voice. His exact voice, his tone, the way he spoke when he was teasing. But the words came out a little wrong. As if someone had learned to talk by listening to him through a wall.

"Hello, hello. So many doors in this one. So many. Nobody ever locked a single one." The smiling side stretched wider. "Fifty years with the old man. One door. One face, one face, one face. Boring. This one has a hundred."

His nose was bleeding. Both nostrils, thick and dark, running over his lips and his chin. He did not notice.

"Give him back," said Rei.

"No." It giggled. Itsuki's giggle, exactly. "Mine now."

Rei threw the salt. It went into Itsuki's face and his hair and his open mouth, and the thing laughed and spat it out. He took the iron filings out of his pocket and held them in his fist and did not throw them, because he could see in his head exactly what iron would do to the skin of somebody he loved, and he could not.

The thing saw him not throw it. It saw everything.

"Soft," it said, and hit him.

It moved like Itsuki had never moved in his life. Fast and loose, like a thing made of rope. It swung Itsuki's arm round in a wide flat circle and caught Rei on the side of the head, and the room went white, and Rei was on the floor among the canvases with fifty years of the same woman's face looking down at him.

He got up. He did not know how. Itsuki's body was at the top of the stairs. It was going down them backward, facing up, its heels finding the steps, smiling with half its face. It was going out. Into Yanaka. Into the city.

Rei did not think. He went down the stairs after it and caught it at the bottom, by the sliding door, and got his arms round it from behind, round Itsuki's chest, and held on.

The thing fought. Itsuki was thin, but the thing did not care about Itsuki's body. It threw him against the frosted glass of the door, and the glass cracked. It drove an elbow back into Rei's ribs, and Rei heard something go. He held on. It bent Itsuki's own arm back at an angle that arms do not go, trying to get loose, and Rei heard that go too, a sound like a stick breaking. He held on.

He opened the door in his chest. Not a finger's width. Wide.

He did not know what he was doing. He only knew that the thing wanted doors, and he had one, and he held it open and pressed his chest against Itsuki's back and thought, as loud as he could, *Here. Here's a door. Leave him. Come here.*

The thing stopped fighting. For one second it was still. Rei felt it look, through Itsuki's back, through his own ribs, at the open door in his chest.

Then it laughed, very softly, in Itsuki's voice.

"No," it said. "Yours has teeth."

And it pulled itself in, deep, down into Itsuki, curled tight round something in the middle of him, and let go of the body.

Itsuki went limp in Rei's arms. His head fell back against Rei's shoulder. The black shrank out of both his eyes at once, back to two small ordinary dots. His face was his own face again, both sides of it, grey-white and wet with blood.

He was breathing. Just.

The thing was still in him. Rei could feel it. Not driving any more. Hiding.

[break Nezu]

Rei did not call an ambulance. He thought about it, standing in the hall of the dead painter's house with Itsuki in his arms and blood running down the side of his own face. He thought about doctors, and X-rays, and a young man in a hospital bed with something inside him that no doctor could see, waking up in the night with half his face smiling.

Nezu was ten minutes' walk.

He carried him. He did not know he could. Itsuki was thin, but he was tall, and his arm hung wrong, and every step went through Rei's ribs like a knife. People in the lane stared. A man on a bicycle stopped and asked if he needed help, and Rei said no, and kept walking. He walked down the hill out of Yanaka with Itsuki's head on his shoulder and Itsuki's blood soaking into his coat, past the cemetery, past the shrine, into the lanes of Nezu, to the long grey wall and the dark gate.

The camera in the roof tiles turned to look at him.

He did not knock. He did not need to. The small door in the gate opened before he got there, and Mrs. Fujii looked out, and saw what he was carrying, and her face did something Rei had never seen a face do.

Then she was shouting. Mrs. Fujii, who had never raised her voice in her life. Shouting for Grandmother Mitsu, for Sōun-sama, for anybody, running back across the gravel in her white socks.

[break Bones of the trade]

They laid him on the floor of the training hall, because it was the nearest room with enough space. On the soft old boards, under the scroll with its one character. *Gate.*

Grandmother Mitsu knelt beside him and put both hands on his chest and closed her eyes. Rei watched her face go grey with the effort. He saw, under her hands, Itsuki's breathing steady, and deepen. She moved her hands to the broken arm, and held it, and the swelling went down a little.

"Two ribs," she said. Her voice was a croak. "The arm. I can hold those. I can mend those, slowly." She opened her eyes. "The thing is still in him. Deep. Holding on to everything it can reach. I can feel it holding. I can't get it out. Nobody in this house can get it out. Not without killing him."

Sōun knelt on the boards at his son's head. He did not say anything. His face had not changed at all. Only his hand, resting on Itsuki's hair, was shaking so hard that Rei could see it from across the hall.

Cousins had come. They stood in the doorway, and along the walls, in their grey training clothes. One of the older ones, a man of about thirty, looked at Itsuki. At the blood. At Rei with his swollen face and his arms wrapped round his own ribs.

"It happens," the cousin said. He said it quietly, almost kindly, as you say something everyone already knows. "Somebody without training, without a gate, walks into the wrong room. They've got nothing to shut. It happened to Aunt Hisa's boy." He shook his head. "Bones of the trade. That's all. Bones of the trade."

Rei stared at him.

He thought of Gin, at the kitchen table, every week for eight years, putting a bowl of rice in front of a skinny twelve-year-old with paint on his fingers who never said thank you and always came back. He thought of Gin standing ten minutes in front of the worst paintings. Gin would have heard that sentence, and put down his cup, very slowly, as he did before he said something he would not take back.

"Bones of the trade," Rei said.

"That's what we call it." The cousin looked at him, puzzled. "Every family has them. Every family loses some."

Rei looked at Itsuki. At the paint on his fingers, mixed with blood now.

*Bones of the trade.*

They had looked at a ten-year-old boy and decided he had nothing to shut, and so nobody had ever taught him to shut anything. And they had let him go and live on his own near Ueno and paint, and sent the rent, and not called. And now he was lying on the floor of their training hall with his arm broken and something curled inside him, and a man was calling it a price.

"No," said Rei.

Everyone looked at him.

"No," he said again. He did not know he was going to say it until he said it. "He's not bones. He's not a price. He's Itsuki." His voice was shaking and he did not care. "You never taught him anything. You never even tried. And now you don't get to call him that. None of you. Not ever."

The hall was silent.

On the floor, Itsuki made a small sound. His eyes did not open. But his right hand, the one that was not broken, moved on the boards. One finger. Through the blood on his own chin, and then on the old wood. Slowly.

It drew a small, clumsy rectangle. A door. Standing open.

Then it went still.

Kuroda Sōun looked down at the door his son had drawn on the floor of the hall. Then he looked up, across the hall, at Rei. And for the first time since Rei had known him, there was something in his face that Rei could read.

It was shame.

"Call the Division," said Sōun, very quietly, to no one in particular. "Tell them the Kuroda need help."

Nobody in the hall moved. Nobody in that family had ever said those words.

"Call them," said Sōun. "*Now.*"
