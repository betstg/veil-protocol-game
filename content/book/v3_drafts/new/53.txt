#title Hinata and Ren

In the first week of July the Director called Shimizu's team into his office and told them who the fifth would be.

Riki was asleep on his cushion by the desk with his bad leg stuck out. Adachi stood at the window with the worn train pass turning in his fingers. Moriya had been given the only armchair, because of the brace. Kato stood by the door. Rei stood by Kato.

"Usami Hinata," said Adachi. "From the Vessel Program. Telepath. Twenty-four. He has been in this building for two years and he has never once been allowed outside it without an escort." He turned round. "I would like that to change."

Shimizu did not say anything. The small line between her eyebrows went deeper.

"Another telepath," said Kato. His voice was level. "On a team that has one."

"Yes."

"After what happened."

"Because of what happened," said Adachi. "What came into Tendo could hear everything on the fourth floor, and the only person in this building who noticed was you. I would like there to be two of you." He looked at Kato with his pale resting eyes. "And I would like you to meet him before you decide you don't like him."

Kato looked at the floor. A strand of his hair had come loose.

"Fine," he said.

[break The top floor]

The Vessel floor was at the end of the corridor on the eighth, behind the keypad door. Rei had been up once before, in February, with Okada and the silver watch. He remembered the shoes in a heap in the hallway and the smell of curry. Tonight it smelled of butter and burnt sugar.

The young man who opened the door had flour on his forearms. He was slight and soft-featured, with dark brown hair a little too long, pushed back with a pink plastic clip shaped like a strawberry, and he wore an apron with a cartoon hedgehog on the front over a faded blue sweatshirt. He was perhaps twenty-four. He had a face people liked before they had decided anything about it.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, you came. Thank you. Thank you for coming up. I know it's a long way. The lift's slow. Sorry." He stood back from the door. "I'm Usami. Please come in. Mind the shoes. Thank you."

Rei had never heard anyone say thank you four times before he got through a door.

Kato came in behind him and stopped dead in the hallway.

Rei felt him go still. He knew the look. A man listening to two radios. Kato had worn it in this same hallway in February, looking at Fujimoto.

But it was a different look this time. His face did something Rei had not seen it do. It went puzzled, and then very quiet.

[break The kitchen]

The kitchen was the same long warm room with the mismatched chairs. The girl who did her homework at the end of the table was there, doing it. Fujimoto was in the armchair in the corner with the television on low, a cooking programme, and he lifted a hand to Rei without looking away from it. Somebody's laundry was drying on a rack by the window.

On the long table there were three trays of small round cakes cooling on wire racks. Each one was iced pale yellow, and on each one somebody had drawn, in chocolate, with a very fine nozzle, the face of a chick. Forty chicks. All slightly different. One of them was winking.

"I'm practising," said Usami. "For Fujimoto-san's birthday. He likes chicks. He says he doesn't." He looked at the trays and went a little pink. "They're too many. I can't stop when I start. Please have one. Please. You'd be helping."

Rei took the winking one. Kato took one with a frown, and looked at it, and did not eat it, and put it carefully in his coat pocket in a paper napkin.

Usami put the kettle on. He had a mug for himself that was white with a little cat painted on the side, sleeping in a teacup. When he turned from the counter with the tea, his eyes came round first, and his head a half second after them. As if two people were looking out of the same face, and one of them was always a little quicker.

Rei sat down at the table. He lifted the latch on his wrist and opened the door in his chest a finger's width.

He saw them.

Two. Not one person with something on top, like Tendo. Not one man and one dead man side by side on a bench, like Fujimoto and Abe. Two people, wound into each other, like two kinds of wool in one jumper. One of them was warm and quick and a little anxious, a young man who said thank you because he meant it every time. The other was quieter, and older, and very much stranger, and it was wrapped all round the first one, close, like a coat round someone in the cold. It was not cold itself. Rei had not known a thing from the far side could feel like that.

It turned, the second one, inside Usami's eyes, and looked at him.

"*He sees us,*" said Usami's mouth. The voice was Usami's voice, but slower, and lower, and very careful with its words. "*Both. He's looking at me.*"

"Yes," said Usami, in his own quick voice, to nobody Rei could see. "I told you he would."

Rei put down his cake.

"Hello," he said. To the second one. "I'm Rei. What's your name?"

The kitchen went quiet. The girl at the end of the table had stopped writing. Fujimoto had turned the television down.

Usami's eyes filled up very suddenly. He put his mug down on the table and held on to it with both hands.

"*Ren,*" said the slow voice. "*My name is Ren.*"

"Hello, Ren."

"Nobody asks him," said Usami. He wiped his eyes with the back of his floury wrist and laughed, a little wet. "Sorry. Sorry. It's been two years. People ask *it*. What's *it* doing. Is *it* behaving. Is *it* awake. The doctors. The officers who take me down for the tests. They're not unkind. They just don't ask." He sniffed. "Thank you. Sorry. Thank you."

Kato was looking at the table. He had his hand in his pocket on the knight.

"I can hear both of you," he said. "Clearly. I've never heard that before."

"We know," said Usami. "We can hear you too. You're very quiet. You keep everything in rows." He smiled, shy. "Ren says it's like a library."

"*Neat,*" said Ren.

Kato's ears went red.

[break What happened]

They told it at the table, with the tea, taking turns, sometimes in the middle of each other's sentences.

Usami had been a student at a university in the west of the city. Food science. He wanted to open a café one day with cakes that looked like animals. He had a part-time job in a patisserie in Shinjuku where they let him do the decorations. When he was twenty-two he had gone to the doctor for a cough that did not go away, and the doctor had sent him for tests, and the tests had found something in his lungs and his blood, and the specialist had said a year. Perhaps less.

"I was so scared," said Usami. He said it plainly, turning the cat mug round. "I wasn't brave about it at all. I cried in the hospital toilet every day for a month. I didn't tell my mum how bad it was. I kept icing cakes because I didn't know what else to do."

A doctor at the hospital had been a quiet woman who did a second job for the Division. She had heard him in his head, crying in the toilet, while she washed her hands at the next sink. Two weeks later, a man in a grey suit had come to his room and told him about the Vessel Program.

"*I was small,*" said Ren. "*Very small. On the other side for a long time. I don't know what I am. Nobody there knew either. I wasn't any of the big ones. I just wanted to see. Your side. What it's like to have mornings.*" A pause. "*The Division had a list of things that had asked to come through. I was on it. They asked me if I would hold a sick body still. I said yes.*"

"There was a contract," said Rei.

"One line," said Usami. "*Ren will keep the body of Usami Hinata alive.*" He touched his own chest, lightly. "And he does. Every second. The cancer's still in there. It always will be. He holds it still so it can't move. If he went, I'd have about an hour."

He said it the way someone tells you their train times.

"*He doesn't like it when I say that,*" said Ren.

"I know you don't." Usami looked down at his mug. Something went across his face that was not quite his and not quite the other one's. "Ren's clairsentient," he said. "Did you know that? Like you. He reads rooms. I read people, and he reads the room they're in. So when I'm in a place, I get both. It's like being in the cinema with someone who whispers what everything means." He smiled. "I didn't know I was a telepath until he came in. I thought I was just good at people."

"*He is good at people,*" said Ren. "*I'm good at walls.*"

Usami laughed, properly this time.

Rei looked at the two of them in one chair, and found he did not have anything wise to say, and ate the rest of his winking chick.

[break The escort]

"The Director wants me on your team," said Usami, at the door, much later, when the trays were half empty and Fujimoto had fallen asleep in front of the television. "I know. He asked me first. He asks first. He's the only one who does." He held the door with one floury hand. "I said I'd like to try. If you'd have me. I'm not brave. I don't fight. I've never been out on anything." He looked at Kato. "And I know you're worried I'll get in the way. I would be too."

Kato looked at him for a long moment.

"I'm not worried you'll get in the way," he said. "I'm worried about the officers who'll stand behind you in the van wondering what's behind your eyes." He took his hand out of his pocket. "I know what that's like. It's tiring. I don't want it for anybody."

Usami's face went very still. Then he did a thing Rei had never seen anyone do to Kato. He stepped forward and hugged him. Quickly, one arm, floury, the strawberry clip bumping Kato's chin.

"Thank you," he said. "Thank you. Sorry. Thank you."

Kato stood in the hallway of the Vessel floor with flour on his good coat and his arms out at his sides like a scarecrow and did not know where to put them.

[break First time out]

The Director signed the paper the next morning. Usami Hinata could go into the field with Shimizu's team. He could not go into a building until Shimizu said so. He would have a Containment officer within thirty metres at all times. He would be back in the Vessel floor by midnight.

The first time, it was a small job. A dry-cleaner's in Sugamo where the steam presses kept switching on by themselves at night. Shimizu put Usami in the van with Moriya, who could not climb stairs anyway, and a tired Containment officer called Nagai who sat by the van door with a seal kit on his knees and did not look at Usami once.

Usami had brought a box of cakes. Rabbits, this time. He gave one to Nagai first.

"Thank you for coming," said Usami. "I'm sorry it's so late. I know you'd rather be at home."

Nagai looked at the cake. Then at him. He took it.

Rei went into the dry-cleaner's with Shimizu and Kato. It was a narrow shop with a counter and racks of plastic-wrapped suits going back into the dark, and it smelled of solvent and hot cloth. Behind the counter, in the back room, the big steam press was hissing on its own. Rei opened the door in his chest and felt something small and sad and fussy in the back room, ironing the same shirt collar over and over.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. Usami, from the van.

*ren says the back wall. behind the boiler. there's an old shrine shelf bricked in. someone covered it when they did the renovation. it's very upset about the dust.*

Then, a second later.

*sorry. thank you.*

They found the shelf behind the boiler at one in the morning. A tiny wooden god-shelf, bricked into the wall when the shop was refitted twelve years before, with a dried-up rice offering still on it and a photograph of a man in a white shirt standing proudly by the very first press. The old owner. He had pressed shirts here for forty years. Shimizu got the current owner on the phone, and the current owner came down in his pyjamas, and stood looking at the photograph for a long time, and said, "That's my uncle. We didn't know where it went."

The press switched off at half past one and did not switch on again.

In the van on the way back, Usami fell asleep against the window with his mouth a little open and the empty cake box on his knees. Nagai, by the door, looked at him for a while. Then he took off his own jacket and put it over him.

Rei saw Kato see it. Kato did not say anything. He looked out of the window at the dark streets going by, with his hand in his pocket, and the paper napkin with the chick cake in it still there from three days before, because he had not been able to decide whether to eat it.
