#title Tendo

On Monday morning Tendo came in with cream buns.

He always did on Mondays. A paper bag from the bakery by Tokyo station, the one with the queue, with eight buns in it, because he never remembered how many people there were on the team and eight seemed safe. He went round the Operations floor with the bag open, the way he did every week, and stopped at every desk, and said something about the weekend. His sister's dog had eaten a sock. A pigeon had got into the station again. He had found a ramen shop in Kanda where the old man shouted at you if you put pepper in before you tasted it.

He stopped at Rei's desk and held out the bag.

"Crow kid," he said. "Last custard one. I saved it. Don't tell Moriya."

Rei took it. It was still warm through the paper. He said thank you. He put it on his desk beside his keyboard and did not eat it, and Tendo did not notice, because he had already gone on to Kato's desk and was telling Kato about the sock.

Kato said, "That's terrible," at the right moment, without looking up from his screen.

He had been very quiet since Nakano. He came in on time and did his work and went home. He ate lunch at his desk. When people spoke to him he answered, politely, in as few words as possible, and he did not make a single joke, and he did not look at Tendo at all. Rei had watched him do it for four days. Kato could hear everyone on the floor. Everyone except one. And for four days he had sat ten metres from that one and listened to a hum where a person should be, and said *that's terrible* about a sock.

At ten o'clock Shimizu put her head out of her office and looked at the two of them, and went back in, and left the door open.

[break Four cards]

Shimizu's office was the smallest room on the floor. It had one window, onto a light well, so that whatever the weather it was always the same grey-white light inside. There was a cork board on the wall covered in maps of Kita with pins in them, and a desk with nothing on it except a mug of green tea gone cold, and two chairs for visitors that did not match. Riki was asleep under the desk with his chin on her shoe.

She shut the door after them and pulled the blind down over the little window in it. Then she sat down and did not say anything for nearly ten minutes.

Rei had learned to wait. The small line between her eyebrows came and went. Her eyes moved over the maps on the cork board, not reading them. Kato stood by the wall with his hand in his pocket on the knight, and Rei knew he could hear her thinking and was being very careful not to.

Then she took four blank cards out of her drawer, the kind they used for evidence labels, and a pen, and wrote on each one. She wrote slowly, in small square letters, with her arm curved round the card so that even the two of them could not see.

"Four operations," she said. "None of them real. Four different places. Four different times." She laid the cards face down on the desk in a row. "Each one goes to one person. Only one. In a sealed envelope, by hand, today. Moriya gets this one. The two officers from the other team get these two."

She pushed the last card a little apart from the others.

"And Tendo gets this."

Kato did not move.

"If someone from that house turns up at one of these places," said Shimizu, "we'll know which card they read." She did not look at the cards. "I haven't told anyone. I wrote them just now. Nobody in this building has heard me think about them, because I didn't think about them until I sat down." She looked at Kato. "You heard me just now. Did you hear what's on them?"

"No," said Kato. "You thought about the maps. And your mother's knee. And whether the tea was still warm." He swallowed. "You didn't think about the cards at all."

"Good." Shimizu picked up the cold tea and drank it anyway. "That's the point."

"And if nobody turns up?" said Rei.

"Then I'm wrong," said Shimizu. "And I've insulted four colleagues and an old friend for nothing. And I'll apologise to every one of them in person and buy them dinner." She put the mug down. "Go back to your desks. Don't look at anybody. Kato. Eat something. You haven't eaten since Thursday."

[break Asagaya]

Thursday was hot and grey and close, the kind of late May day when the rainy season is coming and has not arrived, and the whole city sits under a lid of cloud and sweats.

They went out to Asagaya in the surveillance van at four. The van belonged to Field. It was white, with the name of a plumbing company painted on the side, and inside it had two folding seats and a bench and a box of equipment nobody used and a smell of old coffee that no amount of airing ever got out. Moriya drove, with his braced leg out stiff and his good foot on the pedals, swearing quietly at every gear change. Shimizu sat beside him with a map on her knees. Rei and Kato sat in the back on the bench.

Asagaya is two stops west of Nakano on the Chūō line. It is an old neighbourhood of narrow streets and small wooden houses that somehow survived the war and the bubble and everything after, with a long covered shopping arcade running north from the station where the old men sit outside the tea shop in the afternoons and the cats sleep on the tops of the vending machines. It is the kind of place people move to when they want to write a novel and never do. Moriya parked behind a public bath two streets from the park, in the shade, and turned off the engine, and the van began at once to get hotter.

The place on Tendo's card was a small park with a pond.

Rei had seen it on Shimizu's map. He had not seen it with his eyes. He was not going to. Shimizu had said nobody from the team would go within two hundred metres of any of the four places. So that morning he had stood on the walkway outside the flat in Jūjō at six o'clock, with Gorō on the railing, and said it out loud, the way he had said the river in January. *Asagaya. The park with the broken fountain. The big tree by the pond. Sit there and look.* He had said it three times. Gorō had looked at him with the blue eye and the black eye and then flown off in completely the wrong direction.

At dusk Rei sat on the floor of the van with the black-backed mirror on his knees, and lifted the latch on his wrist a finger's width, and looked.

Grey glass. His own face, faint. Then nothing. Then, slowly, like a photograph coming up in a tray of developer, leaves.

A branch. Green leaves, very close. Through them, a pond with a skin of green on it, and a fountain in the middle that had not worked for years, with a stone fish on top whose mouth was dry. Paths. A bench under a lamp. An old man throwing bread to ducks that were not interested.

Gorō was there.

"He went," said Rei. His voice came out strange. "He actually went."

"Of course he went," said Kato, without opening his eyes. "He's been listening to you talk to him every morning for six months. He's only pretending not to."

At seven minutes past seven, a woman came along the path and sat down on the bench under the lamp.

Rei knew her. Not her face. Her coat. A grey coat, too warm for May, buttoned to the throat, and a cloth bag on her arm, and soft flat shoes. The woman who had walked the children of Kabukichō to a waiting room in Nakano. She sat on the bench with her bag on her knees and looked at the pond.

She sat for twenty minutes. She looked round the park, slowly, carefully. At the bushes. At the paths. At the old man and his bread. At the dry stone fish. Once she turned her head and looked straight up into the tree, straight at the branch, straight at the bird on it, and smiled. A soft kind teacher's smile.

Rei's nose had started to bleed. He did not wipe it.

Then she stood up, and took something out of her cloth bag, and laid it on the bench where she had been sitting, and walked away along the path toward the station.

A white chrysanthemum.

Through the mirror, Rei heard Gorō scream, and the leaves went everywhere, and then there was only grey glass and his own face with blood on it.

Shimizu took the radio off the dashboard. She spoke into it quietly, three times, to three other vans parked near three other parks and one car park in three other wards. Three times, a voice answered that nobody had come.

She put the radio back on the dashboard.

Nobody in the van said anything. Moriya sat with his hands on the wheel, looking at the wall of the bath house. After a while he took out a packet of tissues and passed it back to Rei without turning round.

Kato had opened his eyes. He was looking at the floor of the van. He took the knight out of his pocket and held it in his closed hand and did not turn it.

"He bought me a bun," he said. "On Monday. Red bean. He remembered I don't like custard."

[break The sixth floor]

They did it in Sena's office on Friday morning, because Internal Affairs was the only floor in the building where the doors locked from the inside.

The sixth floor was quiet the way a library is quiet. Thick carpet. Wooden doors. Nobody ever ran on the sixth floor. Sena's office was at the end of the corridor, a narrow room with a window over Marunouchi and grey filing cabinets all along one wall, floor to ceiling, and a desk with nothing on it at all, not a pen, not a piece of paper, until Sena sat down and took a black notebook out of his inside pocket and laid it square to the edge.

Shimizu stood by the window. Moriya sat on a hard chair in the corner with his crutches across his knees and his braced leg out in front of him. Kato stood by the wall. Riki lay across the doorway, so that nobody could leave the room without stepping over him. Rei stood beside Kato.

At nine o'clock exactly, there was a knock, and Tendo came in.

He had a paper bag in his hand.

"Sena-san," he said, cheerfully, ducking his head under the doorframe as he always did although he did not need to. "Shimizu said you wanted a word. Is it about the expenses? I know about the expenses. I can explain the expenses." He stepped over Riki and held out the bag. "I brought these. Sorry. I didn't know who'd be here. I got eight."

Nobody took one.

Tendo looked round the room. At Shimizu by the window. At Moriya with his crutches. At Kato, and Rei. His big friendly face went puzzled, and then a little worried, and he put the bag down on the corner of Sena's empty desk, carefully, and sat down in the chair in front of it.

"Okay," he said. "Not the expenses."

Sena told him.

He told it plainly, in order, in his quiet measured voice, looking at his notebook and not at Tendo. The four cards. Four envelopes, sealed, delivered by hand. The park in Asagaya. The bench. The woman in the grey coat. The white chrysanthemum. Three other places where nobody came. The empty flat in Nakano, a week before, and the note about the young man with all the room in him. Kato on the fourth-floor landing, saying he had not been able to hear Tendo since the middle of April.

Tendo listened to all of it with one ankle on his knee. He did not interrupt. He listened with polite interest, like a man hearing a story about someone he had met once at a party.

When Sena finished, Tendo smiled.

It was not his smile. Rei saw that at once. It was close. It used the same face. But it went up only on one side, and stayed there, and the eyes did not come with it.

"*Four cards,*" said Tendo's mouth. The voice was Tendo's voice, but slower and flatter, and very interested. "*That was clever. I didn't hear that one coming. She wrote them with her arm round them and thought about her mother's knee.*" The eyes went to Shimizu. "*You think very quietly, for a woman with so many ways for things to go wrong.*"

Nobody moved.

"Who are you?" said Sena. Quite calmly. His pen was ready over his notebook.

"*Nobody you'd know.*" The eyes went round the room, one person at a time, and Rei felt each look land like a hand going through a pocket. "*Something from the far side that was invited in. He came to see us, you know. In April. On his own. On a Sunday. He didn't tell any of you. He'd heard about the white house and he wanted to be the one who found it. He wanted to walk into the office on Monday with something nobody else had.*" The half-smile widened. "*And there I was. And I asked him if he'd like to be stronger. Much stronger. Strong enough that nobody on this team would ever have to carry him out of a temple. And he said yes before I'd finished asking.*"

"There's no contract," said Moriya quietly, from the corner. "Is there."

"*No.*" The eyes went to him. "*Why would I sign a contract? Contracts are for things that want to share. I don't want to share. I want the coat.*" It looked down at Tendo's big hands, turning them over, as if admiring a new pair of gloves. "*He's very strong. You were right to want him on your team. Another few months and he'll be very quiet indeed in here. And then he won't be anywhere at all.*"

Kato made a sound. A small sharp sound, like something catching in his throat.

"*And you,*" said the thing, turning Tendo's head toward him. "*The other one who hears. You've been so useful. Every time anything went wrong they looked at you. I didn't have to do a thing. I only had to be friendly.*"

Then something happened to Tendo's face.

It was like watching somebody come up from deep water. The half-smile broke. Both sides of the mouth went loose. The eyes, for one second, were Tendo's eyes, brown and wet and terrified, and they found Kato, and held on.

"I didn't know," said Tendo. In his own voice. It came out cracked and very fast, as if he were shouting it up a well. "I didn't know it could hear. I didn't know it was telling them. I just went to look, I wanted to be the one, Kato, I'm sorry, I bought you red bean, I'm sorry, I'm..."

And the smile came back over his face like a lid closing.

[break Fire]

"*That,*" said the thing pleasantly, "*was rude of him.*"

It snapped Tendo's fingers.

Every file in the room caught fire at once.

Not with a roar. With a soft eager sound, like a gas ring lighting. Every grey cabinet along the wall went up in tall white flame from the inside, all at the same moment, drawer after drawer, from the floor to the ceiling. The heat hit Rei in the face like an oven door opening. The window cracked across. The paper bag on the corner of the desk caught and curled and the smell of burning sugar filled the room.

Riki was already moving. He came off the floor in the doorway and went straight for Tendo, low and fast and silent, as he had been trained to. The thing turned Tendo's head and looked at him, and the dog stopped in the air in the middle of his leap, and hung there, legs out, and then was thrown sideways across the room into the burning cabinets. He yelped once. Then he was on the floor among the flames, trying to get up, and his back leg would not hold him.

Rei went for the dog. He did not decide to. He was on his knees in the heat with his arms round Riki's chest, dragging him back toward the door, and the dog's fur was smoking and he was not making a sound.

Shimizu threw up both her hands. Rei felt it go across the room behind him, the whole of her strength, a push like a wall of water. The thing in Tendo's body slid backward, chair and all, and the chair went over, and Tendo's head hit the floor. It laughed, lying there. It began to get up.

It did not get up.

Moriya had dropped his crutches. He had come across the room on his one good leg, hopping, falling the last metre, and landed on his knees beside Tendo with his braced leg twisted out to one side, and he had his hand flat on the side of Tendo's neck, where the big vein is. He did not hit him. He did not hold him down. He simply kept his hand there, and closed his eyes, and his face went grey.

Rei felt it through the latch on his wrist. Tendo's heart, slowing. Not stopping. Slowing. A beat. A long pause. A beat. A longer pause. Like a clock running down.

"*What are you*..." said the thing, and its voice was slurring.

"Keeping him alive," said Moriya, through his teeth. "Very slowly. Too slowly for you to drive." He did not open his eyes. "Shimizu. Containment. Now."

The thing tried to burn him. Rei saw it try. A small white flame stood up on the cuff of Moriya's jacket, and flickered, and went out, as if the hand under it were too slow and too cold to be worth burning.

Tendo's eyes closed.

Sena had not moved from his desk. He sat in the middle of the burning room with his black notebook in his hand, and he was writing, very small and very fast, and his hand was perfectly steady. When the fire alarm began to scream, he finished his sentence, and put the notebook back in his inside pocket, and stood up, and went to help Rei with the dog.

[break Downstairs]

They took Tendo down to Containment on a stretcher, with four seals across his chest in Uruma's handwriting, and Moriya hopping beside it on one crutch with his other hand still flat on Tendo's neck, all the way down in the lift. Six floors. Nobody spoke. Somewhere above them the fire alarm was still going and people were running along corridors with extinguishers.

Tendo Akira went into a sealed room two doors along from the one where Itsuki had spent ninety days. The nurses in Containment had known him for six years. He used to bring them cream buns too. One of them, a short woman with grey hair called Mrs. Ogura, stood at the window of his door with her hands over her mouth for a long time after they had closed it.

Rei took Riki to the animal hospital in Kanda in a taxi, with the dog across his knees wrapped in Sena's suit jacket. The driver did not ask any questions. He drove very carefully and did not charge for the waiting. The vet said the back leg was broken in two places, and the burns were not deep, and he was a very lucky dog, and Rei sat in the waiting room for three hours on a plastic chair under a poster about worms until they came out and said Riki would keep the leg.

When he got back to Marunouchi it was evening. The sixth floor smelled of smoke and wet paper. There was a man from the building's management standing in the doorway of Sena's office with a clipboard, looking at the black walls.

Kato was not at his desk. Rei found him in the stairwell between the fourth and fifth floors, sitting on a step with his back against the wall and his knees up.

He had the paper bag on his knees. It was scorched along one side. He must have gone back for it.

Rei sat down on the step beside him.

"There's one left," said Kato. He opened the bag. Most of the buns inside were black. One, at the bottom, was only brown on top. "Red bean." He looked at it. "I could have heard it. If I'd listened properly. To the hum. Instead of being glad I couldn't hear him."

"You told me about the hum," said Rei. "On the landing in Nakano. You told me first."

"After a month."

"After a month."

Kato took the bun out of the bag and broke it in half and held one half out to Rei without looking at him. Rei took it. They ate them sitting on the stairs, slowly, in silence. The red bean paste was still sweet under the burnt crust.

When he had finished, Kato folded the empty bag into a small square and put it in his inside pocket, next to the knight.

[break The paper]

Three weeks later, the thing in Tendo had been starved and sealed and argued with until it was small enough to hold. The Director signed a paper. Tendo went down to the level under the sub-level, where there are no windows in the doors at all, and where the only visitors are the ones who write ahead and wait a month for an answer.

Rei read the paper over Shimizu's shoulder, in her office, with the grey light coming in off the light well.

*He went to them alone. He allowed a hostile entity into his body and into this building. There is no evidence that he intended harm. There is no reliable way to know how much of the entity remains once it is sealed.*

"It isn't fair," said Rei.

"No," said Shimizu. "It isn't." She folded the paper along its creases and put it in her drawer and shut the drawer. Then she sat looking at the shut drawer for a while. "I've known him six years. He carried me down four flights of stairs in Kōtō once, with a broken ankle, singing." She took out a pen. "I'm going to write to the sub-level. To ask to visit. They'll say no the first time. I'll write again."

"Can I come?"

"Ask me in a month," said Shimizu. "When they've said no twice."

[break The box]

Riki came home from the animal hospital at the start of the second week of June with his back leg in a cast and a shaved patch on his side where the burns had been.

He did not go back to the dog room on the ground floor. The Director went down to the car park himself to collect him, in his grey suit, and Rei, who happened to be coming in from the station, saw him kneel on the concrete beside the taxi in front of a red dog in a cast. He saw Adachi take the dog's face very gently in both hands, and heard him say, in a voice nobody in the building had ever heard him use, "I am sorry. That should not have happened to you. It was my building." Then the Director picked the dog up, cast and all, and carried him to the lift, and the security guard at the barrier looked very hard at his own shoes.

After that Riki lived on a cushion beside the desk on the fourth floor.

On the second Monday of June, Adachi sent for Rei. When Rei went in, the Director was sitting on the floor beside the cushion, brushing the dog, very slowly, from the ears to the tail. He did not get up.

"Just a moment," he said. "I am finishing here."

Rei waited. Riki lay with his eyes half shut and his cast stuck out sideways, making a small low sound of contentment every time the brush went down his back. The Director brushed him until he was finished, and put the brush in a drawer, and got up, slowly, with a hand on the edge of the desk, and sat down behind it and was the Director again.

"Tendo told you he was sorry," said Adachi. "Kato put it in his report. In Tendo's own words. In the one second he had."

"Yes."

"You believe it."

"Yes."

Adachi nodded slowly. He opened a drawer of his desk and took something out and put it on the desk in front of Rei.

It was a small black plastic box, about the size of a pack of cards, with rounded corners and a clip on the back. It looked like an old pager, the kind doctors carried before phones. On the front there was a narrow green screen, dark and empty. Above it, a row of five small round lights. Below it, one larger light. Three small rubber buttons. And a fourth, under a little plastic flap that had been taped shut.

"A Spirit Capturer," said Adachi. "Every field officer carries one, after a time. After we trust them." He looked at it. "It holds one small thing at a time. A spirit. A piece of one. You can carry it with you. You can talk to it, through the screen. The light tells you what kind of thing it is." He touched the taped flap with one finger. "That button you will not use until you know the name of what you are holding. Never before."

Rei looked at it. He did not pick it up.

"Why now?"

"Because nobody in this building checked Kato," said Adachi. "Except you. And you did not check him. You simply did not doubt him." He sat back. "That is either a very good quality in an officer, or a very dangerous one. I have decided that it is a good one. For now." He looked at Rei with his pale resting eyes. "It is made for small things. There is a limit to what it can hold. Do not point it at anything large and expect it to help you."

Rei picked it up. It was lighter than it looked, and cold. On his wrist the latch went cold too, very faintly, and then was still.

"Your brother carried one for six years," said the Director. "He told me once that it taught him most of what comes through the veil only wants somebody to ask its name." He looked down at the dog on the cushion. "Tell Shimizu her team is short one. I am working on it."

Rei clipped the box to his belt, under his jacket, where Gin used to wear his. Riki opened one eye and watched him go to the door, and closed it again.
