#title Tsuru-no-yu

On the first Saturday of October, Tanizaki put down his newspaper after an hour and said, "Go and have a bath."

Rei waited for the rest of the sentence. There was no rest of the sentence. Tanizaki had already found his place in the obituaries again and was reading them with the quiet satisfaction of a man who keeps finding people younger than himself in there.

"A bath," he said.

"Tsuru-no-yu. In the lane behind the market, under the tall chimney. Ask for Setsu." Tanizaki picked up his newspaper again. "Tell her I sent you. Tell her the fig is doing well. She will not believe you."

[break The bath house]

A sentō is a public bath house. In old Tokyo, before every flat had its own bathroom, every neighbourhood had one, sometimes three or four. People went every evening with a small towel and a plastic basin and a bar of soap, and washed, and soaked in a big hot pool with their neighbours, and talked. Most of them have closed now. A few are left, mostly in old neighbourhoods like Kita, kept open by old people for old people and for a few young ones who like the heat.

Tsuru-no-yu was easy to find. Its chimney was the tallest thing in that part of Kita, a thin brick tower that you could see from the station, with the name of the bath painted down its side in white letters. The building under it had a curved roof like a temple and a dark blue cloth curtain hanging over the door with the character for hot water printed on it in white. 湯. Beside the door a sign gave the price. Four hundred and sixty yen. A bicycle leaned against the wall with a towel drying on its handlebars. On the step by the curtain sat a small white plate heaped with salt, fresh, as if somebody had filled it that morning before doing anything else.

Inside, there was a raised wooden booth between the men's side and the women's side, and in it sat an old woman.

She was tiny, a hundred and fifty centimetres at most, and she sat with her back as straight as a ruler. Her white hair was pinned up so neatly that not one strand had escaped. She wore an indigo apron over a grey sweater. She was knitting something long and red. She did not stop knitting when Rei came in. She looked at him over the top of the needles for one second, and he felt the same thing he had felt in Café Asahi. Being read. All the way through, like the front page of a newspaper.

"Tanizaki sent you," she said.

"Yes. He said to tell you the fig is doing well."

She snorted. "The fig is dying. It's been dying for thirty years. He's too stubborn to cut it down." She held out her hand without looking. "Four hundred and sixty yen. Towel's another hundred. You don't have a towel, I can see that from here."

He paid. She gave him a small thin white towel and a tiny bar of soap wrapped in paper.

"Wash first," she said. "Properly. Then the big bath. Then come to the back door when you're done. I'll be in the kitchen." She went back to her knitting. "Your brother always took too long in the bath. Don't take too long."

[break The water]

The men's side of the bath house was one big tiled room with a high ceiling and windows near the roof. Along the walls were rows of low taps and little plastic stools, where you sat and scrubbed yourself clean before getting in the bath. At the far end was the pool itself, big and square and steaming, and behind it, covering the whole wall, was a painting of Mount Fuji. Every old sentō has one. A huge blue and white Fuji with a blue sea in front of it and little white sails on the sea. This one was faded and peeling at the edges, but Rei thought it was beautiful.

There were three old men in the bath. They nodded at him. He nodded back. He washed, carefully, sitting on a plastic stool, the way Gin had taught him when he was very small. Then he got in.

The water was so hot that it hurt. Forty-four degrees, the sign said. It went up his legs like fire. He sank in to his chin very slowly, holding his breath, and then let it out, and then everything in him let go at once. His shoulders. His back. His jaw, which he had not known he was clenching. He had not felt warm, properly warm, since the morning at Kamiya.

He closed his eyes.

And he felt the water.

Not the heat. Something under it. The water in that pool had been there, in one form or another, for seventy years. Every evening for seventy years, the people of this neighbourhood had come and sat in it and let go. Their tiredness. Their worries. Their small griefs. A wife who had died. A son who did not call. A job lost. A baby born. All of it had soaked into the water, night after night, and been washed away, and come back. The water knew this neighbourhood the way a grandmother knows her family. It was warm, and it was heavy, and it was kind.

And in one corner, there was somebody.

He opened his eyes. The three old men were at the other end of the pool. But in the corner by the painted sea, where nobody was sitting, the water was hotter than anywhere else, and it was occupied. He could feel it, as surely as you feel someone sit down next to you on a bench with your eyes shut. An old man. Patient. Pleased with the heat. Keeping it.

And under the heat, very faint, a cold thread. As if, not long ago, something had frightened him, and he had not quite got over it.

He got out of the bath much sooner than he wanted to. One of the old men watched him go and said to the man next to him, not quietly, "Young people. No stamina. In my day we sat in there till we saw God," and the other one said, "You saw God in nineteen eighty and you've been unbearable since," and they both laughed until one of them had to cough.

[break Hara Setsu]

The kitchen behind the bath house was small and warm and smelled of soy sauce and steam. Hara Setsu sat at a low table with her knitting in her lap and a pot of tea in front of her. There were two cups. Rei sat down opposite her without being asked, and she poured him one, and did not say anything for a while.

"You felt the corner," she said at last.

"By the painting. By the sea. There's someone sitting there."

"Mr. Kishi." She nodded slowly, as if he had passed a small test. "He came every night for thirty years and sat in that corner. In 2009 he died in it, of his heart, with his towel on his head, very happy. He didn't leave." She drank her tea. "He keeps the back of the bath warm for me. I can turn the boiler off in January and that corner stays hot. He keeps the gate quiet too. He's a good customer. He doesn't pay, but he never did."

Rei did not know what to say to that, so he drank his tea.

"Some nights the corner goes cold," said Hara Setsu. "Not often. When it does, it's because he's hiding. Something has gone past that he doesn't like. I've kept a note of it." She reached behind her without looking and took down a notebook from a shelf, an old one, with a cover of faded green cloth, and pushed it across the table. "There. Read it."

It was a list of years, in pencil, in a small upright hand. Beside each year, a few words. The first pages were older than Mr. Kishi, notes about the water and the boiler and the customers, going back forty years to the year she took the bath over. Then, *2009. Mr. Kishi, June. Corner warm ever since.* After that the entries were all about the corner. *2010. Cold two nights in November.* *2011. Cold three nights in March. The earthquake.* *2013. Cold one night in August.* *2014. Cold four nights. August.* The last line, this year, was underlined twice. *2015. August. Ice.*

"Ice?" said Rei.

"In August." Hara Setsu picked up her knitting. "On the twelfth of August, at ten at night, I came in to check the boiler. The water by the painting had a skin of ice on it. Thin. Like the ice on a puddle in January. In a bath at forty-four degrees." Her needles clicked. "It was gone by morning. Forty years in this bath, and I've never seen that. Never." She looked at the wall, toward the corner. "He was gone for three days after. Hiding so deep I thought he'd left for good."

The twelfth of August. The night Gin did not come home.

Rei put the notebook down very carefully.

"August," he said. "It's nearly always August."

"Obon. When the dead come home, other things come along with them." She did not look up from her knitting. "Tanizaki knows. Your brother knew. He used to sit right there, where you're sitting, and read that notebook, and copy things out of it into that black book of his."

"He came here?"

"Since he was nine. Tanizaki brought him. A skinny boy who took too long in the bath and asked too many questions." Something softened at the edges of her square face. "He came on the twelfth of August too. That afternoon. I didn't tell you that yet."

Rei's heart went cold and hard in his chest.

"He came at four in the afternoon. He didn't bathe. He sat right there and drank one cup of tea, and he asked me one question." She stopped knitting. "He asked me if I'd ever felt the corner go cold in the same week as the trains at the old freight yard."

"What freight yard?"

"The old one. By the river, past Tabata. Where the goods trains used to stop before they built the new lines. Most of it's empty now. Weeds and old tracks and a few rusted cars." She looked at him with her flat, steady eyes. "I told him yes. Every time. Every time the corner goes cold, the old men who still walk their dogs by the freight yard come in here and tell me they saw lights there at night. White lights, they say. Moving. Like torches. But nobody carrying them."

"And what did he say?"

"He said thank you, Setsu. He said, thank you, I think I understand now." She put down her knitting entirely. "And then he finished his tea, and he looked at me, and he smiled. A good smile. The one he had when he was nine and had worked something out. And he said, *Will you keep an eye on Rei for me.*" She looked at the cup in her hands. "I said, of course. I said, why, are you going somewhere? And he said, *Not far. Just to look.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere through the wall, in the bath, an old man was singing a folk song, badly, his voice echoing off the tiles.

"So I'm keeping an eye on you," said Hara Setsu. "Every Saturday. After Tanizaki. You come here, and you have a bath, and you sit in my kitchen and you drink tea, and you tell me how you are. Properly. Not *fine*. Your brother always said *fine*." She picked up her knitting again. "That's my price. For the notebook. And for anything else I know."

"Anything else?"

"I'm seventy-one years old, child, and I've lived in Kita for all of them. I know where everything is buried." Click, click. "Some of it I buried myself."

[break The red scarf]

When he left, at the back door, she held out the long red thing she had been knitting. It was a scarf. Long and thick and soft, a deep dark red.

"It's going to be cold this winter," she said. "Colder than you think."

"I can't take this."

"It's not for you. I started it in August for your brother." She pushed it into his hands. "He can have it when you bring him home. Till then, you keep it warm for him."

Rei walked home through the dark streets with the red scarf round his neck and his skin still glowing from the bath and Hara Setsu's notebook in his head. *Corner cold. Freight yard lights.* *2015. August. Ice.* He looked up at the sky above Kita. A few faint stars. The glow of the city on the clouds.

Somewhere past Tabata, by the river, there was an old freight yard with white lights in it that nobody carried.

He stopped under a streetlight and lit a cigarette, his first since Kamiya, and stood there smoking it with the red scarf round his neck like a man waiting for a bus that had been cancelled years ago. He did not go there that night. He wanted to. He wanted to so much that his legs kept turning toward the station on their own. But he had sat in a garden for an hour every Saturday for six weeks, learning not to be in a hurry.

So he went home, and wrote *freight yard* in Gin's book in pencil, small, in the margin, under Gin's handwriting, and underlined it twice, the way Hara Setsu did.

Then he went to sleep with the red scarf folded under his pillow, and it smelled faintly of the bath, and of soap, and of a kitchen he had sat in for an hour and already felt he had known all his life.
