#title The Reserve

The bank rang on the Monday morning, at ten past nine, while Rei was standing on the platform at Jūjō waiting for the train to work.

It was a landline number with a Kita prefix, and he nearly let it ring. When he answered there was a pause, and the sound of a man clearing his throat in a small room, and a careful middle-aged voice that apologised twice before it said anything else.

"Uedera Rei-san? My name is Imai. Imai Tetsuya. I'm the manager at the Ōji branch." Another pause. "You came in last August. To the counter. About your brother's account. I was in the back office. I saw you. I wasn't allowed to come out." A breath. "I'm sorry to ring you on the way to work. Could you possibly come in? Today, if you can. It isn't something I want to say on the telephone."

Rei rang Shimizu from the platform and said he would be an hour late, and she said *fine* in the voice that meant she would want to know why, and he got on the train going the other way.

[break The branch]

The Ōji branch was the same as it had been the summer before. A low grey building by the station with the bank's green sign over the door and a row of cash machines in a glass box at the side, where an old man in a cap was doing something very slowly while a queue built up behind him. Inside, the air conditioning was turned up so high that Rei's damp shirt went cold on his back. The same rows of plastic chairs. The same ticket machine that gave you a number. The same young women behind the long counter in neat navy uniforms with little scarves at the neck, bowing to each customer as they sat down and bowing again as they got up.

There was a poster on the wall about fraud. *Is that really your grandson on the phone?* There was a fish tank with one large orange fish in it that did not move.

Imai Tetsuya came out of a door at the back before Rei had time to take a number.

He was in his fifties, short and neat and a little round, in a white short-sleeved shirt with a blue tie and a name badge clipped to the pocket. His hair was combed carefully across. He had the face of a man who had spent thirty years saying *I'm afraid that won't be possible* to people very kindly, and minding every time. He bowed to Rei, and Rei bowed back, and Imai said *please, this way* and held the door.

The back office was small and cold and full of grey filing cabinets. There was a desk and two chairs and a calendar on the wall from the bank, the same one the bank sent out every December, with a photograph of Mount Fuji for July. A young woman brought two paper cups of green tea on a tray without being asked and went out again.

Imai sat down. He put his hands flat on the desk. Then he took a phone out of his shirt pocket, an ordinary work phone in a black case, and found something on it, and turned it round on the desk so that Rei could read the screen.

"This came at eight minutes to midnight on Saturday," he said. "From your brother's phone. I've read it perhaps forty times."

*Imai-san. Please release the reserve to Rei. All of it. He'll need it now. I'm sorry I never came in to say thank you for the calendars.*

Rei read it. He did not trust himself to pick up the tea.

"Your brother has a second account with us," said Imai. "A savings account. He opened it six years ago, in his first month at the Division, with his first proper salary. He has put something into it on the twenty-sixth of every month since, the day after he was paid, and he has never once taken anything out. Not once. I know, because I set it up for him, and I look at it sometimes. I'm not supposed to." He looked at the cup of tea. "He told me when he opened it that it was for you. If anything happened to him. He was very young. I remember thinking it was a strange thing for a young man to say."

"He was twenty-two," said Rei.

"Yes." Imai nodded slowly. "He set it up in a particular way. If a message ever came from him asking us to release it, I was to ring first. Before I did anything. Always ring first. Not his number. He was very firm. He said, *never trust a message from my phone without ringing, because if something's happened to me, it might not be me holding the phone.*" Imai's mouth tightened a little. "I thought that was a strange thing to say, too. At the time."

He was quiet.

"I rang his number on Saturday night anyway," he said. "I know I shouldn't have. It rang out. On Sunday I rang it again. Nothing. So I'm ringing you, because you're the second name on the file, and I have to ask." He looked up. "Do you confirm the instruction? Is it really from him?"

Rei looked at the screen. At the small black words. At *the calendars*, which nobody else in the world would have known to write.

"We send everybody a calendar," said Imai, apologetically. "Every December. From the bank. I didn't know he kept them."

"He hangs them by the door," said Rei. His voice did not work very well. "Every year. He writes my exam dates on them."

He thought of the hand that had typed it. Gin's hand. On a Saturday night, at eight minutes to midnight, eleven months after the walkway, with something else living behind his eyes. Somewhere in the city. One minute. Long enough for one thing.

He had used it for this.

"Yes," said Rei. "It's from him. I confirm it."

[break The passbook]

In Japan most people still keep a bankbook. A *tsūchō*. A small thin booklet with a cardboard cover in the bank's colours, and inside, pages of printed lines, one for every time money goes in or out. You feed it into a slot in the cash machine and the machine pulls it in and prints the new lines on the next empty page with a clatter, and gives it back to you, warm. Young people have apps now. But the old ladies still queue at the machines on pension day with their passbooks in their hands, and so do a surprising number of the young, because there is something about seeing it written down on paper.

Gin's was dark green. Imai gave it to Rei across the desk in a plastic sleeve, with a new card and a form to sign, and Rei opened it on his knee.

Six years of lines.

The same small figure on the twenty-sixth of every month, in a column, page after page. Sometimes a little more. In the December of Gin's third year, a lot more, which Rei worked out after a moment must have been his bonus. In the April of the fifth year, a little less, which was the month Rei had needed new shoes for the entrance exams and Gin had said the shoes were nothing, he had found the money in a coat pocket. Then the same figure again. Every month. Including, Rei saw, with something going over in his chest, the last twelve. The Division had not stopped Gin's salary. It had gone on coming into his ordinary account every month while he was missing, and on the twenty-sixth of every month an automatic transfer that Gin had set up six years ago had gone on moving the same small amount across into this one, without anybody touching it.

At the bottom of the last printed page, a total.

Two million, four hundred and eight thousand yen.

Rei sat in the cold little office with the passbook on his knee and looked at the number.

"I'll do the transfer now," said Imai gently. "It'll be in your own account by the afternoon. You can keep this book. It's his. I thought you'd want it."

[break The bench]

There was a bench outside the bank, under a plane tree by the station, where people sat to wait for buses. Rei sat on it for a long time with the passbook in his hands, and did not go to work.

It was a great deal of money. Not a fortune. It would not buy a flat. But it was more than he had ever had at once in his life, and he knew exactly what it was for, because Gin had said so. *He'll need it now.*

Not *he'll need it for the rent.* Not *he'll need it if the agency writes.* Gin would have thought of the rent. Gin always thought of the rent. He kept a list.

*Now.*

Rei thought about what Gin would have done. Gin, a year ago, going down to look at something on his own on a Wednesday night because it was late and he did not want to bother anybody. Gin keeping a list of promises in the back of his book so as not to forget one. Gin who had stood in the doorway of the flat at three in the morning saying *no* to whatever was at the window, every night, alone, for years.

Gin, in the end, alone on a walkway.

Rei sat on the bench and understood that the one thing Gin had never had, in all those years, was enough people. Not friends. He had those. People who would come when he called and stand in a doorway with him and not leave. People who were paid, and ready, and knew what they were standing against. Gin had done the job of ten people on a junior officer's wage and his own nerve, and it had not been enough.

Rei had a woman in Asagaya with a teapot and a white house who was getting ready for something. He did not know what. He did not know when. He had a month, perhaps. Perhaps less.

He put the passbook in his inside pocket and got up and went to work.

[break Salt]

He started with salt.

There is a street in Taitō ward called Kappabashi where the restaurants of Tokyo buy everything that is not food. A long straight avenue lined on both sides with shops selling knives and pans and paper lanterns and lacquer trays and the plastic models of dishes that sit in restaurant windows, so real you want to eat them. At the end of it, behind the knife shops, there were wholesalers who sold salt to noodle shops and pickle makers by the twenty-kilo sack.

Rei bought eleven sacks. The man in the warehouse, a cheerful old man in a towel headband, asked him what kind of restaurant it was. Rei said it was a bath house. Several bath houses. The man looked at him, and at the sacks, and at the bag over Rei's shoulder with the Division owl on it, and stopped asking, and gave him a discount for cash, and had it delivered that evening to the back door of a bath house in Kita with a chimney like a factory.

Hara Setsu came out of her kitchen in her apron and looked at the eleven sacks on her step for a long time.

"What's this for?"

"For when it starts," said Rei. "Whatever it is. You know every bath house in Kita. Every old man in every bath house. If one night something walks down the street that shouldn't, I want every one of them to have a box of salt by the door and know what to do with it."

Hara Setsu looked at him over the top of her glasses. She was knitting something grey and long, and she did not stop.

"Your brother asked me to do that once," she said. "Three years ago. He couldn't afford the salt." She put her needles in her apron pocket. "Help me carry them in."

[break Paper]

He went to Kanda next, on a Saturday.

There was a paper shop near the old temple in Kanda that Gin had used for years, for the washi he drew his seals on. Rei had found it in the back of the book. *Kanda. The paper shop by the temple steps. The owner thinks I'm a serious amateur calligrapher. Don't correct him.* It was a narrow old shop, two hundred years old according to the board outside, with the sheets laid flat in long wooden drawers that went up to the ceiling, and a smell of dust and mulberry bark, and an old man at the back grinding ink.

Rei asked for the paper Gin bought. The old man went very still, and then came round the counter and looked at Rei's face.

"You're his brother," he said. "The calligrapher. Uedera-san. He hasn't been in for a year. I wondered if he'd given it up." He pulled out a drawer and took out a sheet and held it up to the light from the door, so Rei could see the long fibres in it, like hair. "This one. He always had this one. He said it holds the ink right. I've kept some back for him."

Rei bought all of it. And ink. And two brushes the old man chose for him, because Rei did not know how. And when he took the paper up to the Sigil Room on the Monday, Uruma unrolled the first sheet on the long table and ran one long finger down it and did not say anything for a long time.

"This is what he used," said Uruma. "I've been making your seals on Division stock. It's like writing on a bus ticket." He looked up. "How much of this have you got?"

"Enough for everyone," said Rei. "Everyone who's going to be there."

[break Silver]

He bought silver from an antique dealer in Shimokitazawa who did not ask questions, sterling, ninety-two and a half per cent, old spoons and a serving dish with somebody's initials on it, and gave it to Uruma for the wards that would need it. He bought iron nails, the old square hand-made kind, from a temple carpenter in Yanaka. He bought a proper medical kit for Moriya, who had been using a box from a chemist's held together with tape, and Moriya looked at it for a long time and then said *thank you* in a voice Rei had not heard him use, and went away down the corridor with it under his arm.

He bought clothes.

That was Hana's fault. She had told him in the alley in June, handing him a clean white shirt that smelled of somebody else's washing powder, *put that on, they like you in white.* So he went to the department store in Ikebukuro, to the floor for men, where the shirts are folded on tables in colours and sizes and a quiet man with a tape measure comes and stands beside you before you have decided whether you want him to. Rei bought three white shirts that fitted. And a dark suit. His own. Not Gin's second suit with the arms too long. Not Haruki's. The quiet man took it in at the waist and turned up the trousers and had it ready in four days.

Shimizu looked at him on the Monday morning when he came onto the sixth floor in it.

She looked at the suit. She looked at the shirt. She looked at the tie, for a long time.

"Better," she said, and went back to her report.

[break The Grid]

Then he went to the Grid.

The message point that week was a coin laundry in Ikebukuro, under a railway arch, with eight washing machines and a bench and a sign on the wall in three languages about not leaving your washing overnight. Rei went at two in the morning with an envelope in his inside pocket. Switch was sitting on the bench in black with their square face set and their signet ring turned inward, reading a manga with a cover of a girl with a sword.

They did not look up.

"I need people," said Rei. "To watch the white house in Asagaya. Day and night. Every Sunday when the Circle meets, and every night, and anywhere she goes. I need to know who comes and who goes and what they carry in. And the river. The freight lines on the river past Tabata. Somebody on that every night till Obon." He took the envelope out. "I can pay. Properly. The Grid rate. For a month."

Switch turned a page of the manga.

"The Grid doesn't work for the Division," they said.

"I'm not asking for the Division. The Division doesn't know about this money. It's my brother's." Rei put the envelope down on the bench beside them. "He saved it for six years. He sent it to me on Saturday from inside his own head with something else holding the phone. He said I'd need it now." His voice was not quite steady. "He did all of this on his own for years. He never had enough people. I'm not going to make that mistake."

Switch closed the manga.

They looked at the envelope. They did not open it. They looked at Rei for a long time, the way they had looked at him in the doorway of the launderette in Akabane in the rain.

"Your brother came to the Gathering three years ago," they said. "He asked the Grid to watch a building in Kita for a month. He offered to pay. He had thirty thousand yen. Everybody laughed." They picked up the envelope and put it inside their jacket without counting it. "Not unkindly. We did it anyway. Some of it." They stood up. "I'll have six people on Asagaya by tomorrow night. Four on the river. They'll report to me. I'll report to you. Not to the Division."

"Not to the Division," said Rei.

"And when it starts," said Switch, "whatever it is. If it's on the river. The Grid will come." They turned their ring round on their finger, once, outward, and back. "Not because of the money. The money pays for the watching. It doesn't pay for that." They went to the door of the laundry. "We'll come because you asked properly. Nobody from the Division has ever asked properly."

[break Hana]

The last of it went on Hana.

She turned up at the flat in Jūjō at one in the morning at the end of July, on the walkway, with no warning, because she never warned anybody of anything. She had a cut along the back of her left hand from the knuckles to the wrist, deep, wrapped in a convenience store towel that had gone dark red, and she was furious about it.

"It's nothing," she said. "A door. A stupid door in Nippori with a nail sticking out of it. I need you to hold it closed while I tape it."

"It needs stitches."

"The Grid doesn't do stitches. The Grid does tape."

He took her to the all-night clinic by Ōji station anyway, in a taxi, with Hana swearing at him the whole way in two languages. The doctor was young and tired and asked for her insurance card, and Hana did not have one, because nobody in the Grid was on any register for anything if they could help it, and the doctor named a price for treating somebody without insurance that made Hana stand up and head for the door.

Rei paid it.

Hana sat on the paper sheet on the examination couch while the doctor put in nine stitches, and stared at the ceiling, and did not say anything at all. When they came out into the street at three in the morning she walked ahead of him with her bandaged hand in her pocket and her hood up.

At the corner by the station she stopped.

"I'll pay you back," she said, without turning round.

"No you won't."

"No," said Hana. "I won't." She was quiet for a moment. "I'll jump you somewhere. Some time. When you need it. Anywhere you like. That's what I'll do."

Then she was gone, as she always went, all at once, and there was only the corner and the station clock and the smell of hot concrete.

[break The drawer]

On the thirtieth of July Rei fed the dark green passbook into the slot of the cash machine at the konbini on the corner, and waited while it clattered, and took it back warm, and looked at the new line printed at the bottom of the page.

Thirty-one thousand yen.

He stood by the magazine rack with it for a while. He went through it in his head, sack by sack and sheet by sheet. The salt in the bath houses. The paper on Uruma's table. Moriya's kit. Six people on the white house and four on the river. Nine stitches. The suit. He would have done all of it again.

At home he put the passbook in the drawer of Gin's desk, on top of the bank's calendars, which Gin had kept, every one, with Rei's exam dates still circled in red.
