#title Kishin

She came up the grey beach at a walk.

A small neat woman in a pale grey cardigan buttoned to the throat, with her hair in a low bun and her feet bare on the grey sand, and a little river stone on a cord swinging at her neck. Behind her the low white table, and the cushions, and the teapot still steaming. In front of her, at the edge of the weeds, a tall man in a grey jacket with the sleeves pushed up, standing very straight, with two people in him.

She stopped three metres away.

*Well,* she said. Not out loud. In the air. In the weeds. In the empty frame where Rei's door had been. *Well. Look at you. The mender. Come all this way through the veil to find me, and put on a policeman, and learn to buy rice.* She tilted Rinka's head. *I've watched you all year. I couldn't believe it. You healed a child's arm on a bridge tonight. A child. Nobody asked you to. What were you going to do, mend the whole city?*

The body did not answer.

*You were the strongest of us,* she said. *On the other side. You could have mended anything. You could have mended the skin of the world if you'd wanted. You never wanted. You only ever wanted me gone.* She smiled. *And now you've gone soft in a borrowed coat, and you're going to lose to me on a beach, and the man inside you is going to watch.*

[break The fight]

She did not come at them herself. Not at first.

She lifted one hand, and out of the weeds round the edge of the beach, out of the shadows of the freight cars, out of the dark along the river path where the salt had not reached, the last of them came. The hollow ones she had kept back. Twenty, perhaps. Thirty. The strongest. The ones who had been drinking her tea for years, who had almost nothing left in them but the soft far voice and whatever small hungry thing she had let in to ride them. They came across the sand toward the tall man in the grey jacket from every side at once, without a sound, with their hands out.

Rei had seen his brother work before. Not often. Gin did not like being watched. Twice, maybe three times, when Rei was small, from the back seat of a car or the far end of a platform, he had seen Gin do the job. He had seen him stand very still in a doorway with his eyes half shut, reading a room. He had seen him talk a frightened old man down off a bridge railing in the rain. He had never seen him fight.

The tall man was not where he had been.

He was beside the first of them, a man in a tracksuit, with two fingers on the side of the man's neck. Then he was beside the next, a woman in an apron, three metres away, with his palm flat on her forehead. Then the next. Then the next. He did not walk. He did not run. He was simply somewhere else each time Rei looked, a little to the left, a little behind. Gin used to step from one end of a corridor to the other in the flat in Jūjō to make Rei laugh when he was small. This was faster, and it did not stop.

And everyone he touched sat down.

Rei could feel what he was doing through the empty frame in his chest. Gin's sight going ahead of the hands, finding where the small hungry thing sat in each of them, under the heart or behind the eyes or curled at the base of the skull like a cat in a basket. Then the mender's hand, laid on the place. A little heat. Not fire. Just enough, as you warm your hands round a cup, and the thing inside could not bear it and let go, and went up off them like steam. And then, in the same breath, the mender's other gift, the one he had been made of. The heart that had been racing, asked to slow. The breath that had gone shallow, asked to deepen. The body, which almost always wants to live, mended a little, there and then, and set down gently on the sand.

One of them got past him.

A big man in a security guard's shirt, near the back, who had not come at the tall man at all. He had turned in the weeds and gone the other way, up the yard, toward the gate, fast and low, toward the smallest and softest thing he could find. Rei felt where he was going before he got there. A girl of eight in a police jacket three sizes too big, standing beside Ono at the gate with her hand in his.

The tall man was on the far side of the ring, with his palm on a woman's forehead. He could not be in two places. Rei saw him begin to turn.

Riki got there first.

He had been sitting at Adachi's feet in the gateway with his bad leg stuck out, and then the lead was hanging empty from Adachi's hand and the dog was halfway down the yard, running on three legs and a cast, silent, with his ears flat and his curled tail straight out behind him. A Shikoku is a hunting dog from the mountains of Kōchi, bred for a thousand years to go after wild boar in the forest and hold them till the hunter comes. He did not bark. He did not go for the throat. He took the man by the forearm, low, as he had been trained to, and planted his feet in the gravel and held on.

The man threw his arm out. The dog went with it and came down and held on.

Rei felt the small hungry thing in the man feel the dog. It let go of the man's chest and went down his arm toward the teeth, quick and cold, looking for a way in. It had got into a hundred people that summer. It had never tried a dog.

Riki shook his head once, hard, as a dog shakes off water after a river.

The thing came off him in pieces. Rei felt it scatter. There was nothing in the dog for it to hold on to. No *what's the point*. Nothing of that kind at all. Only a smell of burnt fur and bone and Adachi's office and the job, and the job was to hold on, so he held on.

Adachi came down the yard at a walk in his grey suit. He did not hurry. He went down on one knee in the gravel beside his dog with some difficulty, a man who had come in off the field twenty years ago and sat behind a desk ever since, and put his hand on the dog's back.

"Good," he said. "Good. You can leave it now. Leave it, Riki."

Riki let go of the arm and sat down on the man's chest instead, with his tongue out, and looked at Adachi, very pleased.

The big man in the guard's shirt lay in the weeds and looked up at the stars with brown eyes. A moment later the tall man was beside him with two fingers on his neck, and then was not.

"Who's a clever lad," said Adachi to the dog, quite quietly, in a voice nobody on the third floor had ever heard him use. "Who's a clever, horrible lad." Then he stood up, and looked round, and saw Rei watching from the edge of the beach, and went a little pink, and walked back up to the gate with his dog.

He went through thirty of them in less time than it took Rei to count them. He did not hurt one. When it was done they were sitting in a ring on the grey sand round him, dazed and blinking and alive, like people woken in the middle of a picnic.

The tall man stood in the middle of them, breathing hard, with Gin's hair fallen in his eyes.

*Oh,* said Kishin. Pleased. *Look at that. The policeman's gifts and yours together. What a waste. All that, to put people down gently.*

"Yes," said the voice in Gin's mouth.

*Then let's see what you do with this,* said Kishin, and lifted her hand again.

[break Despair]

Rei felt it go into them.

Not a blow. Not fire. It was what she was. Grey and quiet and enormous, the river he had stood in that afternoon in the round room with eleven hands on his back. All of it. Every person in the city who had ever thought *what's the point*. Poured, all at once, into one body on the edge of a beach.

The body bent.

Rei felt the voice in it go under. Not a little way. All the way. The old thing that had come through to end her, that had been patient and cold and certain for longer than the city had been here, went under the grey like a stone into a pond. It had never felt despair. It had never had anything to feel it with. It had no idea what to do.

And Gin went under with it.

Rei felt that too. He felt his brother try to catch it, as he would have caught Rei falling off a kitchen chair, with both hands, without thinking. He felt Gin get his hands on it. And he felt the weight of it come down on both of them. The mender stopped where he stood, as cold stops a clock. And Gin went past him, down and down through the grey river, to the very bottom of himself, where it was dark and very quiet and nothing mattered at all.

The body went down on its knees on the grey sand.

Its eyes were open. There was nobody in them. The voice had stopped. Gin had gone down. Nobody was looking out.

*There,* said Kishin. Very softly. *There. That's better. Isn't it. It's so quiet down there. You can stay as long as you like.*

She came across the sand toward the kneeling body, slowly, with her hand out, as if to lay it on Gin's cheek.

[break Haruki]

*Haruki,* thought Rei.

He did not decide to. It came out of him through the empty frame, as loud as he had ever thought anything, louder, in capitals, as Kato always said he thought. Not at the yard. At one man, up on the road under the one working street lamp, with a wooden sword in his hand, who had been in everybody's head all night and had never once looked at anything he was not asked to.

*Haruki. He's at the bottom. She's pushed him down. I can't reach him. I can't go in. I don't know how. You do. You're the only one who knows him well enough to find him and not just the thing that's wearing him. Please.*

There was no answer.

Then there was a sound behind him. Somebody coming down through the weeds from the road, fast, not running, a heavy steady walk, with the swish of long grass against trouser legs. Rei turned his head.

Haruki came down off the road and through the weeds onto the grey sand in his sweater in August, with his flask gone and his wooden sword in his hand. He did not look at Kishin. He did not look at Rei. He walked straight across the sand to the body kneeling in the middle of the ring of dazed people, and knelt down in front of it, and dropped the sword on the sand beside him, and put both his hands on either side of Gin's head.

*Don't,* said Kishin. Interested. *You'll go down too.*

"Probably," said Haruki, out loud.

He closed his eyes.

Rei went with him. He did not mean to. He was holding everything on the beach through the empty frame, and Haruki was on the beach, and so when Haruki went in, Rei went a little way behind him, as a younger brother follows an older one down a dark stair without being asked.

[break The workshop]

Telepaths say there is a door behind the eyes. It is not a door. It is only the word they use, because there is no better one, for the place where you stop listening to a person from outside and start walking about inside them. Most of them never go further than the doorway. It is not allowed, without permission, and it is not safe. Inside, a person is a country, with its own weather, and the weather is whatever has been living there longest.

What had been living inside Gin for a year was the mender.

Rei had expected the dark. He had imagined it so many times, lying awake in the flat in Jūjō. Gin trapped under the floor of his own head in a black hole with a circle of sky very far above. And there was a dark, somewhere below. He could feel it. But that was not where the door let them in.

It let them into a workshop.

A long low room with a floor of beaten earth and walls of old dark wood, and a high window of oiled paper that let in a soft yellow light like late afternoon. Shelves on every wall, from the floor to the beams. And on the shelves, mended things.

Bowls, mostly. Plain country bowls and fine tea bowls and a child's rice bowl with a rabbit on it, every one of them broken once and put back together, with the cracks filled in a line of gold. In Japan they call that *kintsugi*. You mend a broken bowl with lacquer and dust the seam with gold, so that the break does not disappear. It shows. It is the most beautiful part of the bowl. The bowl is worth more afterwards than it was before, because now it has a history, and somebody cared enough to keep it.

Not only bowls. Rei walked along the shelves behind Haruki and saw what else was there. A boy's arm, small and thin, mended at the wrist with a line of gold, lying on a folded cloth like something in a museum. An old man's teacup, chipped at the lip, with the chip filled in gold. A walkway in Tabata, no bigger than a hand, with a tired man asleep on it. A woman's heart, still beating slowly in a dish. A step. Just a stone step, with a gold line across it where it had cracked.

A year of work. Everything the mender had put back since he came through. Kept in order, on clean shelves, labelled in a careful old-fashioned hand that Rei could not read, as a craftsman keeps the things he is proudest of.

It smelled of lacquer and warm wood and green tea.

It was the kindest place Rei had ever been.

And the grey was coming in.

It came under the door at the far end of the workshop like flood water under a shop door in a typhoon. Slow and flat and quiet. It had already covered the earth floor. It was rising up the legs of the workbench. Where it touched the lowest shelves the gold seams on the bowls went dull, one after another, and the bowls looked only broken. In the middle of the room the lamp on the workbench had gone out, and the oil in it was cold.

The mender was not there. Rei could feel why. He was not a man in a room. He was the room. He was the shelves and the light and the smell of lacquer and the lamp, and the grey had come in over all of it, and stopped him, as cold stops a clock.

Haruki did not stop. He waded through the grey to the far end of the workshop, past the lamp, and found what Rei had not seen. A trapdoor in the earth floor, under the workbench. Old wood, with an iron ring. The grey was pouring down through the cracks round it.

Haruki pulled the ring. The trapdoor came up. Below it there were stairs going down into the dark.

He went down them.

[break Gin]

Below the workshop, everything was Gin's.

Rei knew it at once, as you know your own house in the dark by the smell of it. The stairs came out into the corridor of the Division building, on the sixth floor, with the strip lights humming and the brown carpet and the water cooler that made a noise like a stomach. But it was not quite the corridor. It went on too long. The doors along it were not offices. They were Gin's.

Through the first one Rei saw the kitchen in Jūjō. The table with the two chairs. The rice cooker with *Eat* written on the lid in marker. A pan of ginger pork on the stove, and the calendar by the door with Rei's exam dates circled in red. Nobody in it. The grey was lying on the floor in there too, a centimetre deep, and the light was very dim.

Through the next, a basement bar in Nezu on a Thursday night, with an old man playing piano. And behind the counter, making coffee very slowly with a kettle with a long thin spout, in a dark green sweater with the sleeves pushed up, was Haruki.

Haruki stopped in the doorway.

Rei had never seen him stop for anything. He stood in the corridor and looked through the door at himself, as Gin had seen him. Rei felt how Gin had seen him. The warmth of it. The steadiness. A man who never asked a question he did not need the answer to and always had the coffee ready before you sat down. There was nothing in that room that Haruki did not already know about himself, except how much Gin had liked it.

Then the grey rose round Haruki's ankles, and with it came the voice.

*You didn't see,* it said. Kishin. Quiet and reasonable, from everywhere. *All those Thursdays. He stopped coming in August, and you thought he was busy. You're a telepath. You're his friend. He sat across the counter from you in July with a thing growing in him and you made him coffee and talked about jazz. What's the point of you?*

Haruki stood very still in the grey.

Rei felt it go into him. It was true. That was the worst of it. Kishin never lied. She only picked the true thing that hurt most and held it up to the light.

Haruki put his hand into the pocket of his sweater. There was a flower in it. Rei had never known it was there. A small dried flower, pressed flat and brown, from a house by a river in Kita in 2012, where a family kept getting sick and nobody could work out why, and two young Division officers had sat up all night in a kitchen and worked it out together. Haruki had carried it in a pocket ever since.

He held it in his fist. He did not answer the voice. He walked on.

Down the corridor. Past a room full of case files stacked to the ceiling. Past a room that was only the noise of trains. Past a room with a white-haired woman asleep in a chair, and one with a crow on a wall. The corridor sloped down, and the grey got deeper, up to Haruki's knees, then his waist, and the light got dimmer, and the voice went on saying true things, very quietly, and Haruki walked through it like a man walking into the sea, steadily, without lifting his arms.

At the bottom of the corridor there was a walkway.

The open-air walkway outside the flat in Jūjō, under the orange lamp, at night, with the rain coming down slantwise past the railing. A door with a brass number. And on the concrete outside the door, wet, a key.

This was where it had happened. Rei knew it before he saw the clock. Nothing moved here. The rain did not reach the ground. It hung in the air in lines, stopped. A quarter to four in the morning, on the thirteenth of August, a year and three days ago, held still, like a photograph somebody could not bring himself to take down.

Haruki stepped over the key and opened the door.

[break The room]

Inside, there was no flat. There was only one small room.

It was not much. Four tatami mats. A low window with the curtain drawn. A sofa much too small for the room, and on the sofa, asleep, with his school jacket still on and one foot off the end, a boy of about ten. That was all there was in the room. That was all there had ever been in it. The one thing Gin had kept in the middle of himself for as long as it had been the two of them in the flat in Jūjō, behind a door that nobody else was let through.

The grey had not got in here. It lay outside the door like water outside a ship's hatch.

And on the floor, with his back against the door and his knees drawn up and his arms round them, sitting very still and guarding the boy on the sofa, there was Gin.

Alone.

He looked up when the door opened behind him. He did not look surprised. He looked very tired.

*Took you long enough,* said Gin.

*I was making coffee,* said Haruki. *For a thousand people.* He crouched down on the tatami in front of him. His sweater was wet to the chest with grey. *Get up. Rei's on the beach and she's got her hand out.*

Gin did not get up.

*He's better at it than me,* he said. His voice was flat. Kishin's flat. *The mender. You saw his room. Everything he touches, he fixes. He fixed a boy's arm tonight on a bridge. He fixed Tanizaki's step. I've been watching him do it from down here for a year. All I ever did was read things and write them down. What's the point of me going back up? He's got the body. He's better in it.*

Haruki looked at him for a long moment.

*He can't get up,* he said. *I came through his room. The lamp's out. She's put the grey over all of it, and he's never felt it before, and he doesn't know what it is. He's stopped. He doesn't know you can get up out of it.* He paused. *You do. You've done it every morning for a year. Down here, under all of that. You got up every single morning and sat by this door. And before that, for years, in that flat, with nobody helping you. You know how to get up when there's no point. He doesn't. He's never had to.*

Gin said nothing.

*Rei's on the beach,* said Haruki again, quite gently. *He's got no door. Everything's coming into him. He can feel you not getting up.*

Gin looked past him at the boy on the sofa.

Then he unfolded his arms from round his knees, and put one hand flat on the tatami, and pushed, and stood up. It took him two tries. Haruki did not help him.

He went out past Haruki, through the door, onto the walkway in the stopped rain. He bent and picked up the key from the concrete and put it in his pocket, and the rain began to fall again, properly, onto the ground, and the clock in the kitchen behind them went on from a quarter to four.

Then he went up.

[break The lamp]

He went up much faster than Haruki had come down. Up the walkway steps and along the sloping corridor, past the bar and the kitchen, through the grey, which was up to his chest now and saying true things in his ear the whole way, and he did not answer them either. He went up the stairs under the trapdoor two at a time and came out into the workshop, where the mender's year of work stood on its shelves in the dimness with its gold gone dull, and the lamp was out on the bench.

Gin waded to the bench. He found the lamp by touch. He found, by touch, on the shelf above it, where he knew it would be, because it was the place he would have put them, a box of matches. Ordinary kitchen matches. The kind with a red head that you buy at the konbini by the bridge in a little box with a picture of a bird.

He struck one. It went out. The grey was in the air.

He struck another, and cupped it in his hand, and held it to the wick, and waited, with his hand round the little flame, while it caught.

The lamp lit.

It was only a small light. Yellow. Steady. It did not drive the grey out. But on the shelves, nearest the lamp, the gold seams on the bowls began to show again, one at a time. And Rei felt the room wake up round it, slowly, as a man wakes who has been knocked down, and does not yet know where he is, and opens his eyes, and sees somebody he knows bending over him with a light.

*There you are,* said Gin to the room. *It's all right. It's just grey. I know it. I've lived in it. You get up anyway. Come on. I'll show you.*

Haruki opened his eyes.

He was kneeling on the grey sand with his hands on Gin's head, and his nose was bleeding into his mouth and down his chin onto his sweater, and his face was the colour of ash. He took his hands away. He sat back on his heels.

In front of him, Gin's eyes were not empty any more.

[break Severance]

Kishin had stopped a metre away with her hand still out.

She looked at Gin's face. At the eyes, with two people in them again. At the man kneeling beside him with blood on his chin. Her kind borrowed face did not change. But the hand that was out went, very slowly, into a fist, and she looked at the body in front of her with something new in her eyes.

Rei saw it, and understood it before anybody else.

She wanted it.

Not to drown it. To have it. A body the mender had chosen because it was the right shape, the strongest she had ever seen, with two gifts of its own and two more put into it, kneeling on the sand in front of her, exhausted, with two tired people in it who had only just stood up. She had worn a great many coats. She had never worn one like this.

*Oh,* she said. *Oh, I could do so much in that.*

And she came into him.

Not with her hand. With the rest of her. Out of Rinka. Out of the small neat woman in the cardigan, who sagged like a coat slipping off a hook, and across the metre of grey sand between them, grey and quiet and enormous, into the kneeling body on the sand. Rei felt her go in. He felt her come in through the eyes and pour down into the workshop like flood water, over the shelves and the lamp Gin had just lit, and begin to settle in it as if it were hers.

He felt his brother be very, very frightened.

He had felt Gin frightened before. Twice in his whole life. Once, when Rei was eight and had a fever and would not wake up. Once on the walkway outside the flat at a quarter to four in the morning, a year and three days ago, for one second, before the dark came down. This was more than both.

*He asked me how fast a man could wake it,* Uruma had said, in the Sigil Room, with a needle in his hand. *I said it depends how frightened he is. He laughed. He said, then I'll have to be very frightened.*

Something woke up in the middle of Gin's chest.

Rei felt it. Through the empty frame. Through the sand. Through everything. A seal. Old black lines laid into the skin over his brother's breastbone years ago by a man with a shaved head and silver in his ears, asleep ever since, waiting. It woke the way a held breath goes out. All at once. All of it.

*Severance.*

Uruma had told Rei about it in the Sigil Room, with a needle in his hand. The strongest seal he had ever put on anybody. A seal that cuts a thing out of a body. It does not stay awake like other seals. It sleeps, and it has to be woken, every time, and waking it takes everything you have, and how fast it wakes depends on how frightened you are.

Gin was more frightened than he had ever been in his life.

It cut.

Not her. Gin could not cut her. She was too big, too old, too much. It cut the place where she had come in. The thread. The one grey line between the woman sagging on the sand and the body she was trying to wear. It cut it as scissors cut a kite string, cleanly, at the knot, and everything of her that was still on the far side of the cut, still in Rinka, still on the beach, still holding on to the old coat because she had never in her life let go of anything, stayed on that side.

And everything of her that had already come into Gin was suddenly not attached to anything at all.

*Now,* said Gin, inside, to the mender, through his teeth. *Now. Get her out. I can't. That's all I've got. Now.*

The mender did not get her out.

The mender did what it was made of. It found every piece of her that had come into Gin's body, every grey loose drifting piece, and put it back. Where it belonged. Out through the cut place, across the sand, into the old coat she had left. Into Rinka. Into the vessel, where a demon can be ended, on the human side. Gently. As you put a stone back where you found it.

Gin fell forward onto his hands on the grey sand.

Haruki caught him by the shoulders.

The Severance seal in the middle of his chest went cold and dark and still, and stayed that way. Rei felt it. All of it spent. All those years of sleeping, gone in one breath, as Uruma had said it would be. Gin knelt on his hands with his head hanging and his hair in his face, shaking from head to foot, and Haruki held him up and did not let go.

"That's mine," said Gin. Hoarse. To nobody. To the sand. "That seal was mine. I was saving it. For years. For if something came for Rei." He laughed, wet and broken, with his head down. "I was very frightened."

"I know," said Haruki. "I could tell."

[break What Gin said]

Kishin was back in Rinka. All of her that was left. Rei could feel her in there, pushed back into the old coat through a cut she could not close, and for the first time all night she was not standing very straight.

Gin lifted his head. Haruki let go of his shoulders, slowly, and Gin knelt up on the sand and looked at her.

Kishin's face went very still.

"You think he's gone soft," said Gin. The body's jaw set, as Gin's jaw set over a bad report. "He has. I watched him do it. He sat on an old man's step and drank tea for ten months and listened. He mended a boy's arm on a bridge tonight and the boy said thank you and he didn't know what to say back. He grieved an old man who was nothing to him. He told me his name over three cups." The body's hands closed into fists. "He's not a coat and I'm not a coat. There are two of us in here, and we decided. On a beach. Ten minutes ago."

*You,* said Kishin. Very quietly. *You're awake. He let you.*

"He moved over," said Gin. "Like on a bench. And when you pushed us under, he stopped, because he'd never been sad in his life and didn't know what it was. And I went to the bottom. And a friend of mine came down and got me, and I came up and lit his lamp." His voice cracked. "You wouldn't know. You've never sat next to anybody in your life. You only ever sat across a table from them and poured."

And the voice spoke, very quietly, in Gin's mouth, in the polite careful voice that set each word down like a cup.

"You broke things," it said. "On the other side. For as long as there has been another side. You broke them and you kept the pieces. That is all you have ever been. A thing made of pieces that belong to other people." The body took a step. Onto the grey sand. "I mend. That is all I have ever been. I came through to end you, and I did not know how, because ending is not a thing I know how to do." Another step. "Tonight I found out that I do not need to end you. I only need to do what I am. I am going to put the pieces back."

[break Mended]

He got up off his knees. It took him two tries. Haruki pushed him the second time.

He put his hands on her face.

Both of them. Gin's long hands. Flat, on either side of Rinka's face, as she had put her hand on Tanizaki's cheek on the step. Gently. And Rei felt them do it together, Gin and the voice. One pair of hands, two people. Gin's clairsentience, finding every piece. The mender's gift, putting each one back where it had come from.

Rei never forgot what it looked like.

It looked like somebody mending a broken bowl. Every piece. Every chip. Every crack. Found, and turned the right way up, and set back in its place.

He felt it go out of her. Not into the body. Not into the river. *Back.* A grief she had drunk a hundred years ago went back to a house in Fukagawa that was not there any more, and settled into the ground under a car park, and was quiet. A lonely boy she had sat with long ago got his loneliness back, and with it, somehow, the memory of the friend he had made the week after, which she had taken too. Mika's longing went back to Mika, sitting in the weeds beside an old dog. Kaneda's tiredness went back to Kaneda, on a walkway in Tabata, with a glass of barley tea. The woman in the cardigan got back the warmth of a hand on her knee, and with it the knowledge that it had not been real, and under that, smaller, the knowledge that a boy holding out his hand on a walkway had been.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Going out of her into the dark like birds released from a net.

And as each one went, there was less of her.

That was the thing. That was what she had never known, in all that time, because she had never once given anything back. She was made of what she had taken. Nothing else. And as the mender found every piece and put it back where it belonged, there was less and less of her left to hold the shape.

*Stop,* said Kishin.

Her voice was very small now. Not the bottom of a lake. A puddle.

*Please.*

The hands did not move.

"That word," said the voice quietly, "you have to mean." It looked at her out of Gin's eyes. "I learned that this year. It took me a year. You do not have a year."

The last piece went.

It was very small. A cup of tea. Offered, on a step, by an old man in a faded blue jinbei, at the end of his life, to the thing that was killing him. *You came all this way. Have some tea.* She had not taken it. She had walked away. But for one second she had wanted to. Rei had seen it. Something small and quick, very far down, like a hand reaching out by old habit for a thing it had not been offered in a very long time.

The mender found that too. And put it back on the step.

And there was nothing left of Kishin at all.

[break Rinka]

The woman on the beach did not fall.

She stood with Gin's hands on either side of her face and blinked. Her eyes were brown. Small and ordinary and wet. She looked up at the tall man in front of her as if she had never seen him before, and she had not.

"Where," she said. Her own voice. Thin and hoarse, like a voice that has not been used for years. "Where is she. Where did she go."

The hands came away from her face, very carefully.

"She is gone," said the voice. "There is nothing left of her."

Fukai Rinka looked round the grey beach. At the low white table. At the teapot. At the cushions. Her face crumpled.

"She was the only one who ever..." she said, and could not finish it, and sat down on the grey sand beside the table with her knees drawn up and put her face on them and cried. Not quietly. Like somebody whose god has died.

The little river stone on its cord had slid off her neck and lay in the sand. Grey, with a white band through it. Ordinary. Just a stone.

It cracked. Very quietly. Along the white band. And lay there in two pieces.

Gin's body knelt down on the grey sand beside her. It did not touch her. It knelt there, a little way off, and stayed. Rei could not tell, from where he stood, which of them was doing it. He thought perhaps it was both.

[break The sea]

Out on the grey sea, the shapes in the fog went very still.

Rei felt them look. At the beach. At the woman crying on the sand. At the empty table with nobody to call them to it any more. At the tall man kneeling, who turned his head and looked out at them across the grey water. Calmly. Politely. Like a doctor looking up from a patient at somebody who has come into the room without knocking.

*Next,* said that look. *Who's next.*

The shapes turned.

One by one. Slowly. Without any hurry. Like clouds when the wind changes. They turned away from the beach, and went back down into the fog, and the fog took them, and the grey sea was empty.

Inoue Misaki lowered her hand. The bubble of nothing in front of her went out like a soap bubble popping. She looked at her palm with mild interest. On her shoulders, the ginger cat yawned.

"Huh," she said. "That's that, then."

Beside her, Kurosawa took his hands out of his pockets for the first time.

He walked up the sand to where Gin's body knelt beside the crying woman and crouched down on his heels behind it, and put two fingers flat on the back of Gin's neck, under the hair, as a doctor feels for a pulse. He stayed like that a long time. His dark eyes moved, a little, as if he were reading small print.

"Chest's burnt out," he said, to Haruki, who was sitting in the sand with his sleeve pressed to his nose. "That seal won't wake again. The one on the rib is still asleep. So's the little one on the sole of his foot. He's got perhaps an hour of standing left in him tonight, and the policeman's got less than that, and he's going to be sick in about four minutes." He took his fingers away. "Both of them are in there. Neither of them is going anywhere. I don't know what you'd call that. I've never seen it."

"Friends," said Haruki, through the sleeve.

Kurosawa thought about it. Then he stood up and put his hands back in his pockets.

Gin's body was sick on the grey sand four minutes later. Haruki held his hair.

And at the edge of the beach, where the old rails ran down into the water, the grey began, very slowly, to go out of the sea.

Not all of it. Not yet. The night was still thin. But Rei could see the real river now, black and ordinary, coming up through the grey like a street through morning fog. The lights of the city on it. A long way off, on the other bank, somebody's last sending fire still glowing red in a dish on a balcony.

He looked down at his own chest. At the empty frame where his door had been. Everything was still coming in.

And through it, very faint, from very far away, from somewhere beyond the white and the grey and the river and the city, something he had felt on the back of his neck twice before and had never once turned to look at.

The light.

It had turned. He could feel it, as you can feel with your eyes shut that someone in the room has turned to face you. It was facing north, toward Kita, toward the yard where he stood.

Rei put his hand over the cold latch on his wrist and did not turn round. Behind him Haruki was wiping his nose on his sleeve. Gin was still kneeling on the sand beside the woman who was crying, a little way off from her, not touching her. On Misaki's shoulders the ginger cat had gone to sleep.
