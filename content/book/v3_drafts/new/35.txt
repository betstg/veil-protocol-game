#title Gorō

After that, whenever Rei had half an hour in the building, he went up to the roof.

He did not go every day at first. There was too much else. A trainee at the Division had to be everywhere at once. He sat in Investigation with Okada and learned the boring half of three hundred files. He sat at the bottom of long tables in meetings with Field Operations and Containment and Assessment and Public Confidence, one after another, and was introduced, and bowed, and forgot everyone's name, and had to be introduced again. On Saturdays he went to Tanizaki, and then to the bath, and then to Hara Setsu's kitchen. On Tuesdays, Café Asahi. On Fridays, Haruki and his slow circles of coffee. Every group had its ladder. The Division. The Grid. The families. Even the kids in Kabukichō had theirs. He was at the bottom of every one, and he had to learn all of them at once.

But whenever he had half an hour, he climbed the last flight past the sign that said SERIOUSLY, and pushed the steel door, and stood at the low wall and looked at the city.

And every time, within a few minutes, the black and white crow came.

It never came straight to him. It landed on the water tank first, or on the humming air conditioning boxes, and sat there pretending to be interested in something else. A pigeon. A cloud. The Shinkansen sliding past in the distance like a white needle. Then it walked along the wall toward him, very casually, as if it happened to be going that way anyway. Then it stopped a metre from his elbow and looked at his pocket.

Rei always brought two rice balls now. One for himself and one for the crow. The crow always took his.

After the first week it started bringing him things. The green cap off a melon soda bottle. A screw. A length of red ribbon. A child's plastic hair clip shaped like a strawberry. It dropped them on the wall beside his hand, stepped back, and waited, very stiffly, to see what he would do. Rei put them in his pocket and said thank you, and the crow made a small satisfied noise in its throat, like a creaky door being proved right about something.

He talked to it. He did not mean to, at first. It was easier than talking to people. He told it about Okada's finger tapping the page. About Kato in the lift, listening to everybody and pretending not to. About the cactus on Gin's desk by the window that Kato watered on Mondays. About the hook by the door at home where nothing hung. The crow listened with its head tipped to one side, the pale blue eye on top, and when he stopped, sometimes, it made the creaky noise again, as if to say *go on, then*.

[break The aviary man]

In the second week of January he found out where it came from.

He was on the roof at lunchtime, with the crow beside him on the wall eating his rice ball with its back turned, when the steel door crashed open behind them, and a man came out onto the roof backwards, laughing, talking to somebody on the stairs.

"No, no, I've got him, I can see him, go and eat your lunch. Yes. I promise. I'll bring you a bun." He turned round. He had a paper bag from a bakery in one hand and a thick leather falconer's glove on the other, and on the glove sat the largest crow Rei had ever seen. Glossy black from beak to tail, without a feather out of place, with an expression of enormous patience, like a butler.

The man was about fifty. Short and weathered, with grey stubble and a padded work vest over a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled up, and rubber boots spattered with something that was almost certainly bird. His lanyard was tucked inside his vest so you could not read it. He had an easy, open, creased face, and when he saw Rei and the piebald crow side by side on the wall, it opened even further.

"Oh, no way," he said. "No *way*. He's eating out of your hand?"

"Out of my pocket, mostly," said Rei. "Sorry. Is he yours?"

"He's nobody's. That's the whole tragedy." The man came over, picking his way between the air conditioning boxes, and the big black crow on his glove turned its head and looked at the piebald one with the weary contempt of an older brother. "Sugita. Aviary. I look after the birds, because nobody else would, and I like birds better than people. Don't tell my wife I said that." He held out the bakery bag. "Cream bun? I bought eight. I always buy eight. I never remember how many people there are."

Rei took a cream bun. It seemed rude not to.

"That," said Sugita, nodding at the piebald crow, "is Gorō. And this," lifting the glove, "is Mame. Mame finds the others when they get out. He's very good at it. He's been finding Gorō on this roof three times a week since October." He sighed. "Gorō doesn't care. Gorō doesn't care about anything."

Gorō finished the rice ball, turned round on the wall, and looked at Sugita with the black eye. Then at Mame with the blue one. Then he walked along the wall, very slowly, until his feathers were touching Rei's sleeve, and stood there.

Sugita stared.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. That's new."

[break The training camp]

[img cvd_camp.png | The training camp]

The aviary was in the training camp, and the training camp was the strangest place in the building.

You reached it through a fire door on the third floor, a green door with a little running man on it that should have opened onto a stairwell. It opened onto grass instead. A wide walled green, open to the sky, with bare winter trees and a pond and a long wooden shed, much too big to fit inside a building in Marunouchi, and nobody ever explained it, and after a week nobody ever thought about it. Trainees did forms there on Tuesday and Friday mornings, the slow careful movements every practitioner learned first, how to stand and breathe and open and close, so the body would know what to do when something frightened it, before the mind had time to argue. Rei did them badly, in a tracksuit, in a row of trainees all doing them better than him, while Aoi beside him murmured "left foot" and "breathe" and "other left foot" without moving her lips.

Afterward, with grass on his knees, he went and stood at the aviary wire.

It ran the whole length of one wall, three metres high, full of noise. Crows on the perches squabbling over food and shouting at each other in the cold. At the far end, under a wooden roof, the owls sat in a row with their eyes shut, round and grey and silent as stones.

And in the top corner, alone, sat Gorō.

Sugita told him the story on the second Friday, leaning on the wire beside him with Mame on his glove and a cream bun in his other hand.

"Hatched here. Six years ago. Out of an egg somebody found in a nest on the roof after a typhoon." He took a bite. "Clever. Cleverest bird I've ever seen. Learned everything we showed him faster than any of the others. Sit on the mirror. Come to the glove. Go where you're sent. Come back." He chewed. "And then he decided he wasn't going to do any of it. Ever. For anyone."

"Why?"

"No idea. Nobody knows." Sugita shrugged. "Four handlers tried to train him properly. Then a fifth. Then they gave up. He won't fly where he's sent. Some days he won't fly at all, he just sits there looking at you like you're a disappointment. He bites. He steals. He undoes knots. In October he got into the Director's office through a window and took a pen off his desk and dropped it in that pond." He pointed at the pond with his bun. "A very expensive pen. The Director was very nice about it, which was worse."

Gorō, in his corner, turned his head and looked at them both with the blue eye. Then he looked away, at the sky over the wall, the way he looked at it every day.

"You know what I think?" said Sugita, more quietly. "I think he's bored. I think he's the cleverest thing in that cage and he knows it, and he's watched the others go out on jobs for six years and come back, and go out and come back, and he's never once been sent anywhere he wanted to go." He finished the bun and dusted his fingers on his beautiful trousers without looking. "I was going to let him go. In the spring. Open the door and let him go over the wall. He's no use here. He's miserable here. And he's making the others miserable."

Rei looked at the crow in the top corner.

"Don't," he said.

Sugita turned and looked at him.

"Could I have him?" said Rei. "Instead of you letting him go. Could I take him?"

[break The mirror room]

[img cvdroom_mirror_room.png | The mirror room]

Sugita did not answer straight away. He took Rei to the mirror room first, so that he would know what he was asking for.

It was through a door beside the camp door that Rei had never noticed, and it was the strangest room in a strange building. A small, dim room, and every wall of it, from floor to ceiling, was covered in mirrors. Every shape and size and age. Round and square and long and narrow. Heavy carved frames, and no frames at all. A cracked bathroom mirror. A little round one from a woman's handbag. Hundreds of them, fitted edge to edge like the scales of a fish. A few old armchairs. A kettle on a shelf, and a tin of tea, and four people in cardigans sitting in the armchairs with cups in their hands, looking at the walls.

And not one of the mirrors showed the room.

Each one showed somewhere else. Somewhere in the city, seen from above, moving. A roof in Itabashi with laundry flapping. A corner in Ikebukuro with people crossing at the lights. The surface of a river, very close, the light shaking on it. A temple roof. A car park. A schoolyard full of running children. A train going over a bridge. A branch, swaying, and through the leaves a window, and through the window a woman sitting at a table with her head in her hands. Hundreds of small silent moving pictures of Tokyo, glowing softly in the dark like the windows of a building at night.

One of the people in cardigans glanced up, nodded at Sugita, and went back to watching a mirror that showed a petrol station in Adachi from the top of a lamp post. She drank her tea. She did not blink very much.

"Surveillance," said Sugita quietly. "They do eight-hour shifts. They drink a lot of tea." He stood with Mame on his glove, looking at the walls. "Every mirror in here is bound to a crow from the aviary. When the binding's done, the mirror shows what that crow sees. Always. Day and night. Wherever it is." He nodded up at a few mirrors near the ceiling, older and darker than the rest. "Those were made by a medium for the Division, a long time ago. They see further. Nobody touches those without a form from the eighth floor." He was quiet for a moment. "And some mirrors aren't here at all. They're in a desk drawer, or a coat pocket. Those are the people who have their own bird. Maybe four in the building."

"You're one."

"I'm one." Sugita lifted the glove an inch, and Mame shifted his weight with immense dignity. "I do the bindings, too. The mirror rite. My grandmother taught me. I did the last eleven in this building." He hesitated. "I did your brother's. Six years ago. His first winter."

Rei turned and looked at him.

"I didn't know he had one."

"He did." Sugita did not look at him. "His mirror was in his desk. Third floor. By the window." A pause. "Nobody's touched it since August. Nobody's allowed to, while he's on assignment."

Rei stood in the dim room among the hundreds of small bright moving windows and could not speak. Somewhere among them, he thought, there might be one that showed a branch outside a white house on a quiet street. Or a kitchen with yellow walls. Or a third-floor walkway on a hill above Ōji, with a brass hook by the door.

"Does Gorō have one?" he said at last.

"Gorō's never let anybody finish," said Sugita. "Four people started. He bit all four." He turned to the door. "There's no mirror in this room for Gorō. I'd have to ask the Director to let you even try. A bird's Division property. Even that one."

"Will you ask him?"

Sugita looked at him for a long moment, with his easy, open face for once not easy at all.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, I will. I'm curious now. Anomalies should stick together."

"Anomalies?"

"You're a Postulate," said Sugita, with a grin. "So was I, for thirty years. The birds don't care." He held the door open. "Come on. I'll buy you a coffee. You can tell me why a bird who bites everybody doesn't bite you."

[break The fourth floor]

The Director saw him on the Tuesday, at four o'clock, for six minutes.

Adachi stood at the window with the worn transit card turning in his fingers, and Sugita stood by the door with his hands behind his back, and Rei stood in front of the desk in his too-long suit.

"Sugita tells me," said Adachi, "that you want the bird who put my pen in the pond."

"Yes, sir."

"Why?"

Rei had thought about it all weekend. He had thought of several good answers. He gave none of them.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Sir. I just know he's the only thing in this building that doesn't want anything from me except a rice ball."

Adachi turned his head and looked at him. That fast, complete look that seemed to finish before it started. The card stopped turning.

"That is a very bad reason," he said. "It is also the only honest one I have heard in this office this week." The card turned again. "One month. Take him out. See what he is for. If at the end of the month he is still useless, Sugita opens the door and lets him go over the wall, and you do not argue." He looked out of the window. "If he is useful, come back and show me. I would like to see that."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir."

"Don't thank me," said Adachi. "Thank Sugita. He told me you were worth the pen."

[break Out]

The first day, Gorō would not leave the aviary.

Sugita opened the door, and Rei stood in the doorway with a boiled egg in his palm, and Gorō sat in his top corner and looked at the egg and looked at Rei and looked at the open door and did not move. For forty minutes. The other crows went mad, flapping and shouting at the open door, and Sugita had to shut it on them twice. Gorō did not move.

The second day, he came down to the egg. He ate it standing on Rei's wrist, his claws cold and tight through the shirt cuff, his heavy beak knocking against Rei's palm. Then he looked at Rei's wrist, at the place where the pulse was. Looked at it with the blue eye for a long time. Then he pressed the side of his head against it, once, briefly, and made a small low sound in his throat that was not a *kaa* at all.

"He's never made that noise," said Sugita softly, outside the wire. "Six years. I've never heard him make that noise."

The third day, Rei walked out of the aviary and across the grass and through the green door with the running man on it and down the stairs and out of the front door of the Division building onto the street by Ōji station, with Gorō sitting on his shoulder like a parrot on a pirate, and nobody in the lobby said a single word. The young man at the front desk, the one in the neat tie who had read Hagiwara Mizuki's sentences to Rei on the first morning, opened his mouth, looked at the crow, and closed it again.

Rei walked across the road. He stood on the pavement outside the station, in the cold, with the trains going over the bridge above him.

"Okay," he said, to the crow on his shoulder. "Go on."

Gorō looked at him with the black eye.

Then he went straight up, in four huge beats of his patched wings, up past the station roof and the electric wires and the bare trees on the hill, and up again into the pale January sky, and away east over the railway lines, getting smaller and smaller, a black and white speck, and then nothing.

Rei stood on the pavement and watched the empty sky.

"Well," he said to himself. "That's that, then."

He went back to work. He did not go up to the roof. He did not look out of the window. At six o'clock he walked home up the hill with his hands in his pockets and his collar up, and climbed the stairs to the third floor, and turned onto the walkway.

Gorō was sitting on the railing outside the door of the flat.

He had his feathers fluffed up against the cold and something in his beak. When Rei came along the walkway he hopped down and dropped it at Rei's feet, on the exact spot where, five months earlier, a cold wet key had lain in the dark.

It was a bottle cap.

Rei stood on the walkway and laughed until the woman in 304 opened her door to see what was wrong.

[break What he was for]

For three weeks Rei took him everywhere.

Or rather, Gorō came everywhere, which was not the same thing. He was never close. He was never far. Rei came out of the station in the morning and there was a black and white crow on the station clock. He walked to the Division and there was a black and white crow on the bicycle rack. He went to Tanizaki on Saturday and Gorō was already sitting on the stone lantern in the wild garden, as if he had been invited, and Tanizaki looked at him over the top of his newspaper for a long moment and then got up, without a word, and fetched a saucer of water, and set it on the step. He went to the bath, and Gorō sat on the chimney. He went to the Kondō bakery in Jūjō, and Mr. Kondō, without being asked, threw a corner of curry bread up onto the awning.

He never went where Rei told him.

Once, on a grey Sunday, Rei stood on the walkway and said, out loud, feeling stupid, "The river. The Arakawa. By the floodgate. Go and look." Gorō looked at him with the black eye and flew off very fast in completely the wrong direction. That evening Rei found him on the bakery awning in Jūjō again, watching a girl eat a melon bun.

But in the third week, something changed.

It was a Wednesday, and Rei was out shadowing Shimizu and Aoi on a call in Takinogawa, a flat where a family said their bathroom mirror fogged up in the shape of a hand every night at the same time. They went up. The mirror fogged. Aoi read it and said *nothing. A memory. Harmless*. They came down into the street, and the family thanked them, and Shimizu was already walking back toward the car.

And Gorō came down out of the sky like a thrown brick and landed on the roof of the car, right in front of her, and screamed.

He did not caw. He screamed. Over and over, a harsh furious sound, his wings half open, his beak wide, the blue eye fixed on the building next door. A narrow old wooden house with its shutters closed.

Shimizu stopped. She looked at the crow. She looked at the shuttered house next door, and Rei saw the small line between her eyebrows go very deep.

There was an old man in the house next door. He had been dead in his chair for four days, with the television on, and something had started to sit with him. Not much. Not yet. A small cold hungry thing in the corner, getting comfortable. In a week it would have been too comfortable to move. They moved it that afternoon. It took twenty minutes and one jar of iron.

In the car on the way back, Shimizu drove for a long time without speaking.

"Your bird," she said at last, "is better than the instruments."

"He's not my bird yet," said Rei.

"He is now," said Aoi, from the back seat, turning her bead bracelet round and round. "Did you see Shimizu's face? She's going to have to write a report about a crow. She's never going to live it down."

"Thank you, Nakamura," said Shimizu. But her eyes went to the mirror, and to the black and white shape sitting on the roof of the car in front of them at the lights, and stayed there a moment longer than they needed to.

[break On the roof]

Rei went up to the roof that night after everyone had gone home.

Gorō was on the wall. He was always on the wall now, when Rei came up. Rei stood next to him and looked at the city. The lights. The river. The hill across the tracks.

"They're going to want you to do that every time," he said. "You know that? Now they've seen it. They're going to want you to go where they send you, and come back, and sit on mirrors, and find cold places for them every day for the rest of your life."

Gorō looked at the city.

"Don't," said Rei.

The crow turned his head.

"I mean it." Rei put his hand on the wall, beside the crow's feet. "Do what you want. Most of the time. Go where you want. Come back if you want. If it's important, if somebody's going to get hurt, then help. Like today. But the rest of the time." He looked at the lights. "Don't just do what you're told. Not by them. Not by me either. Make your own mind up. You're cleverer than all of us." He laughed, very quietly. "You already knew that."

Gorō looked at him with the blue eye for a long time.

Then he pressed the side of his head against Rei's hand, on the cold concrete wall, once, and made the small low sound that was not a *kaa*.

"And don't tell anybody I said that," said Rei. "Especially not Sugita."

[break Dusk]

At the end of the month he brought Gorō back to the aviary.

He walked in through the green door with the running man on it with the crow on his shoulder, across the grass, to where Sugita was filling the water trough with a hose in his beautiful trousers, with Mame on the roof of the shed watching the water with deep suspicion.

"Gorō," said Rei. "Show him."

He lifted his arm. Gorō walked down it to his wrist. Rei pointed, without a word, at the top of the bare tree by the pond.

Gorō went up off his wrist in three strong beats and landed on the top of the tree. He sat there. He looked down at them.

Rei held out his wrist again, empty.

Gorō came down and landed on it. Light. Exact. His claws closed on Rei's sleeve. He looked at Sugita with the blue eye.

The hose went on running into the trough. It overflowed. The water went over Sugita's boots. He did not notice.

"Oh my God," he said. "Oh my God. He's never. Not once. Six years. *Six years*, and he does it for a trainee in a bad tie in four weeks." He turned off the hose at last and stood there with his trousers wet to the shin, staring at the crow on Rei's wrist, with his whole open face lit up like a boy's on New Year's morning. "How? How did you do it?"

Rei thought about the roof. About *don't just do what you're told*.

"I asked nicely," he said.

Sugita laughed so hard that Mame flew off the shed in disgust.

[break The rite]

The rite was at dusk the next day. Sugita did it.

The Director came down to watch. He stood at the edge of the grass in his grey suit with the worn card in his fingers and said nothing at all. Shimizu came, and stood with her hands in her pockets. Kato came, and Aoi, who had heard from Kato, and Katagiri from the Cattery in her grey cardigan, who had heard from Aoi. Uruma came down from the Sigil Room in an enormous black coat, because he had heard from everybody, and sat on an upturned bucket eating rice crackers, which Sugita politely asked him not to do near the birds, and he went on doing.

Sugita knelt on the cold grass in front of the aviary in his good trousers, the ones he had put on for the rite, without a thought for the knees of them. He laid a mirror face up on the grass. An empty one, from the cupboard at the back of the mirror room where they kept the ones nobody had bound. Square, about thirty centimetres across, in a cheap pine frame with one corner chipped, that looked as if it had come off the door of somebody's grandmother's wardrobe. He had painted the back black. The sky went into the glass, pale and darkening, with one thin cloud.

"The character is *sight*," he said, laying a strip of pale seal paper on his knee, and a fine brush, and a small clean knife. "視. It has to be written in your blood. Not mine. The bird's giving you something. You have to give something back, or the bargain doesn't hold." He held out his hand. "Left hand. Not the thumb."

Rei gave him his finger. Sugita pricked it so quickly and so gently that Rei did not feel it until the bead of blood came up, round and bright. Sugita dipped the brush, and then did a strange thing. He put the brush in Rei's right hand, and closed his own warm square hand round Rei's from behind, so that they held it together.

"I'll do the strokes," he said quietly, close to Rei's ear. For once he was not smiling. "You haven't got the hand for it yet. But the wanting's yours. I can't want him for you." His hand was perfectly steady. "Think about him. Not about the character. You think about him sitting on your wrist with his head against your arm."

Rei thought about it.

The brush moved. Sugita moved it, and Rei moved with it, and the character came out on the pale paper in Rei's blood, seven clean strokes, and Rei felt each stroke go in like a small key into a small lock.

"Oh," said Sugita softly, when it was done. "Oh, that's good. That's better than your brother's." He grinned suddenly, and was himself again. "Don't tell him I said that. He'll sulk."

He laid the seal face down on the glass. He laid three lengths of black cord beside it. He peeled a boiled egg and set it on the edge of the frame. And then he sat back on his heels and let Rei do the rest.

The sky in the glass went from pale to grey to violet. Lights came on in the windows of the building that went round the camp, which should not have been there and was. A train went by on the real railway outside. And the light reached that in-between place it reaches every evening, the moment the old people call *ōma-ga-toki*, the meeting hour, when it is not day and not night and you cannot quite see the face of the person walking toward you.

"Now," said Sugita.

Rei looked up at the roof of the aviary.

Gorō looked down at him.

Then he dropped off the roof, opened his patched wings, and came down in one low slow swoop across the dark grass, and landed with a small hard click of claws on glass in the very middle of the mirror. He stood on his own reflection. He looked at the egg. He did not eat it. He looked at Rei.

Rei laid the three black cords across the glass, edge to edge, very slowly, past Gorō's feet, not touching him. He said the old words, the ones from the back of Gin's book that Sugita had checked with him three times. He opened the door in his chest a finger's width, and then a little more, until the latch on his wrist went cold and held.

Gorō bent his head and looked down into the mirror at himself.

And the mirror changed.

[img animal_crow.png | The crow and the mirror]

The violet sky went out of the glass. It showed grass, very close. And Rei's own face, from above and to one side, huge and pale and serious, with his mouth moving. Too sharp at the edges. Too bright. Full of small things no person would notice, the shine of a button, a loose thread on his coat, the pulse moving in his neck. It was how a crow saw him.

And it stayed. Whatever Gorō turned his head to, the glass showed. The aviary wire. The pond. Uruma on his bucket, with a rice cracker halfway to his mouth. The Director at the edge of the grass, the worn card stopped in his fingers.

"There," said Sugita, very quietly.

Gorō hopped off the mirror and ate the egg in three furious gulps, as if he had been made to wait far too long for it. Then he flew up onto Rei's shoulder, and took the top of Rei's ear in his beak, quite hard, and held it.

"Ow," said Rei.

"That's the bargain," said Sugita, getting up off the grass with wet knees and his good trousers ruined and his face shining. "You've got him. He's got you. Nobody said he was going to be nice about it."

At the edge of the grass, the Director put the card back in his pocket. He looked at the crow on Rei's shoulder for a long moment. Then he looked at Sugita.

"Worth the pen," he said, and turned, and went back inside.

The mirror was supposed to live in the mirror room, with the others. Sugita wrapped it in a cloth and put it in Rei's hands.

"Nobody else is ever going to sign this one out," he said. "Take it home. Don't tell anybody I said so." He hesitated, and for one second the grin went, and there was something else under it, something careful and almost sad. "Look after him, Rei. Things like him don't come twice."

That night Rei tried the mirror, alone, in the flat on the hill, sitting on the floor with the window open and the glass on his knees, because Shimizu said you should always try the first one somewhere you could lie down afterward.

He looked into the black-backed glass and opened the door in his chest and thought, very clearly, *show me*.

And he was flying.

It was so sudden and so huge that he cried out. The flat fell away. The hill. The station. The railway lines, a bright silver tangle going away north and south. The whole of Kita spread out underneath like a dark cloth sewn with lights. Cold air under the wings. The tiny hard strength of a body that weighed nothing at all. And the sight. Gorō's sight. The sharp black eye that saw every lit window and every moving thing. And the pale blue eye that saw something else. Something under the lights. A faint shimmer over the river. A cold place by the old water tower where something unquiet sat. A thin grey thread, going west, very far, toward a quiet street of old wooden houses and one white one.

The blue eye saw the dead.

Then the blood came out of his nose and onto the glass, and the flat came back, and he was on his side on the tatami with his cheek on the cold mat, retching, with the window banging and his head ringing like a struck bell.

When he could sit up, Gorō was on the windowsill. Inside the room. Which he was not supposed to be. Looking at him with the blue eye.

"*Kaa,*" said Gorō. It sounded like a criticism.

Rei laughed, and it hurt, and he wiped his nose with the back of his hand and laughed again.

"Yeah," he said. "I know. I'll get better."

Gorō hopped down onto the floor and walked across the tatami with his rolling crow's walk and settled down beside Rei's hip, against his leg, with his feathers fluffed out, as if he had always slept there.

He was still there in the morning.
