#title Despair

Chen Lian rang at four in the morning on Thursday the sixth of August.

She did not say hello. She said, "It's now," and her voice was very steady, the way voices go when they have been crying and have stopped on purpose. "He woke me. In my head. He said *tell Uedera*. Come now."

Rei was out of the door before she had finished. He did not lock it. Gorō came down off the railing and onto his shoulder and stayed there, gripping through his T-shirt, all the way down the hill.

Kato was waiting at the bottom in the car with the engine running. He had not been asked. He had heard.

[break The round room]

They had moved him.

Not to the little room at the back, with the window onto the narrow street. To the round room on the top floor. To the old dark table with its thirteen chairs. Somebody had carried him up the stairs in the night, very carefully, and set him down in his own chair, the sixth, the one with a crack running across the back of it where somebody had once leaned too hard. He sat in it with his hands on the arms and his head against the cracked wood and his eyes open, facing west.

The grey had reached his throat.

All of him was grey now. His hands, his arms, his neck, the lower part of his face, grey and papery and very fine, like the ash of a cigarette that has burned all the way down without anybody touching it and still holds its shape. Only his eyes were still his. Brown, and tired, and kind, and open.

The thread came out of his chest, just under the heart, as thick as a finger now and the colour of a winter sky, and ran straight across the room and out through the round window that faced west, and away.

They were all there.

Rei saw them as he came through the door and did not count them, but they were all there. Eleven people standing round the old table, not sitting. Mori with his arms folded and his face like a furnace door. Hoshino with his hands clasped in front of his apron. Nakashima in her running jacket, shaking. Sakuma with his eyes closed. Kobayashi in her black suit, looking at the floor. Okabe in her construction jacket with her fists in her pockets. Park Junho with his phone switched off for once, held in both hands. Kasumi on top of a bookcase, not swinging his legs. Inoue in the corner, very still, which was the strangest thing of all. Chen Lian by Watanabe's chair with one hand on the back of it. And a laptop open on the table with Mio's face on the screen, in a dark room in Kyoto, with her plain brown clip in her hair and the dark circles under her eyes darker than ever.

Nobody said anything when he came in.

Mori unfolded his arms.

"Chen told me," he said. His voice was low and rough and very controlled, and the air round him shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers over a road in summer. "On Sunday. All of it. The roof. The thing in your brother. *Please.*" His jaw worked. "I set fire to two chairs and a bookcase and Hoshino's apron. Then I sat in the boiler room for a day and a half." He looked at Rei with eyes like coals. "We are going to have a very long conversation about that, Uedera. Very long. Very loud." He nodded at the chair, at the grey man in it. "After. Do what you promised. We'll hold the room."

[break The promise]

Rei went and knelt in front of the chair.

*Hello, Uedera,* said Watanabe, inside his head. Very, very faint. Like someone speaking from the far end of a long corridor in a library at closing time, with the lights going off one by one. *You came. Thank you. You didn't have to.*

*I promised,* thought Rei.

*I know.* Something that might have been a smile, a long way down. *Promises are for demons. I told you. And here you are.*

The thread hummed.

Rei could feel it now, close up, with the door in his chest open. The way you feel the hum of a power line if you stand under it in the rain. It was pulling. Steady, patient, unhurried, the way it had been pulling for more than a year. But it was pulling harder now. It could feel the end. It was leaning back on the line like a fisherman who feels the fish go still.

At the other end of it, very far away, west, in a white house with a square hedge, or a grey house with a garden full of white flowers, or somewhere else, somebody was waiting with a cup of tea.

*It's going to take the last of me,* said Watanabe. *When I go. That's what it's waiting for. Not the despair. It's had that for months. The last of me. The bit that's still holding the chair. If it gets that, it gets the seat. Both sides. Light and dark. And then it doesn't need to steal any more. It just is despair. All of it. In the whole city. For good.*

*I know.*

*Stand between me and the thread, Uedera.* The voice was so faint now. *Don't let it have the last of me. That's all I want. I don't want to be saved. I'm too tired to be saved. I just don't want to go down that line.*

Rei stood up.

He did not know exactly what to do. Nobody had told him. Nobody had ever done it. Chen Lian had searched every book in the building and not one of them said *how to stand between a dying holder and the demon of his own seat*. He only knew what he had promised. *Stand between.*

So he stood between.

He stepped round the chair, to the west side of it, and turned his back on the window, and put himself in the way. The thread came out of Watanabe's chest and ran straight into Rei's own chest, just under the heart, and out through his back, and on, through the window, west.

It was like stepping into a river in winter.

[break Cold]

He had felt despair before. Everybody has. On the platform at Ōji, a week ago, he had felt it come in through the broken door and fill him up to the eyes, grey and quiet and calm, and he had nearly stepped off the edge.

That was nothing. That was a cup of it.

This was the river.

It went through him from front to back. Every person in Tokyo who had woken up that morning and thought *what's the point*. Every person who had looked at the tracks too long. Every old man alone in a flat with the television on and nobody to talk to. Every girl in a white room drinking tea and counting her heartbeat. Every mother who had stopped expecting her son to call. Every man who had lost his job and sat in the park all day in his suit so his wife would not know. Every child who had stopped crying because crying had never once brought anyone. All of it. All at once. Through him. A cold grey flood going west, toward a woman with a river stone at her throat who was holding out her hands for it.

He could not breathe.

He went down on one knee. He did not let go. He did not know what he was holding, but he did not let go of it.

*There you are,* said a voice. Soft. From the far end of the thread. From the bottom of a lake. *I knew you'd come back. You're the kind who comes back.*

*Go away,* thought Rei.

*Oh, sweetheart. I'm not here. I'm at home. I'm only at the end of the line.* Kanna-sensei's voice, warm and kind, pouring down the thread toward him like honey. *You're so tired. You've been so tired for so long. And now you're standing in the cold for a man who asked you not to save him. He's going anyway. You can't stop it. Why don't you step out of the water? Just one step. Let it go past. Let me have what's mine, and go home and sleep.*

The grey was coming up his arms. He could see it. On his left hand, the latch hand, curling up from his fingertips like smoke going the wrong way, into the skin, ash-grey and papery.

*Let it go,* said Kanna-sensei tenderly. *It's not yours to carry. Nobody will blame you. Nobody even expects you to. Put it down.*

*Put it down,* said every voice in the river. *Put it down. What's the point. Put it down.*

Rei shut his eyes.

[break What held]

Somebody put a hand on his shoulder.

He did not see who. He felt it. A hand, heavy and warm, very warm, so warm it was almost hot, on his right shoulder, the way you put a hand on a friend's shoulder when you are standing behind him in a crowd. And a voice, low and rough and very close.

Mori's fingers tightened. "*No.* *That is wrong.*"

And heat came down through the hand. Not fire. Not burning. Only heat. The heat of a boiler room under a bath house, the heat of a coal fire in a city in winter, the heat of somebody who has looked at something cruel every day for twenty-one years and said *no, this is wrong* every single time.

Another hand. On his left shoulder. Smaller. Floury. Smelling of mackerel and ginger. Hoshino, saying nothing, only standing there, and something came down through that hand too, small and warm and stupid and bright, like the moment halfway through a good meal when you realise you are smiling and do not know why.

A hand on his back. Shaking. Nakashima, terrified, holding on anyway. And with it something like the feeling of standing at the top of a high building and being so frightened your legs shook and not stepping back from the edge because the view was too beautiful.

A hand on his arm. Okabe, rough-palmed, saying *come on, come on, come on* under her breath like somebody pushing a car up a hill.

A hand on the back of his neck. Sakuma. Warm and soft and certain. Love, which does not ask whether it is worth it.

Kobayashi did not touch him. She knelt on the floor in front of him, in her black suit, and looked him in the face for the first time since he had known her, straight in the eyes, and did not look away, and that was harder than touching for her and he could feel what it cost. Shame, holding its head up.

Park Junho put his switched-off phone into Rei's right hand and closed his fingers round it. *Longing.* The wanting that keeps you walking.

Kasumi dropped off the top of the bookcase and landed in front of the window, between Rei and the west, with his arms out, looking up at the sky through the glass with his mouth open like a child at fireworks. Awe. And the grey river going past him hesitated, very slightly, as if even despair had to stop for a second and look.

Inoue was beside him. He did not remember Inoue moving. Inoue was beside him, holding his elbow, very lightly, and his face was perfectly calm, and he said in Rei's ear, quietly, *Hunger, Uedera. I'm hungry. I'm always hungry. I want breakfast. You want breakfast too. Think about breakfast.*

And Rei laughed. On his knees, in the cold grey river, with the ash coming up his arm. He laughed. He could not help it.

Chen Lian had both hands on the back of Watanabe's chair. She was not looking at Rei. She was looking at the thread, at where it went into his chest and came out of his back, with her eyes enormous behind her glasses, and her mouth moving, saying *oh, oh, oh*, the way she said it when she saw something nobody had ever seen before.

*Wonder,* said her face. *I don't know. I don't know. I want to know.*

And on the laptop on the table, in a dark room in Kyoto, Mio had put her hand flat on her own screen, as if she could reach through it. Grief. Grief which is only love with nowhere to go. Holding the door open so it has somewhere to go.

Eleven of them.

Eleven hands on a door with a broken hinge.

Rei opened his eyes.

[break Between]

He was not in the round room any more.

He was standing in white. The same white. Endless, soft, not bright. And in front of him, close enough to touch, the grey thread, running past him like a road, west, into the distance, toward a far shore he could just see. A grey beach. A grey sea. And on the beach, a long white room with no walls, and cushions, and a low table, and a woman kneeling at it, pouring tea into a cup.

Behind him, the other way, east, the thread ran back to a chair. Just a chair. Old dark wood with a crack across the back. Standing on its own in the white. And in it, a man made of ash, with his eyes still open.

Around him, very far away, a ring of lights. Eleven of them burning bright. Brighter than he had ever seen them. One of them almost out. One of them missing.

And in the middle, the column. Very far away. With a small figure sitting at the bottom of it, knees drawn up, head lifted, watching.

Rei looked at the thread.

He could not cut it. He knew that. It was not his to cut. It was Watanabe's, it was the seat's, it ran from the light side of the chair to the dark side, from the holder to the Shell, and it had always been there, in every holder, for as long as there had been holders. It was what holding *was*. You held it from one end so it could not be held from the other.

But it did not have to run straight.

Rei stepped onto it.

He did not know how he knew to do it. He thought afterwards that perhaps the man at the bottom of the column had told him, without words, the way he had said *not yet* and *go home*. Or perhaps it was Tanizaki, on a step, with a cup held out. Or perhaps it was only the shape of him. Duty. The shape that stands in a doorway at three in the morning and says *no*.

He stepped onto the grey thread with both feet, the way you step onto a rope bridge, and turned to face west, toward the far shore, toward the woman at the table, and planted himself, and stood.

And the last of Watanabe Jun, coming down the thread, small and soft and tired, the last little light of a man who had held the whole city's despair for years so that nobody else would have to, came down the thread toward the far shore and met Rei standing in the way.

And stopped.

And did not go round.

Rei held out his hands. Not to stop it. To catch it. The way you hold out your hands to catch something someone has thrown you without warning. The way your hand comes up.

It was very light. It weighed nothing at all. It was like catching a moth.

*Oh,* said Watanabe, in his hands. Very surprised. *Oh. It doesn't hurt.*

[break The chair]

At the far end of the thread, on the grey beach, Kanna-sensei looked up from her teapot.

She looked at Rei, standing on the line between her and what she had been waiting more than a year to drink, with the last of it held in his cupped hands like water.

For a long moment, her face did not change at all.

Then she smiled. The same smile. Kind. Tender. Infinitely patient.

*Next time,* she said.

And she let go of her end of the thread.

It went slack. All at once. Like a fishing line when the fish gets away. It fell, soft and grey, through the white, and curled up, and lay still, and began very slowly to fade, the way the grey had always faded from Watanabe's fingertips when Rei came to sit with him in the evenings, just a little, just for an hour.

Rei turned round.

Behind him, east, the old dark chair with the crack across the back. Empty now. The man of ash was gone from it. There was nobody there.

Rei walked back along the thread, slowly, carrying what was in his hands, and knelt down in front of the empty chair, and opened his hands over the seat.

*Here,* he thought. *This is yours. Stay here. Hold it. Somebody will come.*

*I know,* said Watanabe, very faintly, settling into the wood like warmth settling into a stone that has been in the sun. *Somebody always does. Isn't that strange. I never believed it.* A pause, very long. *Thank you, Uedera. It's lighter. I can't think why. You'd think it would be heavier, at the end. It isn't.*

*No,* thought Rei.

*Tell Chen I'm sorry about the books I never brought back.*

*I will.*

*Tell Kato he thinks very loudly too. Tell him it's a nice noise.*

*I will.*

*And Uedera.*

*Yes.*

*You'll make a terrible demon.* Something that was almost a laugh, from somewhere very deep in the old wood, very far away. *You keep all your promises. They'll never get anything out of you.*

And then there was nothing in the chair but warmth, and the warmth stayed.

[break Morning]

He woke up on the floor of the round room with eleven people round him and the sun coming up over Ochanomizu through the east window.

His left hand was grey to the wrist. Ash-grey, papery, cold. He looked at it. While he was looking, the grey began very slowly to fade from the fingertips, the way frost fades off a window, and the pink came back underneath.

Gorō was standing on his chest. He had been standing there for a long time, by the look of him. He bent his head and looked at Rei with the blue eye. Then he very gently took the edge of Rei's ear in his beak, and held it, and let go, and did not bite at all.

The sixth chair was empty.

While Rei slept, somebody had carried Watanabe down the stairs and laid him on the long sofa in the reading room under Chen Lian's coat, and closed his eyes. There was nobody in the chair now. There was only the old dark wood with the crack across the back, and on the seat, a dent, the shape of a tired man who had sat there for a long time, and a warmth coming off it that you could feel from across the room, like a stone that has been in the sun all day and gives it back slowly into the night.

Kato was sitting on the floor by Rei's head, with his king in his hand and his face swollen from crying.

"He told me," he said, before Rei could say anything. "At the end. He said I think very loudly too." His voice cracked. "He said it's a nice noise."

Chen Lian was sitting in the doorway of the round room with her knees drawn up and her glasses in her lap, the way she had sat the morning Rei came back from the white.

"The books," said Rei. His voice came out cracked and strange. "He said he's sorry about the books he never brought back."

Chen Lian put her face down on her knees and laughed and cried at the same time for a long time.

Mori was standing at the west window.

The round window that faced west, the one the thread had gone out of. He stood in front of it with his back to the room and his arms folded, looking out over the roofs toward the west, toward Asagaya and Kōenji and everything beyond, where the sun had not yet reached.

"She let go," he said, without turning round. "I felt it. She let go of her end."

Rei pushed himself up onto one elbow. "She said *next time*."

Mori was silent for a long time.

"Then there'll be a next time," he said. He turned round. His face was terrible. Grief and rage and something else, something Rei had not seen in it before, and did not have a name for, and Mori did not seem to have a name for either. "Despair's chair is empty. Light side held. Dark side hungry. She doesn't have it. Because a boy stood on the line and caught a dying man in his hands like a moth." He looked at Rei for a long moment with his coal-coloured eyes. "I've held Rage for twenty-one years. I have never once seen a holder do that. I have never read of one doing it." He unfolded his arms. "Now get up off the floor, Uedera. We're going to go and have that very long, very loud conversation about the thing in your brother. And then we're going to go and find it, and make a plan."

He paused, and held out one big hand to pull Rei up.

"Because I'm not letting you do the next part alone either."
