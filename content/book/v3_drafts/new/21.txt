#title Grey Jacket

In the first week of November Rei went back to the police.

He had not meant to. He was walking past the station on Monday morning, on his way from Nezu, with his knees still humming from the boards, and his feet turned in at the door before he had decided anything. Ono was on the front desk, as he always was, big and cheerful, with a can of coffee and a newspaper folded at the horse racing.

"Kid!" he said. "Look at you. You've got thin. Are you eating? You look like a monk who's been on a diet."

"Is Kumagai in?"

Ono's face changed a little. He picked up the phone.

Kumagai came down himself. That was new. He stood at the bottom of the stairs in his shirt sleeves, with his glasses pushed up on his forehead, and looked at Rei for a moment before he said anything.

"Nothing," he said. "I'm sorry, Uedera. Nothing's crossed my desk. No name. No description. I check every morning." He took his glasses down and folded them. "I said I'd call. I'd have called."

"I know."

"Then why did you come in?"

Rei did not have an answer. He thought it was because he wanted somebody to tell him to his face, in a room with a desk and a clock, that nobody had found anything. So that it was a real fact and not just a quiet phone.

"Thank you," he said, and bowed, and went.

He took the Keihin-Tōhoku south to Tokyo station and walked through the cold bright canyon of Marunouchi to the grey block of the Division, and went in through the glass doors, and stood at the long front desk in the lobby. It was a different young man this time, but the same smile, the same neat hair, the same small white owl on his lapel.

"Uedera Gin," Rei said. "Field Operations. I'm his brother."

The young man typed. He looked at the screen.

"Officer Uedera is on assignment," he said pleasantly. "I'm afraid I can't say more than that. Would you like to leave a message?"

"I left one in August."

"Then I'm sure it's been passed on." The young man smiled. "Is there anything else I can help you with today?"

Rei looked at him for a long time. The young man went on smiling. He was not lying. Rei did not need Kato to tell him that. He believed every word on his screen.

"No," said Rei. "Thank you."

He walked back out into Marunouchi with his hands in his pockets, past the banks and the insurance companies and the men in dark suits walking fast with their phones against their ears, and he felt like a stone that somebody had thrown into a lake, and the lake had closed over it, and that was all.

[break Choi Hana]

On Wednesday Switch put him on a job with Choi Hana.

"She asked for you," said Switch, on the phone, in their flat voice.

"She hates me."

"She asked for you anyway. Make of that what you like." A pause. "It's a shop in Okachimachi. A tea seller. Something's been knocking things off the shelves at closing time and the owner's started sleeping in his car. Hana does the moving. You do the reading. Split it fifty-fifty."

They met at the north exit of Ueno station at four in the afternoon. Hana was leaning against a pillar with her hands in the pocket of her black hoodie and her hood down. In daylight she looked younger than she had at the swimming pool. Nineteen, maybe. Her black hair was cut short and blunt, as if she had done it herself with kitchen scissors, which Rei suspected she had. There was a fresh plaster on the back of her right hand and a yellow bruise going green along her jaw. Her left sleeve was pushed up to the elbow, for once, and the scar ran pale and thick and ridged up the inside of her forearm, from the wrist almost to the bend of the arm.

She saw him look at it. She did not pull the sleeve down.

"Everybody looks," she said. "It's fine. You can look."

"What happened to your hand?"

"Door." She pushed off the pillar. "I wasn't paying attention. I walked into the back of one." She did not say whether she had walked into it or appeared inside it. "Come on. It's this way."

[need ch21_ameyoko | Ameyoko market street under the railway tracks in Ueno in the late afternoon, crowded narrow stalls selling dried fish, sweets, tea and cheap clothes, shouting vendors, a slight young woman in a black hoodie with short blunt hair and a young man in a dark jacket walking through the crowd]

The quickest way to Okachimachi was down Ameyoko. It was the old market street that runs beside the railway tracks between Ueno and Okachimachi, under the iron bridges, and at four on a Wednesday it was packed. Stalls on both sides, so close that the awnings touched. Dried squid and dried scallops in heaps. Bags of sweets and nuts, three for a thousand yen. Cheap trainers. Fake handbags. Spices. Tea. Men in aprons shouting prices in hoarse, cheerful voices, the same price over and over until it became a kind of song. Every few minutes a train went over the iron bridge above and the noise of it came down through everything, and everybody shouted a little louder until it passed.

Hana walked through it fast, sideways, finding gaps in the crowd that were not there until she was in them. Rei followed her and kept treading on people.

Halfway down, by a stall that sold nothing but dried mango, she slowed down and walked next to him for a while without saying anything.

"The scar," she said at last. She did not look at him. She looked at the dried mango. "Everybody asks. Nobody gets told. I've never told anybody. Not Switch. Not anybody." She kicked a flattened paper cup out of her way. "I thought maybe I'd tell you. Because of your brother. He never asked. Two days we worked together, and he looked at it once and never asked."

Rei waited.

"It was the winter I was fifteen," said Hana. "There was a..."

She stopped, because Rei had stopped.

[break The jacket]

He was standing in the middle of Ameyoko with people pushing past him on both sides, and he was looking across the street.

Across the street, at a tea stall. A narrow stall with wooden boxes of loose tea stacked to the roof and paper packets hanging on strings. An old woman in a quilted jacket behind the counter, weighing something on a small brass scale. And in front of the counter, with his back half turned, a man in a grey jacket.

Tall. Long black hair, loose past the jaw. Standing very straight, with his weight on both feet, like an officer.

He was holding a paper packet of tea up to his face, smelling it. Slowly. With his eyes half closed. As if it was the most interesting thing in Tokyo.

Rei knew the jacket. He had bought it. Three years ago, for Gin's birthday, at a shop in Ikebukuro, and Gin had said it was too expensive, and worn it every day since.

He knew the hair. He knew the line of the shoulders. He knew the hand holding the packet, with the small white scar across the knuckle where Gin had cut it on a tin of tuna when Rei was eleven.

It was Gin.

It was Gin, standing at a tea stall in Ameyoko on a Wednesday afternoon, smelling tea.

Rei did not shout. He could not. There was no air in him. He moved.

He went straight across the street, into the crowd, through it, between a man with a cart of cardboard boxes and two girls with bubble tea, and somebody swore at him, and a bag hit him in the ribs, and a train went over the bridge above with a noise like the end of the world. For three seconds he could not see the tea stall at all. For three seconds there was nothing but backs and bags and coats.

When he came out of the crowd at the tea stall, there was nobody in front of the counter.

The old woman in the quilted jacket was putting a paper packet back on its string.

"The man," said Rei. He could hardly speak. "The man who was here. Grey jacket. Long hair. Where did he go?"

The old woman looked at him with mild surprise. "He didn't buy anything," she said. "He smelled every packet on this side, one by one, very politely, and said thank you, and went." She pointed vaguely down the street, toward Okachimachi, where the crowd was thickest. "That way. Strange man. Lovely manners."

Rei ran.

He ran the whole length of Ameyoko to Okachimachi, pushing, looking, turning round in the middle of the street to look back, looking into every shop, every gap, every alley that went off sideways under the tracks. Grey jackets. A hundred grey jackets. None of them his. None of them standing straight. None of them stopping to smell anything.

At the end of the street, by the Okachimachi crossing, he stood on the kerb and turned round and round with the cars going past and the lights changing and people looking at him, and he opened the door in his chest wide, all at once, the way Sōun had told him never to.

The whole street came in. A thousand people. Their tiredness, their shopping, their arguments, their headaches, their small happiness about a cheap pair of shoes. A pickpocket somewhere near the station, nervous. A woman thinking about her mother in hospital. Noise. Noise. Noise.

Nothing that felt like Gin.

Nothing that felt like anything.

He closed the door. It took a long time. When it shut he was sitting on the kerb by the crossing, and Hana was crouched in front of him, and her hand with the plaster on it was holding his wrist, hard.

"Breathe," she was saying. "Breathe. Look at me. Breathe."

He breathed.

"That was your brother," said Hana. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Are you sure?"

"I bought him that jacket."

Hana looked at him for a long moment. Then she let go of his wrist and stood up and looked down the length of Ameyoko, at the crowd, and the stalls, and the trains going over.

"Wait here," she said.

And she was not there.

There was no sound. There was a small cold movement in the air where she had been, like the draught when a door opens in another room. Rei looked round. She was on the roof of the bridge over the tracks, thirty metres away, standing on the iron girder against the grey sky with her hood blowing, looking down the street. Then she was not there. She was on the top of a vending machine at the corner. Then on the balcony of a pachinko parlour across the road. Three, four, five places, faster than he could turn his head, each time looking, each time gone.

Then she was back in front of him on the kerb, breathing hard, with her nose bleeding a little.

"Nothing," she said. She wiped her nose on the back of her hand and looked at the blood without interest. "I'm sorry. He's gone. Nobody in a grey jacket with long hair on any of the streets I can see from up there." She sat down on the kerb next to him. "If he was there, he went somewhere I can't see. Into a building. Into a train." She was quiet for a moment. "Or somewhere else."

They sat on the kerb at the Okachimachi crossing while the lights changed, red, green, red, and the crowd went past them both ways.

"He was smelling tea," said Rei.

"What?"

"At the stall. He was smelling the tea. One packet at a time. With his eyes shut." He looked at his hands. "Gin doesn't care about tea. He drinks whatever's in the pot. He drinks hōjicha because it's cheap and Tanizaki taught him to make it. He's never smelled a packet of tea in his life."

Hana did not say anything.

"He looked happy," said Rei. "That's the thing. He looked like he was having a nice afternoon."

[break The tea shop]

They did the job anyway. It was what Gin would have done. It was what Hana said they were going to do, in a voice that did not leave any room.

The tea shop in Okachimachi was small and old and full of shelves, and the thing knocking things off them at closing time was the owner's late father, who did not like the new owner putting the cheap tea on the shelf where the good tea used to go. Rei read it in four minutes. Hana moved the cheap tea to a lower shelf without touching it, which made the owner sit down on the floor. Nothing fell off anything after that. The owner paid them twenty thousand yen and a box of very good sencha each and said he would sleep in his own bed tonight.

On the platform at Okachimachi, waiting for the train, Hana stood next to him with her hands in her hoodie pocket and her sleeve still pushed up, the scar pale in the platform lights.

"I didn't finish telling you," she said.

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to." She looked at the tracks. "Another time. Not today. Today's yours." The train came in with a rush of warm air. "Your brother's alive, Uedera. That's something. A lot of us never get that."

She got on the train going the other way, and the doors closed, and she did not look back.

That night Rei sat at the kitchen table with Gin's book open in front of him and did not read it. He sat with two cups of tea, one on each side of the table, and looked at the hook by the door.

*Grey jacket. Long hair. Smelling tea with his eyes shut, like it was the first tea in the world.*

He did not tell anybody else for a week. Not Itsuki. Not Haruki. Not Kato. He did not know why. Then he understood that he was afraid of what they would say. That somebody would say *are you sure*. Or worse, that somebody would not.
