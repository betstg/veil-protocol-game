#title The Level Under

When somebody dies in Japan, the Buddhists say, they do not go straight on. For forty-nine days the dead are between. Not here and not anywhere else yet. Travelling. Every seventh day of the forty-nine they stand before a judge, and on the forty-ninth they are told where they will be born next, and they go. The family keeps the ashes at home until then, in a white box on a little altar, with a photograph and an incense burner and a cup of water that is changed every morning. On the forty-ninth day they all go to the temple, and the priest reads, and the box goes into the family grave under the stone, and the black clothes go back in the cupboard. After that, people say, it is easier. The dead have somewhere to be.

Shimizu's forty-ninth day was the fourth of October, a Tuesday. It rained.

The temple was in Kawaguchi, just over the river from Akabane, in Saitama. A small grey Jōdo temple between a car dealer and a block of flats, with a gravel yard and a single ginkgo tree that had not yet begun to go yellow, and a stone basin at the gate with a bamboo dipper laid across it. Her family had been parishioners there for four generations. Rei had not known that. He had not known she came from Kawaguchi, or that she had a younger brother who drove a lorry, or that her mother was alive.

Her mother was very small. Smaller than Shimizu, and bent forward at the waist from something in her back, and she sat on a folding chair at the front of the hall instead of kneeling, because of her knee. She wore black, as everybody did, with a string of white prayer beads round her left hand. When the Division people came in, in their black suits, in a line, with the rain on their shoulders, she turned round in her chair and looked at every one of them, slowly, as Shimizu used to look at a room. Then she turned back and looked at the photograph on the altar.

It was not a Division photograph. It was Shimizu at perhaps twenty-five, at somebody's wedding, in a pale blue dress, laughing at something to one side of the camera with her hand half up to her mouth. Rei had never seen her laugh like that. He did not think anybody from the sixth floor had.

The priest read for forty minutes. The incense went up in two thin grey threads in the cold air of the hall. Kato knelt beside Rei in the second row with his hands on his thighs and his eyes on the photograph and did not move once in forty minutes. When it was his turn to go up and put a pinch of incense on the coals, he did it three times, very carefully, as you are meant to, and bowed to the photograph, and then he turned and bowed to the small old woman on the folding chair, very low, for a long time.

She looked up at him.

"You're the telepath," she said. Her voice was dry and quiet and exact. "Kato-san. She wrote to me about you. Every month. She said you were too loud and she should have told you that you weren't." She looked at him for a moment longer. "She said she was going to tell you."

"She told me," said Kato.

The old woman nodded. "Good," she said, and looked back at the photograph.

Afterwards, in the gravel yard, under the ginkgo, in the rain, with everybody standing about in black under black umbrellas not knowing when it was polite to go, Kato took an envelope out of his inside pocket and gave it to Rei.

It was grey. Division stock. Addressed in typescript to *Shimizu, Field Operations, Sixth Floor*. It had been opened along the top with a paper knife.

"It came on Friday," said Kato. "To her desk. Nobody's cleared her desk. I've been opening her post." He looked at the ginkgo. "Somebody has to. She'd hate it piling up."

Rei took the letter out.

*Your request of 21 June to visit TENDO Akira, held at Level B5 under Section 14, has been reviewed. A single visit of thirty minutes is approved for the applicant and one accompanying officer, to take place before 31 October. Food may be brought and will be inspected. Please present this letter at the B4 desk.*

At the bottom, under the stamp, somebody had written by hand in pencil, very small, *Third application. Sorry it took so long. Ogura.*

"She wrote three times," said Kato. "June, July and August. The last one's dated the eleventh. Five days before." He held his umbrella a little further over Rei, as the rain came on harder. "I rang B4 this morning. I asked if the applicant could be replaced. They said the applicant could not be replaced. They said the accompanying officer could go, and could bring one person, if the person was cleared." He looked at Rei. "You're cleared. I checked that too."

[break B5]

They went on the Thursday, at ten in the morning, with a paper bag from the bakery by Tokyo Station.

The Division building had five floors under the street. Most people who worked there had been to three of them. The first was the car park. The second was Containment and Reintegration, the long polished corridor with the grey doors and the windows with wire in them, where Itsuki had spent ninety days. The third was the evidence archive, where new officers were sent as a punishment and Rei had once spent a fortnight with a radio. The fourth was a floor of locked rooms and pipes and humming machinery, and a desk.

There was no lift to the fifth.

The man at the B4 desk was in his sixties, in a grey uniform with no name on it, with reading glasses on a cord round his neck. He read the letter twice, slowly. He looked at their passes. He opened a large ledger bound in green cloth and found a page and ran his finger down it, and wrote their names in the next empty line in pencil, in square careful characters, with the date and the time, and turned the ledger round for each of them to sign. Then he opened the paper bag from the bakery and took the buns out one at a time, and pressed each one gently between his finger and thumb, and broke the last one in half and looked inside it, and put them back.

"Red bean," he said.

"He likes red bean," said Kato.

The man looked at him over his glasses. "He does," he said, and something in his face changed, very slightly, and went back. He took a ring of keys off his belt. "Thirty minutes. I'll be outside the door. You don't touch him, you don't pass him anything except the food, and if I say time, it's time." He unlocked a steel door behind the desk. "Mind the stairs. They're steep. They were built in 1952 by men who didn't expect anybody to come back up them."

The stairs were concrete, painted grey, with a steel rail on one side, and they went down a long way. At the bottom there was a short corridor, very clean, very warm, lit by strip lights behind wire. Six doors. No windows in any of them. Round each door, on the wall, the same black seals as in Containment, only more of them, and older, and painted directly onto the concrete instead of on paper, so that they could not be peeled. The air smelled of nothing at all. Not disinfectant. Not the sweet burnt smell. Nothing. Somebody had made very sure of that.

The man unlocked the third door and stood aside.

[break Tendo]

The room was the size of a small hotel room, and white, and had a bed and a table and two chairs and a shelf of paperback books, and a toilet behind a half wall. On the ceiling there was a circle of black seals the size of a dining table. On the table there was a jigsaw puzzle of a Hokusai wave, about two thirds done.

Tendo was sitting at the table, doing it.

He had lost weight. A great deal of it. He had been a big man, broad and easy and loose, and now his grey sweatshirt hung off his shoulders and his wrists came out of the cuffs like a boy's. His hair had been cut very short, by somebody who did not care how it looked. But when he looked up and saw who was in the doorway, his face did what it had always done. It opened all at once, like a shop with the shutters going up.

"Kato," he said. "*Kato.*" And then, past him, "And the little brother. Look at you. Somebody's let you wear a proper tie."

Then he looked behind them, at the door. At the man in the grey uniform. At the empty corridor.

Rei watched him look for her, and not find her, and understand. It took about two seconds. His face did not close. It went on being open, and the understanding went into it, and stayed.

"Oh," said Tendo. Very quietly. "Oh. When?"

"The sixteenth of August," said Kato. "At night. In a freight yard in Kita."

Tendo put down the piece of puzzle he was holding. He put it down very carefully, by the edge of the board, the right way up.

"They don't tell us anything down here," he said. "There's no radio. There's no papers. Ogura said there'd been something, in the summer. She wouldn't say what. She said she wasn't allowed." He looked at the jigsaw. "I thought she'd stopped writing. I thought she'd given up on me. I thought, fair enough." He breathed out. "Was she... Did she..."

"She saved my life," said Kato. "She walked into a thing that was eating everybody's noise, to get to the middle of it, because it was going for me. She did it on purpose. She'd read it and she knew where to put the paper and she knew she'd have to be close." He sat down in the chair opposite Tendo without being asked. "She wrote to come and see you three times. This is the third letter. They only said yes on Friday. I came instead."

Tendo looked at the grey letter in Kato's hand for a long time.

"She'd have hated this," he said at last. "This room. She'd have walked in and told me the puzzle was too easy and the shelf wasn't straight." His voice went. He got it back. "She'd have been right. It isn't."

Kato put the paper bag on the table.

Tendo looked at it. At the bakery's name printed on the side in brown.

"Monday," he said.

"It's Thursday," said Kato. "They're from Monday's bakery, though."

Tendo laughed. It was not much of a laugh. He opened the bag and looked in, and took out a red bean bun, and held it in his big thin hand, and did not eat it.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Kato. I never got to say. I'm sorry. I didn't mean... I went to that place on my own because I wanted to be the one. That's all it was. I wanted to walk in with the answer for once. I wanted Shimizu to look at me like she looked at you when you heard something. And the thing in there said *I can help you hear everything*, and I said yes before it had finished the sentence." He turned the bun over. "I didn't think about anybody else at all. Not her. Not you. Not the dog." He looked up. "Is the dog...?"

"The dog's fine," said Rei. "The dog's better than fine. He lives in the Director's office now. He's got a velvet bed."

Tendo stared at him.

"Adachi brushes him," said Rei. "Every morning. I went in once and he said, *just a moment, I'm finishing here*, and went on brushing, and talked to him the whole time. He calls him *you rascal*." He hesitated. "He was in the battle. In August. He took a man down who was going for a little girl. On three legs and a cast."

Tendo put his face in his hands.

He sat like that for a while, with the bun in one of them, and his shoulders shaking. The man in the grey uniform outside the door looked at the ceiling. Kato did not look anywhere. He sat with his hands on his knees and waited.

When Tendo took his hands away from his face he looked at Kato, straight, and asked the question Rei had known he would ask from the moment he looked up from the puzzle.

"Is it still in me?"

Kato did not answer straight away.

"You can hear now," said Tendo. "Properly. She told me that much, at least. Through the hatch. Something about a hundred people and one word, and a telepath. She wouldn't say where. I knew it was you." He leaned forward. "So listen. Please. I can't tell. I can't tell what's me any more. Some mornings I wake up and I want something and I don't know if I want it. Listen, and tell me. How much of it's still there?"

Kato looked at him.

Rei felt him do it. He did not need the empty frame for that any more. He had sat beside Kato for a year and he knew what Kato looked like when he was listening on purpose, the small stillness that came down over his face, like a man in a library turning a page very quietly so as not to disturb anyone. Kato listened to Tendo for a long time. A minute. More. Outside, the man in the grey uniform shifted his feet.

"It's there," said Kato at last. "It's small. It's asleep. It's down at the very bottom, under everything, curled up, and the seals on the ceiling are lying on it like a stone on a lid." He did not look away. "It hums. Very faint. I can hear it if I go looking. I don't think you can."

Tendo did not move.

"And over it," said Kato, "there's you. All the rest. You're thinking about Shimizu on four flights of stairs in Kōtō with her ankle broken, and you singing. You're thinking about the dog in a velvet bed and it's making you laugh and you're trying not to. You're thinking you'd like to eat this bun and you're worried that if you do you'll cry again and we'll see." He breathed. "You're very loud, Tendo. You always were. It's mostly you."

Tendo ate the bun.

He cried while he ate it, quite openly, with crumbs on his sweatshirt, and neither of them looked away. When it was finished he folded the empty paper square from the bottom of it into a very small neat square, as Kato did, and put it on the corner of the jigsaw.

"They're moving me," he said. "In January. Out of here. To a proper prison. Fuchū, they think. There's going to be a trial. Not a real one, not in a real court. The Division has its own. They've got a word for what I did that sounds like terrorism, and it's the right word." He looked at the white wall. "I'm going to say I did it. I did. Ogura says I might get eight years. She says there's a section in Fuchū for people like me, with seals on the cells, and a garden."

"I'll come," said Kato.

"You don't have to."

"I know." Kato took a piece of puzzle off the edge of the board, a bit of grey-blue sky, and turned it round, and fitted it into the top right-hand corner without looking. "She wrote three times. I'll write four."

The man outside the door said, "Time."

[break The ninth door]

They went back up the steep stairs without speaking, and signed the green ledger again, and the man at the B4 desk wrote the time beside their names and closed the book.

On the way up, the lift stopped at B2 on its own and the doors opened on the long polished corridor of Containment. Nobody got in. Rei looked down the corridor anyway, and saw a heavy man in a dark jacket sitting on a folding chair outside the ninth door on the left, with a canvas bag at his feet and a newspaper on his knee.

"Go on," said Kato. "I'll tell Okada you're on your way. I need to sit on the roof for a bit."

Rei got out.

Tanaka looked up from the newspaper as he came down the corridor. He did not seem surprised. He did not seem to be surprised by anything. He folded the paper and moved his bag with his foot, so that there was room to stand beside him at the little window.

"Uedera," he said. "She's awake. She's been awake since six. She won't talk to me. That's normal. I talk to her." He looked at Rei's face. "You've been to B5."

"Yes."

"Tendo." Tanaka nodded slowly. "I put my hand on him in July. Through the hatch. They let me, once. It's small. He'll be all right, if they leave him alone and somebody brings him food." He looked at the little window. "This one's different."

Rei looked through the glass.

Room Nine was as it had been. The white bed, the white chair, the white table. The walls covered in white paper written over in black. The square of iron bars set into the floor round the bed. Itsuki's charcoal had been scrubbed off the walls months ago, and somebody had repainted the skirting board, but there was still a small grey smudge on the inside of the door frame, at shoulder height, where a hand had rested a great many times.

Fukai Rinka was sitting on the edge of the bed with her hands folded in her lap.

She was wearing the grey cotton clothes they gave everybody in Reintegration, too big for her. Her hair was down. Without the low neat bun and the pale cardigan buttoned to the throat she looked older, and smaller, and much more ordinary. A woman of about forty with tired eyes and a long thin face, sitting on a hospital bed in a basement, looking at the wall.

"She's eating," said Tanaka. "That's new. Since last week. The plum ones. She doesn't like the salmon."

"Has she said anything?"

"She said *you're not her* on the first day," said Tanaka. "To me. And then nothing for five weeks." He folded his arms. "She thinks somebody killed her god. She thinks the whole world was a big cold room with nobody in it, and then for four years a kind voice came and sat with her in it, and then some people in a freight yard took it away and gave her back her own head. And her own head's got nothing in it but four years of being looked after, and before that a great deal of grief." He was quiet. "I don't blame her for not wanting it."

Rei looked at the woman on the bed.

He had been afraid of her for a year. Not of her. Of what had looked out of her face. The kind eyes. The teapot. The voice in the weeds. He looked for it now, in the woman on the bed, and could not find it anywhere. There was nobody in her but her.

He had the stone in his pocket. He had carried it since August. Okada had picked up the two halves from the stones by the river that morning and put them in an evidence bag, and then, a week later, without a word, had taken them out of the evidence bag and given them to Rei in an envelope, and Rei had not known what to do with them, and had carried them.

"Can I pass her something?" he said.

Tanaka looked at the envelope. He held out his hand, and Rei gave it to him, and Tanaka opened it and looked inside, and turned the two halves of grey stone over on his palm, one at a time, with his eyes half shut. Rei had seen Moriya do that. Reading.

"It's empty," said Tanaka. "It's just a stone." He gave it back. "Yes. Through the slot. Tell her what it is."

Rei knelt down on the polished floor in front of the ninth door, as he had knelt there on a Thursday in February with a crushed slice of cake, and pressed the grey button, and the intercom clicked and hissed.

"Fukai-san," he said.

On the bed, the woman did not move.

"It's Uedera Rei. From the beach." He swallowed. "I brought you your stone. The one on the cord. It broke. In August. It's in two pieces. I thought you'd want it."

He pushed the envelope through the slot, and let go of it, and heard it fall on the other side.

For a long time nothing happened. Then Rinka got up off the bed, slowly, as people get up who have not got up much for a long time, and came across the white floor in her grey clothes and her bare feet, and bent down, and picked up the envelope, and opened it, and tipped the two halves of the stone out into her hand.

She looked at them.

She fitted them back together, along the white band, so that it was one stone again with a line through it. She held it closed in her fist.

Then she looked up, through the little window, at Rei.

"She said it would never break," she said. Her voice came thin and crackly through the speaker. Not the voice from the weeds. Her own, unused. "She said as long as I wore it she was with me."

"I know."

"She lied."

Rei did not say anything.

Rinka stood with the stone shut in her fist, looking at him. Her eyes were brown, and small, and very wet, and full of something that was nearly hatred and was too tired to be.

"You were in her," she said. "At the end. On the beach. When he was putting her back. I felt you. All of you were in there. Everyone she ever took from." She looked at the stone. "I wasn't. I gave her everything. Four years. She took all of it. And when he put the pieces back, nothing came back to me." Her voice cracked. "There wasn't anything of mine to put back. I'd already given it to her. On purpose. I wanted to."

"Then it's still yours," said Rei.

She looked at him.

"What you gave," said Rei. "You didn't lose it. You gave it. That's different. That's the only thing she never understood." He did not know where it was coming from. He thought perhaps from a man on a step in a faded blue jinbei, a long time ago, holding out a cup. "You can give it to somebody else. If you want. Not now. Not for ages. But it's still yours to give."

Rinka stood very still.

Then she turned away from the window, without a word, and went back to the bed, and sat down on the edge of it with her hands folded in her lap and the stone shut inside them.

The intercom clicked off.

Rei stood up. His knees hurt. Tanaka had unfolded his newspaper again.

"That was good," said Tanaka, not looking up. "She'll be angry with you for a month. Then she'll ask for you." He turned a page. "I'll be here. I'll bring the plum ones."

[break The Sigil Room]

Itsuki was on the eleventh floor.

He was there most afternoons now. He was not in the Division and never would be. He had said so, at length, to anybody who asked, and to several people who had not. But the head of the Kuroda house had business with the Division every week, there were papers to sign, and Itsuki signed them in the morning and then took the lift up to the Sigil Room and stayed until it got dark, and nobody had yet found a form that said he could not.

The Sigil Room was as it had always been. Long and narrow and smelling of ink and old paper and green tea. Every wall covered from floor to ceiling in sheets of paper painted with black circles and lines and small careful writing. The paper lamp over the chair. The long table under the window, crowded with pots of ink and brushes in jars and stacks of grey paper weighted down with stones.

Uruma was at the far end of the table, long and thin with his shaved head and the silver in his ears, sitting back in his chair with his feet up on the windowsill and his arms folded. Itsuki was standing at the near end, bent over a single sheet of paper, with a brush in his hand and black ink up to the wrist.

They were arguing.

"It's too pretty," Uruma was saying. "It's a seal. It's meant to hold."

"It does hold." Itsuki did not look up. "Look at it. The outer ring holds. All of that's yours. I haven't touched it. I've only done the middle."

"The middle's the bit that matters."

"The middle's the bit he looks at," said Itsuki. "Every day. For eight years. Lying on his back on a bed in Fuchū looking up at the ceiling. That's what the middle's for." He straightened up and saw Rei in the doorway. His face changed. "You went."

"We went."

Itsuki put the brush down. "How is he?"

"Thin," said Rei. "Doing a jigsaw. He ate a bun." He came in and looked at the paper on the table. "Kato listened. He says it's small. He says it's mostly Tendo."

Itsuki let out a long breath and looked down at the sheet in front of him.

It was a seal. Rei could read enough of Uruma's alphabet now to see that. An outer ring of black characters, close and careful and tight, the same pattern as on the ceiling of the room in B5. A lock. It said *stay down* and *stay asleep* and *do not wake*, over and over, in three different scripts.

And inside the ring, where on every other seal Rei had ever seen there was another ring, and another, tighter and tighter, down to a single hard black point, there was a picture.

It was very simple. A few strokes of the brush. A dog, asleep, curled nose to tail, with one back leg stuck out at an angle as if it had once been in a cast. And round the dog, very faint, so faint that you had to tilt your head to see it, the lines of the seal went on, but bent, curved round the shape of the sleeping animal instead of cutting across it, so that the lock and the dog were the same lines, and you could not tell where one ended.

"It's for the ceiling," said Itsuki. "In Fuchū. When they move him. The one he's got now is just a lock. It holds the thing down, and it holds him down too. That's why he can't tell what's him." He touched the edge of the paper. "This one holds the thing. And it lets him look at the dog."

Uruma took his feet off the windowsill.

He got up and came down the length of the table, slowly, with his long hands in his pockets, and stood beside Rei and looked at the paper for a long time. He did not say anything. He bent closer. He looked at the place where the black ring of the lock went round the dog's back leg and became the line of the leg.

"I've been doing this for twenty-two years," he said at last. "I was taught by a man who did it for fifty. Every seal on these walls." He tipped his head at them. "Every one of them is a lock or a knife. That's what we were taught they were. You make a lock to keep something in and a knife to cut something out. That's all a seal is. It's how we've always thought of them."

He straightened up.

"Your friend here," he said to Rei, "came in here in the spring, thin as a rake from the basement, and looked at my walls and said, *who painted these, they're beautiful.*" He shook his head. "Nobody had ever said that. In twenty-two years. Not once. I thought he was being rude."

"I wasn't," said Itsuki.

"I know." Uruma looked at the sleeping dog. "It'll hold. I'll put my name on it with yours. It'll hold better than mine, probably, because he'll want it to." He went back to his chair and sat down and put his feet up again. "Do the ears again. They're wrong."

"They're not wrong. That's how his ears go."

"They're wrong," said Uruma. "Shikoku ears stand up. Ask the Director. He's got pictures."

Itsuki picked up the brush.

Rei sat down on the stool by the wall and watched him do the ears again. Outside the window the rain had stopped and the light was going over Marunouchi, orange and long, and down in the street the office workers were coming out of the towers in their dark coats, and the paper lamp over the chair came on by itself as it always did at that hour, and Itsuki did not notice, bent over the table with ink to the wrist and his tongue between his teeth, getting the ears right.
