#title The Last Cup

On Saturday the twenty-fifth of July, Tanizaki set out four cups.

He had always set out four. Rei had noticed it the very first morning, eleven months ago, kneeling at the low table with his heart going and his mouth full of everything at once. A tray, a teapot, four cups. Never one. Never two. He had never asked why. He had learned, in that house, that you did not ask things. You waited, and drank your tea, and sometimes, after a long time, the answer came and sat down beside you.

That night it was very hot. The cicadas had not stopped at sunset. They went on and on in the fig tree, shrill and dry, as if they were afraid of what would happen if they stopped. The sliding doors onto the garden were open. The long grass stood still. Not a leaf moved. The stone lantern was swallowed in ivy, and on top of it, in the last light, Gorō sat with his feathers flat and his head turning, turning, toward the lane.

Rei knelt at the table with his hands on his knees.

Tanizaki was reading the newspaper.

He spoke without looking up. "You're nervous."

Rei looked down at his hands. "Yes."

"Good." Tanizaki turned a page. "So was he. The first time he came to this house with something wearing him." He folded the page back. "That was in October. He sat where you are sitting. He did not drink. The thing that was wearing him drank both cups and asked for a third, and I said no." He read for a moment. "It was very polite about it."

Rei stared at him.

"You knew," he said. "In October. You knew he was... You knew where he was. And you didn't..."

"I knew what he was." Tanizaki did not look up from the paper. "I did not know where. It does not stay. It comes and drinks tea and goes." He turned another page. "You were not ready. You were still in a hurry. If I had told you in October that your brother had sat at this table with a demon's hands round a cup, you would have run out of that door and gone looking for him in every alley in Kita-ku, and you would have found something else first."

Rei could not say anything for a long time.

At last he looked at the tray. "Is that why there are four cups?"

Tanizaki put the newspaper down.

He looked at the tray. At the four cups. White, plain, a little chipped, the same ones as always.

"One for me," he said. "One for whoever comes. One for whoever they bring with them." He touched the fourth cup with one finger. "And one for whatever is following them."

[break After dark]

It got dark at a quarter past seven. The cicadas went on.

At eight, a car went slowly past the end of the lane and did not stop. Rei felt it go by the way you feel a draught under a door. Kato. Round the corner, by the little shrine with the two stone foxes, with the engine off and the windows down and Itsuki in the passenger seat with a jar of iron filings between his knees. Further down, on the corner of the old shopping street, under a dead street lamp, a broad shape sitting on a low wall with an old dog lying at its feet.

*I can hear you,* said Kato, very faint, inside his head. *You're all right. You're doing fine. Nothing yet.*

At half past eight, Tanizaki poured the first cup.

At nine, he poured the second.

At half past nine, Gorō stood up on the stone lantern and opened his wings, and closed them, and opened them again. Not flying. Only spreading, slowly, black and white, like someone opening a coat to show what was underneath.

The cicadas stopped.

All of them. All at once. As if someone had turned off a switch. The silence came down over the garden like a sheet over furniture in an empty house.

Tanizaki looked up from his newspaper. He did not look at the lane. He looked at Rei.

He laid the newspaper flat on the table and smoothed it with one palm. "Sit very still."

[break The van]

It came up the lane with its lights off. A white van with no markings, the kind that delivered dry cleaning or flowers. It stopped outside the gate. Its engine ticked as it cooled.

Three people got out of it. Two men and a woman, in dark suits, with Division lanyards round their necks and something in their hands that Rei could not see clearly in the dark. Long strips of paper. White. With black writing on them.

*Rei,* said Kato in his head, sharp and frightened. *That's not us. That's not field. Those are Marunouchi lanyards. That's Public Confidence. Who told Public Confidence?*

Rei did not answer. He was watching the woman at the gate.

She took one of the strips of paper and pressed it flat against the gatepost. The writing on it was very neat. Rei could read it now, with Uruma's alphabet in his head. Not a ward. Not exactly. A ward was a sentence. This was a single word, written over and over, perfectly, the same each time, like a stamp.

*Closed.*

The gatepost hissed.

Rei felt it in his teeth. Something in the house, something old and quiet and woven through the long grass and the fig tree and the ivy and the boards of the floor, forty years of Tanizaki's careful, patient, unhurried writing, *not here, not this, not yet*, flinched like a man struck across the face.

"They're sealing it," said Rei. His voice was strange. "They're sealing everything. They don't know what's a door and what's a wall. They're just closing all of it."

Tanizaki folded his newspaper in half.

"Yes," he said. "That is what that woman does." He folded it again. "She closes things. She thinks it is the same as keeping them safe."

The second strip went on the fence. The third on the stone lantern, right under Gorō's feet, and Gorō screamed and flew up into the fig tree. The fourth on the frame of the open door, three metres from where they were kneeling. The hiss this time was loud, like water on a hot stove. The air in the room went thin. Rei could feel the house's protection coming apart round them, thread by thread, the way a sweater comes apart when someone pulls one loose end.

And behind the van, at the end of the lane, under the dead street lamp, in the dark, there were people standing.

Ten. Fifteen. More. Standing very still, in a loose crowd, facing the house. Old and young. A woman with a handkerchief. A man in a crumpled work shirt. Two university students holding hands. Each one of them holding a single white chrysanthemum.

Their eyes were black.

*Oh no,* said Kato, in Rei's head. *Oh no oh no oh no. Majima. Majima's getting up. Itsuki, the iron, get the iron...*

[break The gate]

She came up the lane last, through the crowd, and they parted for her the way water parts for a boat.

She wore a soft white summer dress and her feet were bare on the hot tarmac. The river stone swung at her throat. She walked past the white van without looking at it. The three officers from Public Confidence turned to look at her, and the woman with the strips of paper opened her mouth to say something official, and Kanna-sensei looked at her, kindly, for one second.

The woman sat down in the road.

She did not fall. She sat. Slowly, like someone whose legs have just decided they are too tired. She sat in the middle of the lane with her strips of paper in her lap and began to cry, quietly, with her face perfectly still. The two men stood beside her and did nothing at all. Their faces were empty.

Kanna-sensei came to the gate. The gatepost with its strip of white paper. *Closed.*

She put out one finger and touched the paper, and it went grey, and crumbled, and blew away like ash.

She did not come through.

"Seiichi," she said. She said it warmly. The way you say the name of an old neighbour you have not seen for a long time. "It's been forty years."

Tanizaki got up.

He got up slowly, the way he always did, with one hand on the edge of the low table. He was not a big man. In his faded blue jinbei, in the yellow lamplight, he looked very thin and very old. He did not look at Rei. He picked up the teapot, and the fourth cup, the one for whatever was following, and he walked out of the open door onto the step, and sat down on it, cross-legged, facing the garden and the gate, and set the cup down on the boards beside him and poured.

He looked at her over the rim of the cup. "Forty-one. You never could count."

[break What she did]

Rei did not remember getting to the door.

He was in the doorway, behind Tanizaki, with iron in his fist and the latch on his wrist so cold it felt like it was cutting him, and he could not go any further. He could not. There was something in the doorway like glass. Like the surface of water. Tanizaki's back, very thin under the blue cotton, and beyond it the garden, and beyond the garden the gate, and the kind woman at the gate with the black eyes, and he could not go one step further than the old man's shoulders.

"Stay inside," said Tanizaki, without turning round. Quite calm. "Drink your tea."

Rei pressed both palms against the nothing in the doorway. "*Tanizaki-san*..."

"Rei." The old man did not raise his voice. He never had. "I have been sitting on this step for forty-one years so that people like you could stay inside and drink tea. Don't make it a waste."

Kanna-sensei smiled at him across the garden.

"He's not the one I came for tonight," she said. "You know that. I came for the other one. The old thing. It was coming here. It owes me, and it was coming here to drink your tea and make friends with a boy, and I couldn't have that." She tilted her head. "Where is it, Seiichi?"

Tanizaki drank from his cup.

He set the cup down on the boards with a small click. "Not here."

He reached out, without looking, to the windowsill beside the door. On the windowsill, Rei saw now, there was a third cup. One of the four. Not on the tray. Set apart. Upside down.

He had not seen Tanizaki put it there. He did not know when it had been done.

Kanna-sensei looked at the upside-down cup for a long moment. Her smile did not change.

She laughed, very softly, and put her hand to the river stone. "Ah. Ah. You told it not to come."

"I told it there would be no tea tonight." Tanizaki picked his cup up again. "It has always had good manners. It listened."

Kanna-sensei shook her head at him, the way you shake your head at a child who has hidden the biscuits. "That was very rude of you."

And she came through the gate.

[break The step]

The gate did not stop her. Nothing in the garden stopped her. Forty-one years of wards had been half sealed shut by a woman with strips of paper and a fountain pen, and what was left of them parted for her the way the crowd had parted, the way water parts for a boat. She walked up the path through the long grass, barefoot, past the fig tree, past the stone lantern with its white strip, and the grass did not bend under her feet.

She stopped at the bottom of the step.

Tanizaki did not get up.

"You've grown old," she said, looking down at him with great tenderness. "You used to be so beautiful. Do you remember? The first time. You were nineteen. You were so sure of everything."

Tanizaki did not move his eyes from hers. "I remember."

"And you've been so alone. All this time. Forty-one years on a step. Pouring tea for children who leave. Nobody to pour it for you." She knelt down, on the path, in front of him, so that their faces were level. She put out one hand. Very slowly. And laid it on his cheek. "Let me show you."

Rei saw it happen.

He saw it through the glass, through the water, from the doorway, and he would see it for the rest of his life, on the sofa, in the dark, at 3:47 in the morning. He saw the black come out of her hand into the old man's face. Not like smoke. Like ink dropped into a glass of water, curling, spreading, slow and soft. He saw it go into the lines round Tanizaki's eyes and his mouth. He saw Tanizaki's whole face open, just for a moment, open the way a door opens onto a room you have not been into for a very long time. A room with something in it that he had carried for forty-one years and had never once put down, and had never told anyone, and never would.

Rei never found out what it was. Nobody ever did.

Tanizaki's hands closed on his knees.

Then he lifted one of them, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal, and picked up the fourth cup from the boards beside him, the one he had poured for whatever was following, and held it out to her.

"Here," he said. His voice was very quiet and perfectly steady. "You came all this way. Have some tea."

Kanna-sensei looked at the cup.

For one second, for one single second, something moved behind the black eyes. Rei saw it. Something small and quick, very far down, like a hand that reaches out by old habit for a thing it has not been offered in a very long time, and stops.

Then it was gone.

Kanna-sensei took her hand away from his face.

She did not take the cup.

[break Majima]

The crowd came through the gate.

All of them. All at once. Black-eyed, silent, with their white flowers held in front of them like candles. Through the broken gate and over the fence and up through the long grass, toward the step, toward the doorway, toward Rei.

And the first of them, a big man in a work shirt, stopped dead in the middle of the garden as if he had walked into a wall, and folded up, and lay down in the grass and did not move.

Majima came over the fence.

He did not hurry. He never hurried. He came over the fence in one movement and landed in the long grass with his weight forward and his hands open, and the old dog came over behind him, stiff and silent and scarred, and stood at his heel with its lips drawn back from yellow teeth. The second of the hollow people reached for Majima, and Majima touched him. Just touched him, with two fingers, on the side of the head. Rei felt the air buckle round the touch, a pressure arriving from nowhere into one single point. The man went down without a sound.

"Kid," said Majima, very calm, not looking round. "Inside. Now."

Behind the house, at the back, there was a crash of breaking wood, and a shout, and a sound like a door slamming shut in another flat, and Itsuki's voice, high and furious, swearing. And then a hole in the air in the back room, round and dark and shining at the edges like wet ink, and Kato's hand coming out of it, and grabbing Rei's arm, and pulling.

"*Not without him,*" said Rei, and pulled back.

He turned to the step.

Tanizaki was still sitting on it. Cross-legged. Facing the garden. The fourth cup was in his hand, still held out, toward the place where Kanna-sensei had been.

She was gone.

Rei looked for her. The garden was full of hollow people and Majima moving through them like a man moving through long grass, and the old dog, and the flowers trampled into the path. But Kanna-sensei was not there. She was walking away down the lane in her white dress, past the van, past the woman sitting crying in the road. She did not look back.

Rei went down on his knees on the boards beside Tanizaki.

"Tanizaki-san," he said. "Tanizaki-san. Come on. Come inside. We're going. Itsuki's got a gate open, we're going."

Tanizaki did not look at him.

He was looking at the garden. At the long grass and the weeds with their tiny yellow flowers, crushed now. At the fig tree. At the stone lantern in its ivy. His face was calm. Brown and lined and calm, exactly as always. Only the eyes had changed. They were looking at something a very long way off, further than the garden, further than the lane.

His lips barely moved. "Rei."

Rei took hold of the thin blue sleeve. "I'm here."

"The tea." He lowered the cup, very slowly, and set it down on the step beside him. "It has gone cold."

Rei pulled at the sleeve, gently, and it did not come. "I'll make more. I'll make more, it's fine, come inside..."

Tanizaki reached out, without looking, and found the newspaper where it had fallen on the boards. He picked it up. He folded it in half. Neatly, the edges together, the way he always did when he had finished with it.

He folded it in half again.

Then he put it down on his knee, and put his hand flat on top of it, and was still.

[break The cup]

Afterwards there were a lot of people.

Okada came, with a Division field team in two cars and her three notebooks and her face like stone, because Kato had called her from the car the moment the van turned into the lane. She walked straight past the hollow people lying in the grass and the Public Confidence officers sitting in the road and the old dog licking Majima's bleeding knuckles, and went up to the man from Public Confidence who was still standing, and took the lanyard off his neck, and read it, and put it in her pocket.

She held the lanyard up between two fingers. "Who signed your order."

He told her. He did not seem able not to.

Okada wrote it down in the first of her three notebooks. Then she came up the path and stopped at the bottom of the step and looked at Tanizaki for a long time, and closed her notebook, and put the rubber band back round all three, very slowly, and did not open them again that night.

Rei did not move from the boards. He sat beside Tanizaki with his knees up and his arms round them, the way the man at the bottom of the white light sat, and did not let anybody move him. Itsuki tried. Kato tried. Gorō came down out of the fig tree and stood on the step beside him and pressed against his side, and did not bite his ear, and did not make a sound.

At some point, very late, he looked up at the windowsill.

The third cup was still there. Upside down. Set apart. A message in a language older than Uruma's alphabet, older than the Grid, older than the city. *There is no tea tonight. Do not come.*

And on the wall of the garden, beside the broken gate, sitting very upright in the light from the open door with his heavy head and his still yellow eyes, there was a big black cat.

Nobody had brought him. Nobody had asked him. He had come across half of Kita-ku on his own, in the dark, from a locked room on the fourth floor of the Division, and nobody ever found out how.

Kuga looked at Rei for a long time.

Then he looked at the old man on the step, with his newspaper folded on his knee.

And he bowed his head, very low, the way Rei bowed to him. And stayed like that, on the wall, in the dark, until the morning.
