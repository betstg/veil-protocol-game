#title Itsuki

Kuroda Itsuki had not walked through the front gate of his father's house in eight months.

He stood in front of it at a quarter past eleven on the last night of Obon with his hands in the pockets of his grey hoodie, in the hot dark, and looked at it. The long grey plaster wall. The one dark gate with the small door cut into it. The little camera tucked up under the roof tiles, which he had once, at fourteen, covered with a sock.

He had an unlit cigarette behind his ear. He had paint on his hands. He had not slept.

*I'm going to go and ask him for something,* he had told Rei on the phone. *I've never asked him for anything in my life.*

That was not quite true. He had asked him for something once. When he was ten. He had stood in the training hall with paint on his hands from his mother's studio and asked if he could stop. Stop training. Stop the doors. Paint instead, like her, in the evenings, when she was not being the family's medium and was only his mother with a brush.

His father had said nothing at all.

Itsuki took the cigarette from behind his ear, looked at it, and put it back. Then he pushed open the small door in the big gate and went in.

[break The garden]

Mrs. Fujii was in the garden with a lantern, setting out the last little dish of hemp stalks by the stone lantern for the sending fire. She stopped when she saw him. She held the rake very still in both hands, in her dark summer kimono, and her old face did something complicated, and then she bowed. Very low.

She held the bow a moment longer than she needed to. "Young master."

Itsuki put out a hand as if to stop her bowing. "Don't. Please, Fujii-san. Not today."

She straightened up. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had looked at him when he was small and had broken something and was trying very hard not to cry.

"He's in the hall," she said quietly. "He's been in the hall since the Director's people came this afternoon. He hasn't eaten." She hesitated. "He said if you came, to send you straight in. He said you would come tonight."

Itsuki stared at her.

Itsuki's hand stayed in the air. "He said that?"

"At six this evening." She bent and struck a match and lit the little dish. "He said, *my son will come tonight, and he will want something, and he will not know how to ask*." She did not look up. "Go on. Before you think about it."

He went.

Past the carp pond, where the fat orange fish came to the surface to look at him. Past the old pines and the stone lanterns. Past the old wing with its mossy roof, where his grandmother lived. Past the little studio at the back with its light on, where his mother was painting, as she painted every night when the training was done. He did not stop. He did not let himself look in.

The training hall stood at the end of the garden. The sliding doors were open. Inside, the old soft boards, worn pale by four hundred years of knees. The scroll on the far wall with its single brushed character. *門.* Gate.

And in the middle of the floor, standing very straight, in a dark haori with the white crest on the back, his father.

[break Sōun]

Kuroda Sōun did not turn round.

He was looking at the black lacquered doorframe that stood on its own in the centre of the hall, with the white paper rope across its top. The family gate. The one Itsuki had been taken through at seven years old in his father's arms, into a sea house in Kamakura fifty kilometres away, and come out with sand on his shoes, and never once been able to open himself, however hard he tried, however many nights he stood in front of it alone.

Itsuki stopped in the doorway of the hall.

He kept one foot outside the hall, on the step. "You knew I was coming."

"Yes." His father did not turn round. "Your friend the telepath thinks very loudly about you. I heard him once. In January. In a car park, when you came out." A pause. "He thinks you are the bravest person he knows. He thinks it every time you open your mouth to make a joke instead of saying what you mean."

Itsuki's face went hot.

Itsuki pushed his hands back into his pockets. "I didn't come here to talk about Kato."

"No." Sōun turned round.

He looked older. That was the first thing. In eight months, he looked older. The silver hair was whiter. The upright back was still upright, but it looked like it cost something now. The face was the same. Long and still and closed, the face Itsuki had been trying to read his whole life and had never once got past the first page.

Sōun folded his hands into his sleeves. "You came to ask for something. Ask."

Itsuki took a breath.

"Tonight," he said. "At the old freight yard past Tabata. The thing that's been eating people since before I was born is going to open the far shore at midnight. There are thousands of people in that yard. Living ones. Hollowed out. Sitting on the rails with flowers in their hands like they're waiting for a train." His voice was steady. He was proud of that. "When it opens, they'll walk into it. Rei can't carry thousands of people. Nobody can. Not on foot."

His father said nothing.

"You can," said Itsuki. "The family can. The gates. All of you. Every Kuroda who can open one. From the yard to anywhere. A hospital. A shrine. This garden. Anywhere that isn't the river." He swallowed. "That's what I came to ask. Come. Bring them. I can't do it. I've never been able to open so much as a cupboard. You know that. You watched me try for ten years."

Sōun's eyes did not move from his son's face. "I know."

"So I'm asking." Itsuki took his hands out of his pockets. They were shaking. He let them. "I'm asking you. Not as the head of the house. Not for the Division. I'm asking you because my friend is going to stand in the middle of that yard tonight and take his own door off, I know he is, I've known him since he was twelve. And I don't want him to be the only person in that yard holding anything up." His voice cracked. "And because you never said anything, not once, not when I asked to stop, not when I left, not in the hospital, nothing, *ever*, and I've been so angry with you for ten years that I forgot you're the only person in this city who could do this. Dad."

He stopped. He was crying. He had not meant to.

His father was looking at him.

The long still closed face did not change at all. But something moved behind it. Like a man in a house with all the curtains drawn, coming to the window and moving one curtain aside, an inch.

"Dad," said Sōun. His lips barely moved.

Itsuki stopped breathing.

"You called me that," said his father. "You have not called me that since you were ten." He looked at the black lacquered frame. "In this hall. You said *Dad, can I stop*." A long pause. "I did not answer you. Because the answer was yes, and I did not know how to say yes to my son in front of the gate my father said no to me in front of." He looked back at Itsuki. "I have not known how to speak to you since. So I called the Division instead, when the thing went into you in Yanaka, because the Division has forms, and I knew how to fill in a form."

The hall was very quiet. Outside, the cicadas. Somewhere in the house, a kettle. At the back of the garden, in the little studio, a light.

"Your mother paints," said Sōun, "when she is not being what this family needs her to be. You used to sit in the doorway of that studio and watch her. Every night. For years." He took one step toward his son. "I used to stand in the garden and watch you watch her. You had the same face she has. When she is only herself." Another step. "You did not leave this house because you could not open doors, Itsuki. You left it so that you could have that face. I have known that for ten years. I should have said so."

Itsuki could not speak.

"I will come," said his father. "I will bring every one of us who can open a gate. Your grandmother. Your uncle. Takuya. Sei, if he can be found." He stopped in front of Itsuki. Close. Closer than he had stood in ten years. "It will take me an hour to wake them and an hour to make them agree. It will not be quick. Kuroda are never quick." He paused. "Go ahead of us. Go to your friend. You see things nobody else in this house can see. You always have. Go and see them for him."

He put his hand on Itsuki's head. On the top of it. Heavy and warm and a little unsteady. Like a hand on the head of a small boy in a hall at night, with paint on his fingers, who has just asked for something.

"Go on," he said. "I'll be there."

[break Seven o'clock]

Itsuki came down the road from the cordon at a run, at five past midnight, with a box of house paint under his arm and his brother beside him.

Sei. Slight and quick, with his buzzed sides and his silver seal ring, who had been standing by the carp pond when Itsuki walked out of the training hall, with his hands in his pockets as if he had been waiting there for eight months. He had not said anything. He had only looked at Itsuki's face, and fallen in beside him, as he had fallen in beside him on the way to school when they were small. On the way, at a hardware shop by Nezu station where the old man was just pulling down the shutter, Sei had banged on the shutter until it came up again and bought the paint.

They reached the gate of the yard a few minutes after the fires went out.

Itsuki saw it all at once. The thousands of hollow people walking toward the grey beach. The tens of thousands of white lights drifting down to the water. The woman at the low white table on the far shore, standing now, with her teapot set down and her smile gone. Majima in the weeds with a girl in a pink hoodie held against his chest. Mori in the gateway with the air round him gone completely still.

And Rei.

Rei standing in the middle of the yard, among the walking dead, with his door off.

Itsuki could see it. He had always been able to see doors. He could see the place in Rei's chest where the door had been for twelve years, the one Gin had made at the kitchen table, the one Itsuki had spent eight years standing beside so his friend would not have to stand there alone. It was gone. Off its hinges. Laid down in the weeds. And through the empty frame, everything was pouring in. The yard. The dead. The far shore. All of it. Into one boy in a clean shirt who had promised a ghost on a riverbank a game of tag.

Itsuki's fingers tightened on the box of paint. "Shit. Oh, you stupid, stupid bastard."

Sei followed his eyes across the yard. "That's him? Uedera's brother?"

"That's Rei." Itsuki dropped the box of paint in the dirt. "That's my brother."

Sei looked at him sideways. He did not say anything.

Itsuki walked out into the yard.

[break The door]

He did not go to Rei. He went to the biggest freight car in the yard. An old one, black with rust, standing on its own on a curved track halfway to the river, with its long side facing the water and the grey beach and the thousands of walking dead.

He did not know why. He only knew that his hands wanted that wall.

He opened the box of paint. White. House paint. The cheapest kind. Two litres. He dipped his hand in it. His whole hand. Up to the wrist.

And he began to paint.

He did not know what. He never did, at the start. He had stopped asking years ago. He painted with his palm and his fingers and the side of his hand, on the long rusted wall of the freight car, as high as he could reach, and then Sei put a crate under his feet and he went higher. A door. A frame. And beyond the frame, inside it, a street.

A narrow old street at night, going away uphill. Lanterns. Low roofs. A bath house with a curtain at its door and steam coming out. A little shop with its shutter half up and a light inside. Shapes in the street, walking, in no hurry, the way people walk on a warm night after a festival. Shapes sitting on doorsteps, talking. A shape that might have been a dog, lying in the middle of the road in the warm, with nobody minding.

It was white on rust. It should have looked like nothing. It looked like the most ordinary evening in the world.

Itsuki stepped back off the crate and looked at what he had painted, and did not know what it was.

"That's not here," said Sei quietly beside him. "That's not anywhere in Tokyo."

"No," said Itsuki. His voice was strange. "It's where they're going. It's what's on the other side of the fog." He stared at it. "She keeps telling them it's cold. It's so far, and so cold, come and sit down. It isn't. Look at it. It isn't cold at all."

He had seen something nobody living had ever seen. He knew that, standing in the weeds with white paint drying on his arm to the elbow. He did not know how he knew. It was the kind of knowing he had always had, at the end of a painting, that he had spent ten years pretending was only good guessing.

[break Together]

Across the yard, among the walking hollow ones, a boy with his door off his hinges turned his head and looked at the freight car.

Rei saw it.

Not with his eyes first. With everything. The wet white door on the rust and the street inside it and the lanterns and the steam from the bath house and the dog in the road. He felt what it was. He did not understand how Itsuki had done it. He understood exactly what it was for.

*Itsu,* he thought, and placed it. Clear. Like a cup set down in front of somebody. *Itsu. Hold it there. Don't let it dry.*

On his crate, by the freight car, Itsuki jerked as if somebody had touched him on the back of the neck.

*Is that you?* Itsuki's thought was so loud it nearly knocked Rei over. *In my head? Since when can you do that? Since when?*

*Since about ten minutes ago.*

*You absolute...*

*Hold it there,* thought Rei, and turned to face the dead.

There were so many. Ten thousand. Twenty. Drifting down toward the grey beach in their small paper lights, slow and patient and puzzled, toward the woman in the grey cardigan standing now beside her low white table with her hands held out. *Come and rest. It's so far. It's so cold.*

Rei did not shout. He did not try to drown her out. He was one person, and she had been doing this since the city was called something else.

He only looked at Itsuki's door. At the street inside it. At the steam from the bath house and the light in the half-open shop and the dog asleep in the road. He looked at it as hard as he had ever looked at anything, until it was the only thing in his head.

And he opened the door that was not there any more, and let it out.

Not into one mind. Into all of them. Every small light in the yard. He did not push. He did not tell them anything. He only showed them. *This is where you're going. This is what's past the fog. Look.*

The nearest white light stopped.

It hung in the air, swaying very slightly. Inside it, very small, the face of an old man in a farmer's straw hat turned. Away from the woman at the table. Toward the painted door. His face changed.

It was the face of a man who has been walking a long way in the dark, and has come round a corner, and seen at the end of the street a light in his own kitchen window.

And he went on.

Not to the freight car. Past the woman's table. Down the grey beach, past her outstretched hands, without stopping, without looking at her, into the fog. Home. The right way.

Then the next one. And the next. And then all of them. Ten thousand. Twenty. Turning in the air over the walking crowd, and going down the beach in a long bright stream, like a river of paper lanterns, past the low white table, past the woman, into the fog and through it and gone. Through her door. Out. All at once. So many and so close together that nothing could have come in the other way if it had tried.

On the grey beach, Kanna-sensei's hands closed into fists.

On the edge of the river, on a concrete block, a ten-year-old boy in a wet striped T-shirt stood up with his dog beside him and pointed, shouting, *Up the rails! Not the water! Up the rails, that way, go on!*, at the lights that had drifted too near the edge, and they turned and went where he pointed.

Itsuki stood on his crate with both white palms pressed flat against the wet painted frame to keep it from drying, and his whole body shaking, and he held it.

He felt a hand on his back. Slight. Quick. A silver ring.

"I've got you," said Sei, behind him, very quietly. "Hold the door, little brother. I've always had your back. You just never turned round."

Itsuki laughed. It came out like a sob. "I hate you."

"I know. Hold the door."

*Thank you,* said Rei's voice, inside Itsuki's head, for the first time in eight years of friendship. *For the door.*

Itsuki held it, and cried, and did not let go.
