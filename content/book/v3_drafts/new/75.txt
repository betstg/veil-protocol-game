#title Itsuki

Kuroda Itsuki had not walked through the front gate of his father's house in eight months.

He stood in front of it at two in the afternoon on the thirteenth of August with his hands in the pockets of his grey hoodie, in thirty-four degrees, and looked at it. The long grey plaster wall. The one dark gate with the small door cut into it. The little camera tucked up under the roof tiles, which he had once, at fourteen, covered with a sock.

He had an unlit cigarette behind his ear. He had paint on his hands. He had not slept.

*I'm going to go and ask him for something,* he had told Rei on the phone. *I've never asked him for anything in my life.*

That was not quite true. He had asked him for something once. When he was five. On the evening of the last afternoon their mother ever took them anywhere, to a bridge over a river with a bag of bread for the ducks. That night, in the hall, he had asked his father to make her stay.

His father had said nothing at all.

Itsuki took the cigarette from behind his ear, looked at it, and put it back. Then he pushed open the small door in the big gate and went in.

[break The garden]

Mrs. Fujii was raking the gravel. She stopped when she saw him. She held the rake very still in both hands, in her dark summer kimono, and her old face did something complicated, and then she bowed. Very low.

She held the bow a moment longer than she needed to. "Young master."

Itsuki put out a hand as if to stop her bowing. "Don't. Please, Fujii-san. Not today."

She straightened up. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she had looked at him when he was small and had broken something and was trying very hard not to cry.

"He's in the hall," she said quietly. "He's been in the hall since six this morning. He hasn't eaten." She hesitated. "He said if you came, to send you straight in. He said you would come today."

Itsuki stared at her.

Itsuki's hand stayed in the air. "He said that?"

"At six this morning." She went back to raking. Small careful strokes. "He said, *my son will come today, and he will want something, and he will not know how to ask*." She did not look up. "Go on. Before you think about it."

He went.

Past the carp pond, where the fat orange fish came to the surface to look at him. Past the old pines and the stone lanterns. Past the old wing with its mossy roof, where his grandmother lived, where she would be sitting right now by the window with her white bun and her opinion of his liver.

The training hall stood at the end of the garden. The sliding doors were open. Inside, the old soft boards, worn pale by four hundred years of knees. The scroll on the far wall with its single brushed character. *門.* Gate.

And in the middle of the floor, standing very straight, in a dark haori with the white crest on the back, his father.

[break Sōun]

Kuroda Sōun did not turn round.

He was looking at the black lacquered doorframe that stood on its own in the centre of the hall, with the white paper rope across its top. The family gate. The one Itsuki had walked through at seven years old into a sea house in Kamakura fifty kilometres away, and come out with sand on his shoes and his father's hand on his shoulder and the first and last thing his father had ever said to him that sounded like pride.

*Again.*

Itsuki stopped in the doorway of the hall.

He kept one foot outside the hall, on the step. "You knew I was coming."

"Yes." His father did not turn round. "Your friend the telepath thinks very loudly about you. I heard him once. In January. In a car park, when you came out." A pause. "He thinks you are the bravest person he knows. He thinks it every time you open your mouth to make a joke instead of saying what you mean."

Itsuki's face went hot.

Itsuki pushed his hands back into his pockets. "I didn't come here to talk about Kato."

"No." Sōun turned round.

He looked older. That was the first thing. In eight months, he looked older. The silver hair was whiter. The upright back was still upright, but it looked like it cost something now. The face was the same. Long and still and closed, the face Itsuki had been trying to read his whole life and had never once got past the first page.

Sōun folded his hands into his sleeves. "You came to ask for something. Ask."

Itsuki took a breath.

"Tonight," he said. "At the old freight yard past Tabata. The thing that's been eating people since before I was born is going to open the far shore and walk the whole city's dead through it. Every grandmother. Every kid. Everybody coming home for Obon." His voice was steady. He was proud of that. "She's lit false lights. Thousands. Down by the river. The dead are following them instead of the fires on the steps. Rei's got half of Kita lighting fires right now. It won't be enough. They're too far away. The dead won't find them in time."

His father said nothing.

"I need gates," said Itsuki. "Not one. Not the family gate. Thousands. From the yard to every fire in Kita. Every doorstep. Every bath house. Every bar in Ni-chōme. A door for every one of them, so they can walk straight home without having to find the way." He swallowed. "I can't do that. I can do one. Two. I can do a hole in the air the size of a car. I can't do ten thousand. Nobody can do ten thousand."

Sōun's eyes did not move from his son's face. "No. Nobody can."

"You can open gates across whole districts. I've seen you. Through rivers. Through walls."

"I can open one gate across a whole district," said his father. "One. Large. It costs me a week in bed." He looked at the black lacquered frame. "Ten thousand small ones. All at once. To ten thousand different places. That is not a thing that can be done by one man." He looked back at Itsuki. "That is what you came to ask? That I do something that cannot be done?"

Itsuki shook his head, once. "No."

He took his hands out of his pockets. They were shaking. He let them.

"I came to ask you to teach me how," he said. "In four hours. Because I think I know how it could be done. I think I've known since the hospital. Since Room Nine. Since I drew doors on the walls in my own blood for forty-nine days because something in my spine wanted out." He held up his right hand. The four small black marks between the knuckles. "I've got twenty-four locked doors down my back. Uruma put them there. And I paint. That's all I've ever done. I paint doors. I've painted ten thousand of them. On canvas and walls and bridges and the backs of receipts." His voice cracked. "I don't need to open ten thousand gates, Dad. I need to paint one, and make it agree with ten thousand places at once. And I don't know how to make a gate agree with anything. Because you never taught me. Because I left. Because I was angry. Because you never said anything, not once, not that night, not when she left, not in the hospital, nothing, *ever*, and I've been so angry with you for fifteen years that I forgot I'm the only person in this city who might be able to do it."

He stopped. He was crying. He had not meant to.

His father was looking at him.

The long still closed face did not change at all. But something moved behind it. Something Itsuki had never seen before, not once, not in twenty years. Like a man in a house with all the curtains drawn, coming to the window and moving one curtain aside, just an inch.

Sōun's lips barely moved. "Dad."

Itsuki stopped breathing.

"You called me Dad," said his father. "You have not called me that since you were five." He looked at the black lacquered frame. "In this hall. You said *Dad, make her stay*." A long pause. "I did not answer you. Because I could not make her stay. And I did not know how to say *I can't* to my son. A Kuroda does not say *I can't*. A Kuroda opens doors." He looked back at Itsuki. "I could not open that one. And I have not known how to speak to you since."

The hall was very quiet. Outside, the cicadas. Somewhere in the house, a kettle.

"A gate is not a hole," said Sōun. "That is the first thing. Every Kuroda child is told that at seven, and every one of them forgets it at once, because a gate looks like a hole. It is not. A gate is an agreement. Between two places. This place and that place agree, for a moment, that they are next to each other. That is all. The skill is not in the opening. It is in the asking." He took one step toward his son. "The doorstep fires are already asking. Every one of them. *Come home. Come home. This way.* That is what a fire on a step is. It is a place asking to be next to the dead." Another step. "You do not need to make ten thousand places agree. They already agree. They are begging to agree. You need one door that says *yes* to all of them at once."

Itsuki stared at him.

Itsuki's hands fell to his sides. "One door."

"One door," said his father. "Painted by someone who has painted ten thousand. Held open by someone with twenty-four locked doors in his spine and something asleep behind them that knows more about doors than any living Kuroda." He stopped in front of Itsuki. Close. Closer than he had stood in fifteen years. "It will cost you. It may cost you a great deal. The thing in your back will wake, a little, to help. You may not be able to put it back to sleep."

Itsuki held his eyes. "I know."

Sōun tilted his head, very slightly, the way a man listens for a sound in another room. "And you would still do it."

"Rei's going to stand in the middle of that yard and take his own door off," said Itsuki. "I know he is. I've known him since he was twelve. He's going to take it right off its hinges and let everything in." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "I'm not letting him do that on his own. Not while I've got a door left."

Sōun looked at his son for a long time.

Then he did something he had never done.

He put his hand on Itsuki's head. On the top of it. Heavy and warm and a little unsteady. The way you put your hand on the head of a small boy in a hall at night, with river mud still on his shoes, who has just asked you for something you cannot give.

He did not take his hand away. "Four hours. Sit down. We'll start with the asking."

[break Seven o'clock]

Itsuki came down the road from the cordon at a run, at a quarter to seven, with chalk dust up to his elbows and a box of house paint under his arm and his brother beside him.

Sei. Slight and quick, with his buzzed sides and his silver seal ring, who had been at the Kuroda house when Itsuki walked out of the training hall at six, standing by the carp pond with his hands in his pockets as if he had been waiting there for eight months. He had not said anything. He had only looked at Itsuki's face, and at the chalk, and at the paint, and fallen in beside him, the way he had fallen in beside him on the way to school when they were small.

They reached the gate of the yard as the meeting hour ended.

Itsuki saw it all at once. The thousands of hollow people walking toward the grey beach. The tens of thousands of white lights drifting down to the water. The woman at the low white table on the far shore, standing now, with her teapot set down and her smile gone. Majima in the weeds with a girl in a pink hoodie held against his chest. Mori in the gateway with the air round him gone completely still.

And Rei.

Rei standing in the middle of the yard, among the walking dead, with his door off.

Itsuki could see it. He had always been able to see doors. He could see the place in Rei's chest where the door had been for eleven years, the one Gin had made at the kitchen table, the one Itsuki had spent seven years standing beside, holding open a crack, so his friend could breathe. It was gone. Off its hinges. Laid down in the weeds. And through the empty frame, everything was pouring in. The yard. The dead. The far shore. All six hands, wide open. All of it. Into one boy in a clean shirt who had promised a kid on a staircase that he would come home.

Itsuki's fingers tightened on the box of paint. "Shit. Oh, you stupid, stupid bastard."

Sei followed his eyes across the yard. "That's him? Uedera's brother?"

"That's Rei." Itsuki dropped the box of paint in the dirt. "That's my brother."

Sei looked at him sideways. He did not say anything.

Itsuki walked out into the yard.

[break The door]

He did not go to Rei. He went to the biggest freight car in the yard. An old one, black with rust, standing on its own on a curved track halfway to the river, with its long side facing the water and the grey beach and the thousands of walking dead.

He opened the box of paint. White. House paint. The cheapest kind. Two litres. He had bought it at a hardware shop in Nezu at five o'clock with his father standing beside him, and his father had paid.

He dipped his hand in it. His whole hand. Up to the wrist.

And he began to paint a door.

Not a hole. Not a gap. A door. A proper one. On the long rusted side of the freight car, as tall as the car and as wide as he could reach, with his hand and his fingers and his palm. A frame. A lintel. Two panels. A handle. A step. The way he had painted ten thousand doors on ten thousand canvases and walls and bridges and the backs of receipts, without knowing why, since he was thirteen years old and had met a boy with a door in his chest.

The thing in his spine woke up.

He felt it. Very small. Very slow. One of the twenty-four locked doors down his back, the one at the top, between his shoulder blades, clicking open the width of a finger. And something behind it, long and thin and grey and faceless, turning in its sleep, the way a cat turns toward a sound in another room. Interested. Just a little. *Doors,* it thought, in a voice like a key turning in a lock. *Doors. Oh. You're making a door. A real one. Let me see.*

*Just look,* Itsuki told it. *Just look. Don't touch.*

He finished the door.

It stood on the side of the rusted freight car, white and wet and enormous, dripping, every line of it exactly right.

Then he did what his father had taught him in four hours in a hall that smelled of four hundred years of incense.

He did not open it.

He asked.

*Come home,* he thought. Not at the dead. At the fires. At every fire on every step in Kita. At the forty little piles of hemp stalks on the third-floor walkway of a building at the top of a hill above Ōji, lit now, all forty, by a seventeen-year-old with bleached hair and a box of matches. At the bath houses, where an old woman with her knitting needles in her bun had put a fire at every door. At the bars in Ni-chōme. At Hoshino's blue curtain on Sakae Street. At a thousand doorsteps where somebody's grandmother had dragged herself out of bed and put a match to a little dish of straw because a boy had knocked on her door and said *please*.

*You're asking,* Itsuki thought at them. *I can hear you. Every one of you. Asking for somebody. Come home, come home, this way.* He put both his white wet hands flat on the painted door. *Here. Here's the way. This door agrees with you. All of you. It's next to every one of you. It says yes.*

The door opened.

[break Home]

It did not open onto the far shore.

It did not open onto the dark, or the white, or anywhere strange.

It opened onto a walkway.

A concrete walkway on the third floor of an old building at the top of a hill, lit orange by a single lamp, with a moth beating against it, and a line of little fires burning in clay dishes and frying pans and a cake tin and an ashtray in front of forty front doors. And a cucumber horse and an aubergine cow on the step two doors down.

And through it, behind it, at the same time, like a picture seen through a picture, a thousand other doorsteps. A bath house with a red noren and a fire at its door. A bar with a purple sign. A set-meal restaurant with a blue curtain. A little house in Jūjō with a bakery downstairs. A watch shop with its shutters down. All of them at once. All of them next to each other. All of them right here, on the side of a freight car, in a painted white door.

The nearest white light stopped drifting toward the river.

It hung in the air, swaying very slightly. Inside it, very small, the face of an old man in a farmer's straw hat turned. Away from the grey beach. Toward the door. Toward the fires.

His face changed.

It was the face of a man who has been walking a long way in the dark and has come round a corner and seen, at the end of the street, a light in his own kitchen window.

The white light turned and drifted into the door.

Then the next one. And the next. And then all of them. Ten thousand. Twenty. Turning in the air over the walking crowd, away from the river, away from the woman at the table, and pouring toward the painted door in a long bright stream, like a river of paper lanterns running uphill, and through it, and home.

On the grey beach, Kanna-sensei's hands closed into fists.

Itsuki stood with his wet white palms flat against the frame of the door and his whole body shaking and something long and grey and faceless watching from behind the first of his twenty-four doors with enormous interest, and he held it.

He felt a hand on his back.

He knew whose without looking. Slight. Quick. A silver ring.

"I've got you," said Sei, behind him, very quietly. "Hold the door, little brother. I've got your back. I've always had your back. You just never turned round."

Itsuki laughed. It came out like a sob.

He pressed his palms harder against the wet frame. "I hate you."

His brother's hand stayed flat between his shoulder blades. "I know. Hold the door."

Across the yard, among the walking hollow ones, a boy with his door off his hinges turned his head and looked at the freight car, and at the white door painted on it, and at the stream of lights pouring through it toward a walkway at the top of a hill where a kid with a box of matches was sitting on a step, watching the fires.

And Rei smiled.

Itsuki saw it. Even from there. Even through the dark and the lights and the shaking. That stupid, rare, wide smile, the one Gin used to be able to get out of him once a year if he was lucky.

*Thank you,* said Rei's voice, inside Itsuki's head, for the first time in seven years of friendship. Placed there. Clear. Like a cup set down in front of him. *For the door.*

Itsuki held the door, and cried, and did not let go.
