#title The Back of the Card

Okada walked fast and did not look back to see whether he was following. He followed.

She took him out of the station by the south exit, away from the blue suits and the lanterns, across the road and up a narrow lane between a pharmacy and a shoe shop with its shutters down. Halfway up the lane there was a coffee shop so small that Rei had walked past it for years without ever seeing it. Four tables. A glass case with three sad sandwiches in it. A radio on the counter playing old enka songs very quietly, a woman singing about a harbour and a man who did not come back. A woman of about seventy stood behind the counter in an apron. She looked at Okada, and at Rei's face, and put the kettle on without a word.

They sat at the table furthest from the window. The old woman brought Okada a pot of strong green tea and brought Rei a tall glass of iced barley tea he had not ordered, and then patted him once on the shoulder, briskly, the way you pat a dog that has been out in the rain, and went back behind her counter.

Okada poured her tea. She took the three notebooks out of her jacket, slid the rubber band off, and laid them on the table in a neat row. She opened the middle one and read something in it, slowly, moving her lips very slightly, for almost a minute. Rei watched her do it and did not interrupt. He had learned that at the police station. People who read before they talk are usually about to tell you something true.

She closed the notebook and looked at him.

"Ask," she said.

He had a hundred questions. The first one that came out surprised him.

"Did that man have a family?"

Okada was quiet for a moment. Her face did not change, but she put her cup down.

"Yes. A wife. Haruna. Two daughters, both in high school." She said it flatly, the way you read a list off a form, and somehow that made it worse. "He was an accountant at a printing company in Akabane. He was on his way to his wife's parents' house for Obon. They live in Saitama. He'd bought sweets for them at the department store at lunchtime. They're still in his bag." She drank some tea. "His name was Ogawa. Ogawa Tetsuya."

Rei looked down at his barley tea. Small drops of water were running down the outside of the glass. He watched one of them go all the way to the table.

"What was in him?"

"Something that wanted to talk to you." Okada turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "That's not what they usually want."

"What do they usually want?"

"Food. A body that still works. A door into somewhere warm." She looked toward the window, at the dark lane outside. "Most of what comes through the veil is small and lost. A few things aren't. The ones that aren't, we call demons. A demon is a spirit that decided to feed and grow, instead of fading the way it's supposed to. It feeds on one feeling. Grief, or fear, or rage, or something else. It finds people who are full of that feeling, and it gets inside, and it lives there. Sometimes it lives in someone quietly for years and nobody ever knows. Sometimes it uses a person up in an afternoon." She was quiet for a second. "Mr. Ogawa got used up in an afternoon."

"It said I had room in me," said Rei. "It said *just like the other one*."

"I heard you."

"The other one is Gin."

Okada did not answer. That was the answer.

"They told me at the front desk that he's on assignment."

"I know what they told you."

"Is he?"

She picked up her cup and held it in both hands without drinking. Her hands were small and very clean, with short square nails, and there was a pen mark on the side of one finger that had not quite washed off.

"I'm going to tell you what I'm allowed to tell you," she said. "Then I'm going to tell you one thing I'm not allowed to tell you. And after that you're going to listen to me. Because I've been doing this job for eight years, and you've been doing it for one evening."

"Okay."

"What I'm allowed to tell you." She said it in a voice like somebody reading off a card at a counter, and Rei realised she was doing an impression, very drily, of the young man in the lobby. "*Officer Uedera Gin has not reported to his supervisor since Wednesday evening. That is not unusual during Obon for a field officer in his position. The Division is aware of his status. You will be informed of any change.*" She put the cup down, and her own voice came back. "What I'm not allowed to tell you. Nobody in that building knows where he is. And the people who ought to be looking for him are not looking very hard."

Rei felt the cold weight of the key in his pocket, against his leg.

"Why not?"

"I don't know yet." She closed the notebook and put her hand flat on top of it. "That's the truth. I don't know yet. I've been watching the numbers in this district for four years, and for four years something has been wrong with them. Small things. Patterns nobody else reads as patterns. Your brother saw them too. I think he went to look at something on Wednesday night. And I think whatever he found is the reason people in very nice suits are making very sure nobody asks."

[break The card]

"Four years ago," said Okada, "your brother came to my desk with two cans of coffee from the vending machine and put one down in front of me without asking if I wanted it. And he said, *I have a younger brother. He sees more than he knows. If anything ever happens to me, he'll do something stupid. Please be there when he does.*"

Rei did not trust his voice. He drank his barley tea instead, too fast, and it went down cold and hard.

"You're about to do something stupid," said Okada. "It's all over your face."

"My left eyebrow goes up," said Rei, before he could stop himself.

She frowned at him. "What?"

"Nothing. A friend says it does. When I'm lying."

She studied him for another second, very seriously, as if she were checking. Then she took a card from her inside pocket and laid it on the table between them. A plain white card with the little white owl at the top. *Okada Shizuka. Investigation. Kita District.* A phone number. She turned it over. On the back, by hand, in very small neat figures, was another number.

"The front is my desk. The back is me." She tapped it with one finger. "What are you going to do tomorrow?"

"I'm seeing the Director. At nine."

For the first time that evening, Okada looked surprised. It did not last long. Her eyebrows went up a few millimetres and came down again, and she picked up her cup and found it was empty and put it down.

"Adachi answered you."

"I wrote to him yesterday. Gin had his number in the book. He wrote back today. He said he'd see me himself."

Okada was quiet for a while. She turned the empty cup a quarter turn on its saucer, and then another quarter.

"Then go," she said at last. "And listen to me. Don't lie to him. Not about anything. Not about whether you slept, not about whether you ate. He can tell, and he won't say so, and after that he won't trust you again. Your brother told me that in my first week, and I've watched it happen to three people since." She pushed the card the last few centimetres toward him. "Keep this anyway. Whatever he tells you, call the back. Not the front. Never the front."

He took it.

She stood up, put the notebooks back in her pocket one by one, snapped the rubber band round them, and went to the counter and paid for both drinks with exact change, counting the coins out into the old woman's palm. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the frame.

"Uedera."

"Yes?"

"The thing on the platform. It didn't come for Mr. Ogawa. Mr. Ogawa was just the closest coat it could put on." She did not turn round. "It came for you. Go home. Lock the door. Don't open it for anybody who knocks. And be in Marunouchi at nine."

Then she was gone, and the little bell over the door rang once behind her and was still.

The old woman behind the counter came and took away the empty glass. She looked at Rei for a moment, at the white card in his fingers. Then she went back behind the counter and came out again with a sandwich from the glass case, egg, slightly squashed, and put it on the table in front of him without a word, and went back to her radio.

He ate it. He had not known how hungry he was.

[break The Director]

He went to Marunouchi in his one good shirt, which he had ironed at two in the morning because ironing was something to do with his hands.

The young man with the bright smile was at the front desk again. This time, when Rei gave his name, the smile did something new. It went careful and very slightly frightened, the way a waiter smiles when the owner of the restaurant has just sat down at one of his tables. He did not ask Rei to take a seat. He came out from behind the counter himself and walked him to the lift.

The Director's office was on the fourth floor, at the end of a corridor of closed doors. It was smaller than Rei had expected. A wide desk with nothing on it except a telephone, a pen, and an old train pass worn soft at the corners, lying face down. Shelves of files in grey boxes. One window, with a view of the glass tower across the street and a thin slice of sky. It was cold in there, colder than the corridor, a dry still cold that had nothing to do with air conditioning. Rei felt it settle on the back of his neck the moment he stepped through the door, and felt the pressure behind his eyes go quiet, as if someone had put a hand over a radio.

[img char_adachi-kenshin.png | Director Adachi Kenshin]

Adachi Kenshin stood up when Rei came in. He was in his fifties, tall and a little stooped, with grey hair cut very short and a lined, tired, patient face. He wore a dark suit that had been good ten years ago. His eyes were the first thing Rei noticed and the thing he remembered afterward. They were pale for a Japanese man, almost grey, and they did not look at Rei so much as rest on him, lightly, the way a hand rests on the bonnet of a car to feel if the engine is still warm.

"Uedera Rei," he said. "Sit down, please."

Rei sat. Adachi sat. Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"How are you?" said the Director.

*Fine*, Rei almost said. It was on his tongue. It was what you said. He thought of Okada turning her cup on its saucer, and of Gin's page. *Do not lie to him. You get one attempt.*

"Not good," he said. "I haven't slept since Wednesday. Last night I watched a man walk backwards off the platform at Ōji, and the thing inside him talked to me about my brother. I ate an egg sandwich at eleven. That's all I've eaten."

Adachi nodded slowly, as if Rei had just handed him a document and he was checking it against another one.

"Thank you," he said. "I know about the platform. Okada filed her report at midnight. She writes very good reports." He turned the train pass over on the desk with one finger, so that it lay face up, and then turned it face down again. "I asked you to come because your brother is listed as on assignment, and I want you to hear from me that I did not list him."

Rei stared at him.

"Then who did?"

"I don't know yet." Adachi said it simply, without any shame, the way Okada had. "This Division is very large, Uedera. It is larger than one man at a desk on the fourth floor. There are parts of this building that do not answer to me, and there are people above me whose names you will never read on any door. Sometime on Thursday morning, a status was entered against your brother's name, and nobody in Field Operations entered it." He looked at Rei with those pale resting eyes. "I am telling you that because you would have found it out eventually, and I would rather you heard it from me than from the wrong person."

"Is he alive?"

The question came out before Rei could stop it. Adachi did not answer at once. The cold in the room seemed to deepen for a second, very slightly, and Rei had the strange feeling that the Director was listening to something that was not in the room.

"I believe so," said Adachi. "I cannot prove it. Do you want me to tell you what I can prove, or what I believe?"

"What you believe."

"I believe your brother is alive, and in Tokyo, and in more trouble than any of us has a form for." He leaned back. "And I believe that whatever spoke to you on that platform knew who you were. Which means that from now on you are not just a missing officer's brother. You are something that things are looking at."

Rei put his hand in his pocket, round the key. It was cold, the same as always.

"What do I do?"

"What would your brother tell you to do?"

Rei thought about it. *Ask how they are first.* The pages at the back of the book. The map with the two stone foxes.

"Go and see the people in his book," he said slowly. "In order."

"Then do that." Adachi stood up, which meant the meeting was over. It had lasted eleven minutes. "Uedera. One more thing. Okada's report says the thing on the platform spoke to you by name, or near enough. I don't know why. Until we do, don't stand near the edge of any platform, and don't open your door at night to anyone who knocks."

"Okada said the same thing."

"Okada is usually right. It is one of her less comfortable qualities." For the first time, something softened very slightly round his mouth. "Go home. Eat something that isn't a sandwich. My door is open to you, and so is my phone. Use them when you need to, not before."

In the lift going down, Rei realised he was shaking again. Not from fear this time. He did not know from what. It was the first time in three days that anyone in that building had told him the truth, and it turned out the truth was worse than the lie, and he was grateful for it anyway.

[break The map]

That night he went back to the pages at the back of Gin's book, and this time he read the first one slowly.

[doc The Ones Worth Knowing. Tanizaki Seiichi.]
Old quarter of Kita. Retired from active work in the technical sense. Has been doing active work since retiring, in the same way I have been doing active work while pretending to be Channel rank. The tea ritual means something. Drink it. Does not explain anything until the third cup and I genuinely cannot tell if this is strategic or if he just likes tea that much. Has seen everything. Will help with almost anything if you ask without performing urgency.
→ Home. Call after 9am.
[/doc]

Under the line Gin had drawn a small map in pencil. A lane off the old shopping street below Ōji, past a little shrine with two stone foxes, to a house with a garden. He had drawn the garden as a wild scribble of pencil lines and written beside it, *Gone to seed on purpose. Do not offer to weed it.*

It was fifteen minutes' walk from the flat. Rei decided not to call. A man who wrote *call after 9am* was a man who would be up at dawn, and who would think more of somebody who knocked.

[break Three cups]

[need ch5_tanizaki_garden | A small old wooden house in Kita with a wild overgrown garden, tall grass, a fig tree through the fence, a stone lantern in ivy, morning summer light]

He went at nine in the morning, because something told him that an old man who lived like that would be up at dawn and would think less of anyone who came late.

It was Sunday, the sixteenth of August. The last day of Obon. All over the city, that evening, families would light the small fires at their doors again and send their dead back where they came from.

The house was old and wooden and small, with a tiled roof that dipped in the middle like the back of a tired horse. The garden in front of it was a wild green mess, exactly as Gin had drawn it. Long grass. Weeds with tiny yellow flowers. A fig tree that had grown straight through the fence and out over the lane. A stone lantern half swallowed by ivy. There was no bell. Rei stood in front of the sliding door for a moment, and straightened his shirt, and knocked.

A voice inside said, "It's open."

The man in the small front room was about sixty. He was thin and brown from the sun, with short grey hair and a face that had settled a long time ago into calm, patient lines. He wore a faded blue cotton jinbei, the loose summer clothes old men wear at home. He was kneeling at a low table, and on the table there was a tray with a teapot and four cups. Four, not one. He looked up at Rei without the smallest sign of surprise, as if he had been expecting somebody, and was not especially interested in finding out who.

[img char_tanizaki-seiichi.png | Tanizaki Seiichi]

"Sit," he said.

Rei sat. And then it all came out, before he could stop it. "Mr. Tanizaki, my name's Uedera Rei, I'm Gin's brother, Uedera Gin, he's missing, he's been gone since Wednesday night and the CVD keeps saying he's on assignment and they're lying, and on Friday something got inside a man on the platform at Ōji and it talked to me about Gin and then the man walked backwards in front of a train, he's dead, and the police won't even take the form, and Gin wrote that you were the first one, the first person he trusts, so I need you to help me find..."

Tanizaki lifted the teapot and poured tea into one cup. He pushed the cup across the table with two fingers.

"Drink," he said.

Rei drank it. Green tea, very hot, bitter at the back of the tongue.

"Mr. Tanizaki, please, I really don't have time to..."

Tanizaki poured a second cup and pushed it across.

Rei looked at it. He picked it up and drank it fast, too fast, and burned his tongue, and put it down harder than he meant to.

"Please," he said. "Gin wrote that you'd help. He wrote that you'd help with almost anything, if..."

He stopped.

*If you ask without performing urgency.*

And under that, from the first page of the book, in the same small hand. *Do not lead with what you need. Ask how they are first.*

Tanizaki was watching him with mild, patient interest, the way you watch a cat that has got its head stuck in a paper bag and is trying to reverse out of it with dignity.

Rei felt his face go hot, all the way to his ears. He was suddenly very aware of how he must look. A boy with red eyes and yesterday's shirt and his hair sticking up at the back, talking too fast, waving his hands, saying *please* every five words. Performing. Exactly what Gin had told him, in writing, twice, not to do. He had come into an old man's house at nine in the morning and not even asked how he was.

Tanizaki did not pour a third cup.

Instead he stood up, slowly, with one hand on the edge of the table, and slid open the door onto the garden. The smell of cut grass and hot earth came in, and the loud dry song of the cicadas.

"Come back," he said, "when you have finished being in a hurry."

"My brother is..."

"Your brother has been in a hurry since he was nine years old," said Tanizaki. "It is the only thing about him I never managed to fix." He stood to one side of the open door. It was not rude. It was the opposite of rude, somehow, which made it much worse. "Go home. Sleep. Eat something that is not from a konbini. Come back when you can sit in this room for an hour without asking me for anything. Then we will talk about your brother."

Rei stood up. His legs had gone stiff from kneeling. He wanted to argue. He got as far as the door and turned round, with his mouth open, and Tanizaki was already kneeling at the table again, pouring tea into the third cup. He drank it himself, slowly, with his eyes half closed, looking out through the open door at his ruined garden. He did not look up again.

Rei walked home through the old shopping street with the sun on the back of his neck and the key cold in his pocket, and he was so angry he could barely see. Angry at Tanizaki and his stupid tea. Angry at Okada and her card and her notebooks. Angry at the young man in the lobby and at Kumagai and at Ono and his someone at the festival, and even, a little, at the Director, for being honest and having nothing to give him. And angry at Gin most of all. For leaving. For lying. For teaching him a breathing trick at the kitchen table and calling it a trick for sleep.

Under the anger, very small, there was another feeling. He did not have a name for it yet. It was the feeling of having been seen clearly by somebody, all the way through, and sent home for his own good.

He did not like it at all.
