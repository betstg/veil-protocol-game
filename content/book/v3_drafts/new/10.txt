#title Nezu

On Saturday Rei went back to Tanizaki's house and sat in the front room for an hour.

He did not ask for anything. It was the hardest hour of his life, and he had had a bad couple of weeks. He knelt at the low table with his hands on his knees. Tanizaki poured him one cup of tea, and later, without comment, a second. Rei drank them slowly, because he had worked out that drinking them fast did not make the third one come any sooner. Tanizaki read a newspaper. He read it properly, every page, including the stock prices, which as far as anyone knew he did not own any of. Out in the wild garden a bush warbler sang the same three notes over and over, as if it had forgotten the rest of the song and was hoping someone would remind it. The cicadas sawed in the fig tree. Once a cat walked along the top of the fence, looked in at them both with flat yellow eyes, judged them, and walked on.

Every few minutes Rei wanted to say *Gin*. The word sat in his mouth like a plum stone. He kept it there. Once he opened his mouth, and Tanizaki turned a page, slowly, and the sound of the paper was so loud in the quiet room that Rei shut his mouth again.

After an hour Tanizaki folded the newspaper in half, and in half again, and laid it beside his knee. He looked at the teapot. There was tea left in it. He did not pour a third cup.

"Better," he said.

Rei bowed. His knees had gone past aching and into a sort of distant hum.

"Come back next Saturday."

That was all. Rei walked home with his legs full of needles and his head full of a strange flat calm that lasted almost to the bottom of the hill, where a man on a scooter nearly ran him over and called him something that would have made Tanizaki fold his newspaper a third time.

[break Oranges]

Itsuki came over that night with a plastic bag of oranges, two cans of beer and a smear of cadmium yellow along his jaw that he did not know about and nobody was going to tell him. He sat on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge and his long legs stuck out across the lino, so that Rei had to step over them to get to the kettle.

"Tell me again," he said. "The arcade. The cup thing. All of it. Slowly. Don't skip the parts where you look stupid."

"I skip those so you don't enjoy it."

"I'll enjoy it anyway. Go."

Rei told him. Itsuki listened with his head tipped back against the fridge door and his eyes closed, the cigarette behind his ear unlit, the beer can held against his neck for the cold. Now and then he said *mm*. When Rei got to the mug in Lilac, and the dead barman who had opened his mouth every night at two o'clock and shut it again, Itsuki opened his eyes.

"You read a cup," he said.

"I held it. And I saw what it saw."

He had told him the other part already. He had rung him from the street outside Café Asahi on Tuesday night, standing under the sign for the noodle place, and Itsuki had gone very quiet and then said *Ni-chōme* three times, as if the word might give up something more if he said it often enough. On Wednesday Itsuki had gone and stood outside Café Asahi from seven o'clock until it closed, and seen nobody in a grey jacket. He had not mentioned it. Matsuda Keiko had, when Rei rang the café on Thursday to ask whether anybody had walked past. "Your friend with the paint stood in my doorway for four hours," she said. "I gave him toast. Tell him a doorway is not a good place to wait."

"Rei. That's a real thing." Itsuki sat up. "That's a thing people train for years to do. My cousins train for years. My brother trained for years and he still can't do it clean." He picked an orange out of the bag and peeled it in one long unbroken spiral without looking, the way Gin had taught both of them to when they were twelve and fighting over who got the peel to make a snake. "They'd sell their grandmothers to do that at twenty with no teacher."

"I've got a teacher. He gives me two cups of tea and reads the share prices."

"That's not a teacher, that's a grandad." Itsuki split the orange and held half of it up without looking, and Rei took it. Itsuki was quiet for a while. He looked at the window, at the dark, at the hook by the door where nothing hung. When he spoke again his voice had gone flat. A boy reciting something he had been made to learn by heart and hated every word of.

"The Kuroda have been training people like you for four hundred years. There's a hall in the house in Nezu with a floor so old it's gone soft from feet. There's a library of things the CVD doesn't have and wouldn't understand if it did. There's people in that house who can do what you did in Jūjō without fainting on a baker." He ate a segment. Chewed. Swallowed. "And there's my father."

Rei waited. He knew that voice. He had been hearing it since they were twelve, every time Itsuki's family came up, which was as rarely as Itsuki could manage.

"I hate it there," said Itsuki. "The house. The way everyone talks quiet in the corridors like somebody upstairs is dying. The way my whole life got decided before I could walk, and then they found out I was the one who paints, and stopped bothering to decide anything." He was picking the white threads off his orange one by one. "But you want to find Gin. And you're never going to do it fast enough with an old man and a teapot. My family are the fastest teachers in Tokyo." He laughed once, without anything in it. "They're also the worst people in Tokyo. But fast."

"You'd take me there?"

"I'd ask my father to see you." Itsuki flicked the threads off his fingers. "I've never asked him for anything. Not once since I was ten. He'll probably say yes from shock. Might have a stroke. Win win."

"Itsuki." Rei put his half of the orange down. "You don't have to do this."

"I know I don't *have* to." Itsuki threw the peel at him. It hit him in the middle of the chest and stuck there for a second, a little orange snake, before it fell into his lap. "Shut up. I'm being noble. It happens maybe once every five years. Let me have it."

Then, quieter, looking at the hook by the door again.

"He fed me, Rei. Every week for eight years. He came to my exhibitions and stood in front of the worst paintings for ten minutes like they were good." His jaw moved. "He's mine too, you know. A bit."

"I know," said Rei.

"Good." Itsuki wiped his nose on the back of his wrist and was himself again, instantly, the way he could be. "Then pass the beer. The cold one. You've been holding it like a baby."

[break The wall]

They went on Monday evening, the last day of August. The heat had broken a little. A wind came down off the hills with rain in it, and the sky over Nezu had gone the colour of a bruise healing.

The Kuroda house was ten minutes' walk from the basement bar with the moon on its door. Rei had walked past its wall a dozen times on his way to Haruki without knowing what was behind it. It was a long, plain, old wall of grey plaster under a roof of dark tiles, higher than a man, running the whole length of the lane like something that had been there before the lane and expected to be there after. There was one gate in it. Heavy wood, very old and very dark, with a small door cut into it for people to step through. No nameplate. No bell. In the corner of the roof tiles, a small black camera turned its head to watch them come up the lane, slowly, the way a heron watches a pond.

Itsuki stopped in front of the gate.

He had dressed for it. A dark shirt with a real collar, ironed, though he had obviously ironed it himself because one sleeve had a crease down it like a knife. Black trousers. Proper shoes instead of the trainers he had painted on so many times they were a kind of art in themselves. He had scrubbed his hands and had not quite won. There was still blue in the creases of his knuckles. He looked like a stranger. He looked like somebody's son, which, Rei realised, was exactly the problem.

"Last chance to run away," Itsuki said.

"Are you talking to me or to you?"

"Both. Mostly me." He pulled the cigarette from behind his ear, looked at it, put it in his shirt pocket instead. He breathed in. Out. He laid his palm flat on the small door.

Before he could push, it opened from the inside. Somebody had been watching the camera.

Inside was another world.

[need ch10_kuroda_garden | Inside a high grey plaster wall in Nezu at dusk, an old Japanese estate garden with raked gravel, a dark pond, stone lanterns being lit, long low wooden houses with deep eaves, Itsuki in a dark ironed shirt and Rei in a black hooded jacket walking a stone path]

A garden, wide and dark and still. White gravel raked into long lines like the surface of water. A pond, black, with the slow orange shapes of carp sliding under it like thoughts nobody was having. Old pines trained over centuries into low twisted shapes, held up here and there by wooden crutches. Stone lanterns along a path of flat stones, and a man in a grey work kimono going from lantern to lantern with a long taper, lighting them one by one without hurrying. Beyond the garden, long low wooden houses with deep roofs, joined by covered walkways. Paper screens glowing yellow from inside.

And the city was gone.

Not quieter. Gone. The traffic, the trains, a television through somebody's window, all of it had been there a step ago and now there was only the gravel under their shoes and the small tick of the carp breaking the surface. As if the wall had cut Tokyo off with a knife.

The pressure behind Rei's eyes started at once. Not hard. Soft and steady and everywhere, like being at the bottom of a swimming pool. The air here was thick with something old and patient. He did the breathing, four in, four held, six out, and closed the door in his chest. It closed. But it was stiffer than usual, as if the latch had been painted over.

A woman in a dark kimono was waiting on the path. Sixty or so, small, grey hair pinned so tight it looked carved. She bowed to Itsuki. Low. Lower than Rei had ever seen anybody bow to anybody outside a television drama.

"Young master," she said. "Welcome home. Your father is in the old wing."

Itsuki's jaw went tight. "Don't call me that, Mrs. Fujii."

"Yes, young master," said Mrs. Fujii, with perfect serenity, and turned and led the way.

Itsuki looked at Rei. Rei looked at the gravel and did not laugh, and it took everything he had.

[break Kuroda Sōun]

The old wing was the oldest of the houses, at the far end of the garden, its roof of grey tiles grown over with green moss so thick it looked soft enough to lie on. Inside it smelled of cedar and incense and very old paper. They went down a long corridor of boards so old and so polished by feet that they had worn into gentle dips, like temple steps. Nobody else was in the corridor. Rei could hear people, though. Somewhere a door slid shut. Somewhere a woman said something in a low voice and another woman answered lower. Itsuki had been right. It was like a house where somebody upstairs was dying, and had been dying politely for four hundred years.

At the end was a room with its screens slid open onto the garden.

A man knelt alone in the middle of it.

[need ch10_kuroda_soun | Kuroda Sōun, head of the Kuroda family, late fifties, upright, severe silver hair, dark formal haori with small white crest, kneeling alone in an empty old tatami room with screens open to a dark garden, a tall black lacquered free-standing doorframe behind him]

He was in his late fifties. He sat very upright, with his hands resting on his thighs. He wore a dark formal haori, the jacket men wear over a kimono when something serious is happening, with a small white crest on each side of the chest. His hair was silver and cut short. His face was long and still, with two deep lines from the nose to the corners of the mouth, the kind of lines a face gets from forty years of not saying things. He did not look round when they came in. He was looking at the garden.

Behind him, in the empty room, stood a doorframe.

Only a doorframe. Two tall posts and a crossbeam of black lacquered wood, standing on their own in the middle of the tatami with nothing either side. No wall. No door. A thick rope of twisted white paper hung across the top, the kind tied across shrine gates to say *the ordinary world stops here*. Through the frame Rei could see the back wall of the room, plain plaster. It was the strangest object he had ever seen. It made the hair on the back of his neck stand up and stay up.

Itsuki knelt. Rei knelt beside him half a beat late, like the dead woman at the Bon dance.

"Father," said Itsuki.

"Itsuki." The voice was low and dry. Each word put down carefully, like a stone in a garden that would stay there for three hundred years. "You have paint on your hands."

"I always have paint on my hands."

"Yes."

That was all. Rei felt Itsuki go rigid beside him, every muscle, the way a cat goes rigid when you pick it up wrong.

Kuroda Sōun turned his head at last. He looked at his son for a long moment, and Rei could not read the look at all. Then he looked at Rei, and Rei felt it go in. Like cold water poured into a cup, finding the bottom and then the sides. Not unkind. Measuring.

"And this is the Uedera boy."

"Uedera Rei." Rei bowed low, forehead almost to the mat. "Thank you for seeing me."

"I know your name. I knew your brother." There was no warmth in it, and no coldness either. A fact, set down. "He came to this house once, six years ago, about a matter in Kita. He was very polite and very stubborn and he did not get what he came for. He did not lose his temper about it." A pause. "I remember that. Most people do."

Rei kept his head down. *Six years ago. Gin's first year.* Gin had never said.

"The family has an opinion about your brother," said Sōun. "It is not entirely negative."

From the way Itsuki breathed out beside him, Rei understood that from Kuroda Sōun this was practically a love letter.

"Itsuki has asked me to train you." Sōun did not look at his son as he said it. "Itsuki has not asked me for anything in ten years. So I wished to see what kind of person could make him."

Rei did not know what to say. He said the only true thing he had.

"My brother's missing. The CVD won't look for him. The police won't look for him. I can see things, and I don't know how to use what I see, and every time I use it I fall over." He made himself look up. "I need to learn fast, sir."

"Everybody needs to learn fast," said Sōun. "Nobody can."

He stood. One smooth movement, no hands, the way old men who have knelt their whole lives can stand. He walked to the black doorframe and laid his right hand flat against one post.

"The Kuroda are a family of doors. Do you know what that means?"

"No, sir."

"Then watch."

He did nothing Rei could see. He said nothing. He stood with his hand on the lacquer and his eyes half closed, and he let out one long slow breath.

The plaster wall through the frame was not there any more.

Through the frame was a garden at night. A different garden, smaller, a low hedge, a stone bench with a cracked leg. Beyond the hedge a long dark line, and above it a dark sky with three stars, and a sound came through the frame into the room. A soft rushing that rose and fell and rose. A wind came through with it, cool and wet, and it smelled of salt and seaweed and wet rope.

The sea.

The candle on the low shelf by the wall leaned over in that wind and nearly went out.

"Kamakura," said Sōun. "Fifty kilometres. Our house by the sea." He took his hand from the post, and the sea was gone, and there was plaster, and the candle stood up straight again. The smell of salt hung in the room for three breaths and faded. "Every Kuroda is born with a door inside. Most never open it. My son has kept his shut for twenty years out of pure stubbornness." Still he did not look at Itsuki. "You have something else inside you. I can see that from here. I do not yet know what."

"Will you teach me?"

Sōun looked at him for a long time. Then he looked, at last, at his son.

"I will teach him," he said. "Every morning at six, in the training hall. Wear clothes you can move in. Do not eat breakfast." He turned back to Rei. "The first morning you do not come, do not come again."

"I'll come."

"Everybody says that too." Sōun looked at his son once more. "You brought him, Itsuki. That is enough. You may go."

Itsuki went white.

Rei saw his hands close into fists on his thighs, the blue in the knuckles gone pale. He had walked through that gate in an ironed shirt. He had put his cigarette away. He had asked. And his father had said thank you for the delivery and shut the door.

"Yes, Father," said Itsuki. His voice was thin as paper.

Sōun nodded once and turned back to the garden, as if they had already gone.

Mrs. Fujii was already at the screen, waiting to lead them out.

[break The vending machine]

Outside the gate the city came back all at once, like someone had unmuted it. A car horn. A television game show. A woman two buildings over calling a child in for dinner by its full name, which is never a good sign. Itsuki walked fast with his hands jammed in his pockets, ahead of Rei, not looking back, until they were round the corner and out of sight of the camera. Then he stopped by a vending machine, leaned his back on it, and slid down it until he was sitting on the pavement with his knees up.

Rei sat down next to him.

"You okay?"

"No." Itsuki laughed, and it shook. "No. That was horrible. Did you *see* him? *You have paint on your hands.* Ten years. Ten years I don't ask that man for a single thing, and that's his opening line." He pulled the cigarette out of his shirt pocket and lit it with hands that were not steady. "Fuck. Sorry. Fuck."

"Don't be sorry."

"I'm not sorry. I'm saying fuck." He blew smoke at the sky. "Rei, listen. You don't owe me for this, okay? Don't go all..." He waved the cigarette at Rei's whole face. "Don't do the face."

"What face?"

"The *I'll repay this debt with my life* face. The Gin face. You've got it on right now."

Rei touched his own face, without meaning to. Itsuki laughed again, properly this time, and choked on the smoke.

"I'm not going," Rei said. "Not if he talks to you like that. I'll find somebody else. I'll..."

"Shut up. You're going. Six o'clock, no breakfast, and you're going to say thank you and bow and do whatever horrible thing they tell you." Itsuki put his head back against the glowing machine. Its red and white light lay across half his face. He was quiet. "You know what's stupid?" he said at last. "I wanted him to ask me. Just a bit. To come in with you." He closed his eyes. "I'd have said no. I just wanted to say it to his face." He breathed smoke out. "Don't tell anyone I said that."

"I won't."

"Especially not him."

"I'll take it to my grave."

Itsuki opened one eye. "Don't say grave. Not this month. Say something else."

"I'll take it to the konbini."

"Better."

Rei got up and fed coins into the machine and pressed the button for two cans of hot corn soup, because Haruki had taught him that at one in the morning in a konbini, and some lessons you keep. The cans clunked down. He gave one to Itsuki. Itsuki looked at the can, and then at him, for a second longer than he needed to, the cigarette burning down forgotten in his other hand. Then he grinned his crooked grin and pulled the ring with his thumb.

"Six in the morning," he said. "No breakfast. In that house. You're going to die."

"Probably."

"Text me after. Every day. I want to hear all the horrible parts."

"Every day," said Rei, and they knocked the cans against each other, and drank, and the soup was too hot and burned their tongues, and neither of them said so.
