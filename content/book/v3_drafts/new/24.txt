#title Ink

The Sigil Room was on the seventh floor, at the end of a corridor where the strip lights had been replaced, one by one over the years, with old paper lamps that somebody had hung on hooks and never taken down. The door was dark wood, very old, much older than the building. On it someone had painted, in black ink gone brown with age, a single character. 印. *Seal.*

Rei had walked past it a dozen times since December. He had never knocked.

He knocked now, on a grey Thursday at the end of January, with Gin's book under his arm and a page marked in it with a train ticket.

"It's open," said a voice. "It's always open. Nobody ever comes in."

[break Uruma]

[need ch24_sigil_room | An old workroom inside an office building, every wall covered floor to ceiling in sheets of paper painted with black seals and circles of tiny writing, long tables of brushes and inkstones and pots of salt, paper lamps, a very tall thin man with a tattooed shaved head lying back in an old black tattoo chair reading a manga]

The Sigil Room was long and narrow and smelled of ink and old paper and green tea. Every wall was covered, from the floor to the ceiling, in sheets of paper. Thousands of them, pinned up, overlapping, curling at the corners. On every sheet, in black ink, a seal. Circles inside circles. Lines of tiny writing running round the edges. Shapes like keys and gates and knots and birds. Some were new and black and wet-looking. Some were so old the paper had gone the colour of tea and the ink had faded to grey. Down the middle of the room ran two long tables covered in inkstones, brushes in jars, pots of coarse salt, rolls of white paper, and a great many empty cups.

At the far end, under a paper lamp, stood an old black tattoo chair, the kind with a padded rest for the arm. A tray of needles beside it, under a cloth.

And in the chair, lying back with his feet up, reading a manga and eating rice crackers out of the bag, was a man.

He was very tall and very thin, all long arms and long legs, folded into the chair like a heron. His head was shaved, and the whole of his scalp was covered in tattoos, fine black lines and small shapes and tiny writing, so that it looked like a map of a country nobody had found yet. Both his ears were full of silver rings, from the top to the lobe. His fingernails were painted black, and chipped. He was perhaps forty. It was hard to tell. He had a long pale gentle face, and when he looked up from his manga, his eyes were very dark and very still and very amused, as if he had just remembered a joke and decided not to tell it yet.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, look. It's the little one."

"I'm Uedera Rei."

"I know who you are. You've got his walk." The man swung his long legs off the chair and sat up. "Uruma. I make the seals in this building, and I've made most of the marks on your brother, and I've heard about you for six years. *Rei passed his exam. Rei burned the rice. Rei sat up all night again.*" He giggled. It was a strange giggle, high and quiet, like a child behind a curtain. "Your brother is the worst client I ever had. I told him. I said, Gin-kun, you are not a person any more, you are a walking notebook. Next time you want a seal you'll have to get it on your eyelids." He giggled again. "He said he'd think about it. He would have, too."

From behind a tall stack of paper at the other end of the room, a woman's voice said, "Is that the Uedera boy?"

"It is. Don't come out, Tomoe-san, you'll frighten him." Uruma leaned toward Rei and said in a loud whisper, "Tomoe. She does the paper seals. I do the paper seals and the skin. She's much better than me at paper and I'm not allowed to say so."

"I heard that," said the stack of paper.

"Why skin?" said Rei. "Why here? I thought tattoos were done in a shop."

Uruma looked at him with something like pity.

"A tattoo *is* a seal," he said. "It's just a seal that lives on you instead of on paper. A seal on paper you use once, and it's spent. A seal on skin you have forever." He waved a long hand at the walls. "You don't take a seal out of the Sigil Room to a shop above a noodle bar in Nippori. Somebody tried that, years ago, before my time. They're still finding bits of him." He giggled. "So I started doing them here. In this chair. Twenty years. Nobody ever told me to stop." He held out his hand, palm up. "Show me what you've got in that book."

[break The latch]

Rei opened Gin's book at the page with the train ticket in it.

It was a page about closing. Gin had drawn, very carefully, in black ink, an old-fashioned iron door latch, the kind on the gates of old temples, a curved bar dropping into a hook. Round it, so finely you had to look twice to see them, ran the lines of an old seal. In the margin, in Gin's small exact handwriting, *Configuration. Holds the door when you can't. Always on.* And under that, smaller, one word. *His.*

Rei had read that word fifty times. He had decided it meant him.

Uruma bent over the page so close that his nose almost touched it. He looked at it for a long time without blinking. He did not giggle.

"He drew this for you," he said at last. "Three years ago. He came in and showed me. He asked if I could do it, if it was ever needed." He traced the latch in the air above the page with one black fingernail. "I said yes. He said, *not yet. Maybe never. I'm managing.*" He looked up. "Are you managing?"

Rei thought about the folded building in Higashi-Jūjō. About lying on the tiles of the arcade at Kamiya. About the door in his chest that would not shut, and the nights when he could feel every person in the building where he lived.

"No," he said.

"Then it's needed." Uruma closed the book very gently and gave it back. "Where?"

Rei held out his left wrist. The inside. Where the skin is thin and you can see the blue of the veins.

"Where I'll see it," he said.

[break The needle]

It took three hours.

It hurt more than he expected and less than he feared. A sharp, hot, scratching hurt, like being drawn on very hard with a pin. On the bones of the wrist it was worse. Uruma hummed while he worked, an old song from Okinawa about a boat, and every now and then he giggled at something only he could see. Tomoe brought tea, once, a small round woman in her sixties with ink on her cheek, and looked at the wrist and said *good*, and went back behind her paper.

"He sat in this chair," said Uruma, without looking up, at about the second hour. "Your brother. First time he was twenty-two. Just joined. Wanted a seal on his arm so he could reach people when his phone was dead. Very serious. Very quiet." The needle buzzed. "After that he came twice a year. Sometimes three times. Always sat in this chair with his eyes half closed like it was a hot bath. Always asked how I was. Nobody else up here asks."

"When was the last time?"

Uruma stopped the needle. He thought about it. For once he did not giggle.

"July," he said. "He didn't want anything new. He wanted me to look at the big one. The one in the middle of his chest." He wiped the wrist with a cloth that smelled sharply of alcohol. "Severance. The strongest seal I've ever put on anybody. It cuts a thing out of a body. But it isn't like yours. It doesn't stay on. It sleeps, and you have to wake it, every time, and waking it takes everything you've got." He started the needle again. "He asked me how fast a man could wake it. If something came at him. If he was tired." Uruma was quiet for a while. "I said it depends how frightened he is. He laughed. He said, *then I'll have to be very frightened.*"

Rei did not say anything. He watched the paper lamp over the chair.

"Then he paid," said Uruma, "and asked me how I was, and went home. And that was the last time I saw him."

[break What it did]

When it was finished, Uruma wrapped the wrist in clear film and told him how to wash it and not to scratch it and not to go in a bath for two weeks, which upset Rei more than anything else he said.

Then he told him to close his eyes and open the door in his chest.

He did. He did it as Sōun had taught him. A finger's width. The cold came in, the faint everyday cold that was always there, in every building in Tokyo, the background hum of the veil.

And on his wrist, under the film, the new mark went cold.

Not painful. A clear, sharp cold, like a drop of water from a tap. And then he felt something else. A weight. Somewhere in his chest, behind the breastbone, where the door was. A small iron bar, dropping into a hook, with a click he did not hear but felt in his teeth.

The door was still open a finger's width. But now it could not open any further. Not unless he chose. Not unless he lifted the latch himself.

He opened his eyes. He was breathing hard.

"It works," he said.

"Of course it works," said Uruma. "He drew it, and I put it in, and you meant it. That's all a mark is." He started cleaning his needles. "It'll go cold whenever something's near. Every time. Forever. You'll get used to it."

"Does it cost anything? The book says they all cost something."

Uruma looked at him with his dark still amused eyes.

"You'll never be able to throw that door wide open in one go again," he said. "Not without lifting the latch first. It'll always take you one breath longer than it used to." He shrugged. "Most days that's a gift. One day, maybe, it'll be the breath you don't have." He went back to his needles, and giggled once, softly, at nothing.

[break Room Nine]

"Now," said Uruma, standing up and unfolding himself to his full height, which was very nearly the ceiling. "You can carry this."

He put a wooden box into Rei's arms. It was heavy, and inside it, in neat rolls tied with string, were sheets of fresh white paper covered in black seals.

"Once a month I go down to Containment and change the papers on the rooms," he said. "Room Nine's due." He looked at Rei sideways. "I'm told there's a boy in Room Nine who draws through the door all day and won't stop talking."

Two floors under the street, in the long corridor with the polished floor, Moriya let them into the little anteroom outside Room Nine where the seals could be changed through a slot. Itsuki was sitting cross-legged on the white bed with a grey pad on his knees and charcoal on his fingers. He looked up at the window and saw Rei, and grinned, and then saw the tall shaved-headed man beside him and stopped grinning.

Uruma took one of the old papers off the wall of the anteroom, and slid a new one into the frame, and pressed it flat with both hands.

The intercom clicked.

"Excuse me," said Itsuki's voice, tinny and polite. "Are you the one who makes those?"

Uruma looked up. "Which?"

"The papers. On my walls. All round me." Through the little window, Itsuki was holding up his grey pad. On it, in charcoal, he had copied one of the seals from the wall of his room. Perfectly. Every circle, every line of tiny writing, every curve. "I've been looking at them for seven weeks. There's nothing else to look at. And they're..." He stopped. "Sorry. This is going to sound stupid."

"Say it anyway," said Uruma.

"They're beautiful," said Itsuki. "That one. The one by the door. The way the outside circle doesn't quite close, and the line goes in instead of round, like water going down a drain. It's the best drawing I've ever seen. Who drew it?"

Uruma stood very still with a sheet of white paper in his long hands.

"I did," he said. "Fourteen years ago."

"It's beautiful," said Itsuki again.

Uruma looked at him through the little window for a long time.

"Twenty years I've been making seals in this building," he said at last, quietly. "People look at them and ask what they're for. What they keep out. What they hold in. How strong they are." He put the paper down on the shelf, carefully. "You're the first person who's ever looked at one and asked who drew it."

He giggled. It was a different giggle from all the others. Softer. Almost shy.

"I'll come back next month," he said. "Keep drawing them. I'll tell you which ones are wrong."

"None of them are wrong," said Itsuki. "That's what I like about them."

On the way back up in the lift, Uruma did not say anything for four floors. Then he looked down at Rei's wrapped wrist and said, "Your brother brought me nothing but trouble for six years." He smiled his long gentle smile. "And now his little one's brought me an artist."

Under the film, the latch was cold, very faintly, the whole way up.
