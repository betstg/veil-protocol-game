#title Losses

Afterwards, when people asked him what it was like, Rei said he did not remember.

That was a lie. He remembered all of it. He had no door, and so everything came in, and everything that came in stayed. He remembered it like a house he had grown up in. Every room. Every stair that creaked. He just did not have the words for it, and when he tried to find them his throat closed, and so he said he did not remember, and people let him.

This is what he remembered.

[break The white one]

The white one came first. Out of the shapes on the grey sea, the largest that had come close, all at once, as if it had been waiting at the back for the others to clear the way.

Thin and tall and endless, like a crack of light under a door, and it did not come across the beach. It came *up*. Out of the grey sand, out of the weeds, out of the old rails, out of every shadow in the yard at once, and everyone it touched took a step back.

Not just a step. Everyone it touched remembered the worst thing they had ever been afraid of. All at once. In the body. In the knees and the stomach and the back of the neck.

Rei felt it go through the yard like cold water through a sponge.

The hollow ones who had woken up, the woman with the handkerchief, the university students, the man in the work shirt, a hundred others standing dazed among the rails with their flowers dropped in the weeds, screamed. All at once. And ran. Not toward the gate. Toward the freight cars. Toward anything with walls. Into the open doors of the rusted cars on the curved tracks, crowding in, dozens of them, hiding, like children under a bed.

The white thing smiled without a face.

And leaned on the freight cars.

They went over like dominoes. Rei felt the weight shift before it happened. A whole line of them. Six cars, stacked two high on the curved track, rusted through, full of people. The bottom ones buckled. The top ones tipped. A long scream of metal, slow and enormous, like a ship going down.

And they stopped.

In the air. Halfway over. Tilted at an angle no freight car should ever be, with rust raining off them like red snow, and the people inside screaming.

Majima was standing under them.

[break Ceilings]

He had come from the far side of the yard. Rei had not seen him move. He was standing on the curved track with his feet planted and his arms up and his hands open, palms flat against nothing, as he had stood in the doorway of the Hotel Rosa with a cracked mirror hanging over Rei's head. The old dog was pressed against his legs. Her lips were drawn back from her yellow teeth. She was not growling. She was holding on.

The six freight cars hung over him.

His face was grey. The vein stood out on his forehead like a cord. His knees were bending, very slowly, a centimetre at a time.

His jaw barely moved. "Out." Not loud. "Everybody. Out. Now."

They came out. Climbing. Jumping. Falling. Out of the tilted doors onto the weeds, onto the rails, scrambling away on their hands and knees. The woman with the handkerchief. The man in the work shirt. A girl in a pink hoodie, who had woken up in the weeds and run with all the others and crawled into the nearest dark place, and was crawling out of it again now, on her hands and knees, and stopped, and looked up, and saw who was holding the roof over her head.

Mika's mouth opened. "Majima-san."

"Get out from under it, you stupid girl," said Majima. His voice was quite calm. "Go on. Go to hell. You told me to once. Go on."

She went.

Rei was running. He did not remember starting. He ran across the yard through the weeds toward the line of hanging cars with everything coming in, and he felt the weight of them. All of it. Six freight cars full of rust and old iron and nothing now, nobody now, everyone out. And under them, one man. Holding.

*Holding a ceiling's easy,* Majima had said, on the stairs of the Hotel Rosa.

It was not easy. Rei could feel it. He could feel the man's heart, the way he felt everything now. Going much too fast, and then much too slow, and then too fast again, like a man running up a hill that does not end.

He got there. He had nothing to push with. He could not move a pencil. He put both hands up beside Majima's anyway, flat against the rusted underside of the nearest car, and he did the only thing he had, the thing he had done on a walkway in Tabata. He opened the empty frame in his chest toward the man beside him and let something go out. Not strength. He did not have strength. Only the feeling of somebody standing beside you who will not leave.

"Let go," he said. "I've got it. Majima-san. Let go. Get out from under."

Majima turned his head and looked at him.

The dark eyes that never stopped working had stopped. They were looking at Rei and nothing else.

"Don't take cases that interest you," said Majima. His voice was very quiet. "I told you that."

Rei shoved harder against the weight. "Let *go*..."

"Feed the dog," said Majima. He breathed. Once. "Chicken skin. Not the stick."

And he let go.

Not of the cars. Of Rei. He took one hand off the weight and turned it toward Rei and *pushed*, without touching him, the way he had once held up a ceiling without touching it, and Rei went backwards ten metres through the air and landed in the weeds on his back.

The freight cars came down.

All six. A long slow roar of metal. A cloud of red rust going up into the white light from the river.

Then nothing.

[break The dog]

When the rust settled, the old dog was lying on the rail beside the wreck with her head on her paws.

She had not been under it. He had pushed her too. Rei saw the marks in the gravel where she had gone, ten metres, the same as him, and come back. She had come back and lain down as close as she could get.

She did not whine. She did not bark. She lay with her head on her paws and her old flat eyes on the twisted metal, keeping watch, as she had kept watch on the gateway.

Mika came and sat down beside her in the weeds.

Neither of them moved again for a long time.

[break Tanaka]

Rei got up onto his hands and knees in the weeds. His back hurt. There was gravel in the palms of both hands and rust in his mouth.

Three metres away, between two rails, a heavy man in a dark jacket was kneeling on the ground with a young man held against his chest.

Rei had seen him come over the fence behind Kurosawa, with the canvas bag. Tanaka. Kurosawa had told him to stay with the kids, and Rei had thought he meant the Kuroda children, and then he saw that he meant everybody under about thirty, which on that yard was most of them.

The young man was perhaps twenty. He wore the blue overalls of a warehouse somewhere, with a name sewn on the pocket that Rei could not read in that light, and his eyes were black from edge to edge. He was fighting. Not like a person fights. Like a cat in a bag. All of him at once, elbows and heels and the back of his head, and none of it doing anything, because the man holding him had both arms round him from behind and was simply not letting go. Tanaka's face was calm. There was blood on his chin from somebody's elbow.

A Kuroda woman of about sixty stood over the pair of them with a seal paper in her hand. Mitsu-san. Rei had seen her at the gate an hour before, opening doorframes in the weeds. She was waiting with the paper held out, as a nurse waits with a needle.

"Now?" she said.

"Not yet," said Tanaka.

"It'll tear him if I do it now."

"I know. That's why not yet."

Rei could feel the thing in the young man. Small and hard and hungry, curled behind his breastbone like a fist. One of the little ones Kishin let in to ride. It was frightened. Rei had not expected that. Under the hunger it was frightened out of its wits, of the salt and the light from the river and the big calm man who would not let go.

And Rei could feel what Tanaka was doing. It was not much. It was the gift Moriya had, and the mender, the one that works inside a body. He was not using it to hurt. He was slowing the young man's heart, a little, and the young man's breath, a little, and keeping him still. Not asleep. Awake, and held, and unable to run. And with the young man held still, the thing inside him could not run either. It had to stay. It had to listen.

"There you are," said Tanaka, very quietly, to neither of them and to both. "All right. I'm not going anywhere. Neither are you. What's the matter?"

The young man's mouth opened. What came out of it was not his voice. It was thin and furious and very young.

*Hungry.*

"I know. What for?"

*Hungry. Hungry. HUNGRY.*

"Yes," said Tanaka. "I heard. What for?"

There was a silence. Rei felt the small hard thing turn the question over, like a dog turning over a shoe it does not know what to do with. Nobody had ever asked it that.

*He doesn't eat,* it said at last, in the young man's mouth. *He doesn't eat. Since the spring. He goes to work and comes home and drinks her tea and doesn't eat. So I eat that. The not eating. It's all there is.* The black eyes rolled. *It's not enough. It's never enough. It's so cold in here.*

The young man was crying. His own tears, out of the black eyes. Rei felt him hear every word of it. He could not stop listening. Tanaka was not letting him.

"All right," said Tanaka. He did not let go with either arm. He bent his head, and got the strap of the canvas bag in his teeth, and pulled it round onto his knee, and opened the flap with his chin. Inside there were bandages and a torch and a blood pressure cuff and four rice balls wrapped in cling film, the plain kind with a pickled plum in the middle, made at home, slightly squashed.

He got one out with two fingers of his left hand without letting go of anything else, and got the film off it with his thumb, and held it up in front of the young man's mouth.

"Eat that," he said.

The young man looked at it.

"Both of you," said Tanaka. "Go on. I made it this afternoon. The rice is a bit hard. I'm not good with the cooker in that flat."

The young man took a bite. Then another. He ate it crying, with rice on his chin, held from behind by a stranger in a railway yard at two in the morning. He ate the whole thing. Tanaka got the second one out and held that up too.

Rei felt the thing inside taste it.

It did not know what it was. It had never tasted anything. It had only ever had the not eating, the cold empty place where a meal should be. And now there was rice in it, warm from a man's bag, and the sour red shock of the plum in the middle, and salt. It did not know what to do with that. It went very still behind the young man's breastbone, and stayed still, and Rei felt the hard fist of it loosen. Finger by finger.

*Oh,* it said.

"Yes," said Tanaka.

*Oh. That's what it was for.*

"That's what it was for," said Tanaka. "He's going to be all right now. He's going to eat breakfast. You can't stay. You know that. There's nothing for you here any more." He waited. "Will you go?"

A long pause.

*Yes,* said the thing, quite small.

Tanaka looked up at Mitsu-san and nodded.

She bent and laid the seal paper on the young man's chest, flat, over the overalls, over the name on the pocket. She did not have to push it. Rei felt the thing come up through the paper of its own accord and go, like breath on a cold window, out and away across the yard toward the grey river, and down. The young man's eyes went brown. He sagged forward in Tanaka's arms and was sick in the weeds, rice and all, and then sat back against the big man's chest and started to shake.

"That's normal," said Tanaka. "You'll be all right."

He let go, at last, and the young man sat in the weeds on his own with his arms round his knees. Tanaka got the third rice ball out of the bag and put it in his hand and closed his fingers round it.

Then Tanaka sat back on his heels between the rails and put both hands flat on his thighs and breathed. His face had gone a bad colour. Rei could feel his heart going much too fast. It had cost him. Holding three of them still at once, himself and the boy and the thing, all the way through, and listening.

"It might have lied," said Mitsu-san, watching the river. "To get out. They do."

"It might," said Tanaka. He wiped the blood off his chin with the back of his hand. "We'll see. There's another one by the signal box. Bring your papers." He looked round and saw Rei on his hands and knees in the gravel, and nodded to him, once, as if they had been introduced. "Uedera. Can you stand? If you can stand, stand. If the Captain sees you on the ground he'll want to read you, and he's busy."

Rei got up.

[break The bright one]

The small bright thing with a thousand mouths came across the grey beach laughing. It was not big. It was fast. It went through the yard like a swarm of starlings, everywhere at once, and wherever it went, it ate.

Not bodies. Noise. Thought. Feeling. Everything that was loud in a person, it wanted, and it took, and the people it took from went quiet and grey and sat down in the weeds with their mouths open.

And the loudest thing in the yard was Kato.

Rei felt it turn. All thousand mouths. Toward the boy by the gate with his knight in his fist and his head full of everybody's thoughts at once, terrified and open and shouting. Kato, who thought very loudly. Kato, whom Watanabe had called a nice noise.

It went for him.

And Shimizu stepped in front of him.

She did not hurry. Shimizu never hurried. She stepped between Kato and the swarm with her hands empty and her face completely still, as it went when she was working out every way something could go wrong.

She was reading it.

Rei felt her do it. Not with a gift. With ten years of looking at things until she saw where they would break. She was looking at the swarm of mouths as she had looked at a phone box in Jūjō and a flat in Nakano and a temple bell in Takinogawa, and she was finding the seam. The one place in all that hunger where it was joined together. The place where, if you put something in exactly the right spot, it would come apart.

She found it.

Rei felt her find it. He felt the small cold satisfaction of it, the same satisfaction she had felt every time she had told him his tie was wrong. Exact. Certain.

The seam was in the middle of it. In the very centre of the swarm. Where all thousand mouths met.

Shimizu did not turn round. "Kato. Stay behind me."

Kato did not move.

Shimizu took a seal paper out of her pocket. One of Uruma's. A small one. White, with a single word written on it in a careful hand. Rei could read it, with Uruma's alphabet. *Shut.*

She could have sent it in from where she stood. She had done that at the temple, with her hands, from across the hall. Rei felt her measure it. Felt her see that it would not be exact enough from here, through all that moving. Felt her decide.

She walked into the swarm.

[break Shimizu]

Rei felt all of it.

He felt the mouths close on her. He felt them eat. Her noise. Her thought. Her feeling. Everything that was loud in her. He felt them find out how little of it there was. Shimizu had never been loud. Shimizu had held everything very still, her whole life, like a cup of water full to the brim carried across a room.

He felt her keep walking.

Through the mouths. Through the eating. To the seam. To the very centre, where it was joined. He felt her put the paper there. Flat. With her palm. And push it in, with the gift she almost never used, the whole of it, all at once.

*Shut.*

The swarm came apart.

It went to pieces in the air like a flock of birds when a hawk goes through it. Scattering. Screaming. A thousand small bright mouths flying apart in every direction and going out, one by one, like sparks from a bonfire going up into the dark.

Shimizu was standing where it had been.

She stood for a moment, very straight, with her hand still held out flat in front of her, as if there were still a wall there to read.

Then she sat down. In the weeds. Carefully. As she sat down at her desk every morning on the Operations floor.

Kato got to her first. He went down on his knees beside her and did not touch her, because he never touched anyone, and then he did. He put his hand on her arm.

Rei got there. He did not know how. He knelt in the weeds on her other side.

Her face was grey. Not ash grey. Not Watanabe's grey. Just tired. Very, very tired. As if all of it had been eaten, all the noise, all the stillness, all the holding, and there was nothing left in her but the shape of where it had been.

She looked at Rei.

She looked at his shirt. The clean one, filthy now, with blood on the collar and rust on the sleeves.

"Better," she said. Her voice was almost nothing. "That one's better."

Rei took her hand without meaning to. "Shimizu-san..."

"Don't," she said. "You'll make it worse. You always talk." Her other hand found Kato's, on her arm. Her fingers closed on it. Once. She was not looking at Rei any more. "Kato."

"I'm here."

"You're not too loud." A breath. "You never were. I should have said. In Nakano. I looked. For one second. I should have said."

"You said," said Kato. His voice had gone completely. "Just now. You said."

Her hand stopped moving.

Kato did not let go of it. He knelt in the weeds with his head bowed over her and her hand in his, and he did not lift his head again until morning.

[break The grey one]

The grey one came last.

The great slow shape like a hill of wet ash. It did not come fast. It did not come laughing. It came as grief comes, slowly, heavily, without any hurry at all, because it has all the time in the world and it knows you cannot outrun it.

It did not come for anyone.

It came for the door.

The great white door painted on the side of the rusted freight car, with the street inside it, the lanterns and the bath house and the dog asleep in the road. The picture Rei was still holding up in every dead mind in the yard. And in front of it, a boy with white paint to his elbows and his whole body shaking, and another boy behind him with a silver ring, and an old man in a dark haori standing beside them, looking at it.

The grey shape lay down on the door.

That was all it did. It did not attack. It did not push. It simply lay down on top of it, as grief lies down on top of you in the night, its whole slow enormous weight, and pressed.

And the picture began to go out.

Rei felt it. Through the open frame in his own chest. He felt the street inside the door begin to fade from the minds of the dead. The lanterns. The steam. The dog in the road. Fading, one light after another, all down the beach. Not because anybody was taking it away. Because nobody could remember, any more, under that weight, why they had wanted to go. What was the point. Nobody was waiting. It would be cold. It would be so far.

The stream of lights faltered. Slowed. A few of them turned back toward the low white table.

Itsuki screamed.

Not with fear. With effort. He had his paint-white hands flat on the freight car and he was painting again, over the top of what he had painted, faster, harder, the lanterns brighter, the steam thicker, a woman in the doorway of the bath house now, waving, *I see it, I see it, it's there, it's THERE*, and it was not enough, the weight was too much.

Sei was crying. He had both arms round his brother from behind and his face pressed against Itsuki's back and he was crying out loud, like a child.

And Sōun stood up straight.

[break The gate]

He looked at the painting, and at his sons, and at the lights faltering on the beach.

Rei saw his face. The long still closed face. And for the first time, it was not closed. Every curtain in the house was open. It was the face of a man looking at his two sons holding a picture against the end of the world.

He did not step in front of the painting. He stepped past it.

Down the beach. Into the grey. Into the stream of lights going home, among them, with his arms open. And a Kuroda opens doors.

He opened one. Rei felt him do it. The largest he had ever opened. Not from the yard to anywhere in Tokyo. From the end of the grey beach, from the edge of the fog, straight through, to the street in Itsuki's painting. The real one. The lanterns, the bath house, the dog in the road. Close. Next to them. So that the dead did not have to walk across the far shore past a woman with a teapot and a fog that went on for ever. They only had to take one step.

*Here,* he said, to every light on the beach. *It is right here. My son painted it. I have opened it. Go home.*

The grey shape pressed down on him.

The whole of it. All that slow enormous weight. On one old man in a dark haori with his arms open, holding a door the size of a district.

And the door held.

It did not close. It did not even narrow. It stood at the end of the grey beach, a black lacquered frame twenty metres high, and through it the street was there, warm, lit, close enough to smell the bath house steam. And the lights went through it, past the old man, around him and over him, thicker now, brighter, as if somebody had reminded them.

The grey shape lifted.

Slowly. Heavily. Like a cloud lifting off a hill. It rose off the door and off the old man, and drifted back toward the river, and sank into the grey sea, and did not come out again.

Sōun stood in front of the door for a moment longer.

Then he let the door go, very slowly, when the last light had gone through it, and turned round, and walked back up the beach toward his sons.

He did not get there. His knees went on the grey sand.

Itsuki got to him first. Sei caught both of them. They went down together on the edge of the beach, the three of them, and the old man lay with his head in his younger son's lap and his hand in his elder son's hand and his eyes open, looking up at Itsuki's face.

"It was a very good painting," he said. His voice was a thread. "I should like to sleep for a week."

He closed his eyes.

Itsuki put his ear to his father's chest, and stayed there, and then lifted his head and looked at Rei across the weeds with his face wet and white and paint all over it, and said, "He's breathing. He's breathing. He's asleep." And then he put his face down on his father's haori and cried like a child.

[break What Rei did]

Rei was kneeling in the weeds between Shimizu and the wreck of the freight cars.

He could feel both of them. Through the empty frame where his door had been. Two places in the yard where something had been, and was not any more. Two shapes in the dark like the dent in Watanabe's chair. Warm, still. Fading.

He did not cry. He could not. Everything was coming in, and there was no room left for anything to go out.

He went to the old dog.

She did not lift her head when he knelt down beside her in the weeds by the wreck. Mika was sitting on her other side with her knees up and her pink hood up and her face hidden.

Rei put his hand on the dog's back. Her fur was warm and full of rust.

"Kinako," he said.

The dog's ear moved. Just one. The torn one.

"He said chicken skin," said Rei. "Not the stick." His voice did not work properly. "I'll remember."

Then he stood up.

On the grey beach, the small woman in the grey cardigan was looking at him. And at the yard. At the two still shapes. At the dog by the wreck, and a telepath kneeling in the weeds holding a dead woman's hand, and two brothers holding their sleeping father at the edge of the sand.

She looked satisfied.

*There,* she said, in the air, in the weeds, in every ear. *There. You see? Everyone who holds something up gets crushed by it in the end. Everyone. It's the only rule there is.* She turned, at last, to the body standing beside Rei, with two people in it. *Now. You. Old enemy.*

And she came up the beach.
