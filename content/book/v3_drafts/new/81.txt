#title Losses

Afterwards, when people asked him what it was like, Rei said he did not remember.

That was a lie. He remembered all of it. He had no door, and so everything came in, and everything that came in stayed. He remembered it the way you remember a house you grew up in. Every room. Every stair that creaked. He just did not have the words for it, and when he tried to find them his throat closed, and so he said he did not remember, and people let him.

This is what he remembered.

[break Fear]

The white one came first.

Thin and tall and endless, like a crack of light under a door, and it did not come across the beach. It came *up*. Out of the grey sand, out of the weeds, out of the old rails, out of every shadow in the yard at once, and everyone it touched took a step back.

Not just a step. Everyone it touched remembered the worst thing they had ever been afraid of. All at once. In the body. In the knees and the stomach and the back of the neck.

Rei felt it go through the yard like cold water through a sponge.

The hollow ones who had woken up, the woman with the handkerchief, the university students, the man in the work shirt, a hundred others standing dazed among the rails with their flowers dropped in the weeds, screamed. All at once. And ran. Not toward the gate. Toward the freight cars. Toward anything with walls. Into the open doors of the rusted cars on the curved tracks, crowding in, dozens of them, hiding, the way children hide under a bed.

The white thing smiled without a face.

And leaned on the freight cars.

They went over like dominoes. Rei felt the weight shift before it happened. A whole line of them. Six cars, stacked two high on the curved track, rusted through, full of people. The bottom ones buckled. The top ones tipped. A long scream of metal, slow and enormous, like a ship going down.

And they stopped.

In the air. Halfway over. Tilted at an angle no freight car should ever be, with rust raining off them like red snow, and the people inside screaming.

Majima was standing under them.

[break Ceilings]

He had come from the far side of the yard. Rei had not seen him move. He was standing on the curved track with his feet planted and his arms up and his hands open, palms flat against nothing, the way he had stood in the doorway of the Hotel Rosa with a cracked mirror hanging over Rei's head. The old dog was pressed against his legs. Her lips were drawn back from her yellow teeth. She was not growling. She was holding on.

The six freight cars hung over him.

His face was grey. The vein stood out on his forehead like a cord. His knees were bending, very slowly, a centimetre at a time.

His jaw barely moved. "Out." Not loud. "Everybody. Out. Now."

They came out. Climbing. Jumping. Falling. Out of the tilted doors onto the weeds, onto the rails, scrambling away on their hands and knees. The woman with the handkerchief. The man in the work shirt. A girl in a pink hoodie, who had woken up in the weeds and run with all the others and crawled into the nearest dark place, and was crawling out of it again now, on her hands and knees, and stopped, and looked up, and saw who was holding the roof over her head.

Mika's mouth opened. "Majima-san."

"Get out from under it, you stupid girl," said Majima. His voice was quite calm. "Go on. Go to hell. You told me to once. Go on."

She went.

Rei was running. He did not remember starting. He did not think to step. He ran across the yard through the weeds toward the line of hanging cars with all six hands open and everything coming in, and he felt the weight of them. All of it. Six freight cars full of rust and old iron and nothing now, nobody now, everyone out. And under them, one man. Holding.

*Seven,* Majima had said, on the stairs of the Hotel Rosa. *Holding a ceiling's easy.*

It was not easy. Rei could feel it. He could feel the man's heart. Psychometabolism. The hand that reads the body. He could feel the heart going much too fast, and then much too slow, and then too fast again, like a man running up a hill that does not end.

He got there. He put both hands up beside Majima's. He pushed with everything he had, with the hand that moves things, and felt the weight shift, just a little, just enough.

"Let go," he said. "I've got it. Majima-san. Let go. Get out from under."

Majima turned his head and looked at him.

The dark eyes that never stopped working had stopped. They were looking at Rei and nothing else.

"Don't take cases that interest you," said Majima. His voice was very quiet. "I told you that."

Rei shoved harder against the weight. "Let *go*..."

"In my jacket," said Majima. "Inside pocket. You know where." He breathed. Once. "Tell her ceilings."

And he let go.

Not of the cars. Of Rei. He took one hand off the weight and put it flat on Rei's chest and *pushed*, the way you push a child back from the edge of a pond, and Rei went backwards ten metres through the air and landed in the weeds on his back.

The freight cars came down.

All six. A long slow roar of metal. A cloud of red rust going up into the white light from the river.

Then nothing.

[break The dog]

When the rust settled, the old dog was lying on the rail beside the wreck with her head on her paws.

She had not been under it. He had pushed her too. Rei saw the marks in the gravel where she had gone, ten metres, the same as him, and come back. She had come back and lain down as close as she could get.

She did not whine. She did not bark. She lay with her head on her paws and her old flat eyes on the twisted metal, keeping watch, the way she had kept watch on the gateway.

Mika came and sat down beside her in the weeds.

Neither of them moved again for a long time.

[break Hunger]

Rei did not have time to stand up.

The small bright thing with a thousand mouths came across the grey beach laughing. It was not big. It was the smallest of them. It was fast. It went through the yard like a swarm of starlings, everywhere at once, and wherever it went, it ate.

Not bodies. Noise. Thought. Feeling. Everything that was loud in a person, it wanted, and it took, and the people it took from went quiet and grey and sat down in the weeds with their mouths open.

And the loudest thing in the yard was Kato.

Rei felt it turn. All thousand mouths. Toward the boy by the gate with his king in his fist and his head full of everybody's thoughts at once, terrified and open and shouting. Kato, who thought very loudly. Kato, whom Watanabe had called a nice noise.

It went for him.

And Shimizu stepped in front of him.

She did not hurry. Shimizu never hurried. She stepped between Kato and the swarm with Kuma at her heel and her hands empty and her face completely still, the way it went when she was reading the structure of something, the way a building's load ran down through its walls.

She was reading it.

Rei felt her do it. Clairsentience. The hand that reads where a thing will break. She was looking at the swarm of mouths the way she had looked at a phone box in Jūjō and a flat in Itabashi and a temple bell in Takinogawa, and she was finding the seam. The one place in all that hunger where it was joined together. The place where, if you put something in exactly the right spot, it would come apart.

She found it.

Rei felt her find it. He felt the small cold satisfaction of it, the same satisfaction she had felt every time she had told him his tie was wrong. Exact. Certain.

The seam was in the middle of it. In the very centre of the swarm. Where all thousand mouths met.

Shimizu did not turn round. "Kato. Hold Kuma."

Kato grabbed the red dog's collar with both hands.

Shimizu took a seal paper out of her pocket. One of Aoi's. A small one. White, with a single word written on it in a careful hand. Rei could read it, with Uruma's alphabet. *Shut.*

She walked into the swarm.

[break Shimizu]

Rei felt all of it.

He felt the mouths close on her. He felt them eat. Her noise. Her thought. Her feeling. Everything that was loud in her. He felt them find out how little of it there was. Shimizu had never been loud. Shimizu had held everything very still, her whole life, the way you hold a cup of water full to the brim while you walk across a room.

He felt her keep walking.

Through the mouths. Through the eating. To the seam. To the very centre, where it was joined. He felt her put the paper there. Flat. With her palm. The way she had put her palm flat on a hundred walls to read them.

*Shut.*

The swarm came apart.

It went to pieces in the air like a flock of birds when a hawk goes through it. Scattering. Screaming. A thousand small bright mouths flying apart in every direction and going out, one by one, like sparks from a bonfire going up into the dark.

Shimizu was standing where it had been.

She stood for a moment, very straight, with her hand still held out flat in front of her, as if there were still a wall there to read.

Then she sat down. In the weeds. Carefully. The way she had sat at the kitchen table that morning in Gin's chair.

Kuma tore out of Kato's hands and ran to her and lay down across her lap, all of him, his whole red weight, with his bad back leg stuck out to one side and his head on her chest.

Rei got there. He did not know how. He knelt in the weeds beside her.

Her face was grey. Not ash grey. Not Watanabe's grey. Just tired. Very, very tired. As if all of it had been eaten, all the noise, all the stillness, all the holding, and there was nothing left in her but the shape of where it had been.

She looked at Rei.

She looked at his shirt. The clean one, filthy now, with blood on the collar and rust on the sleeves.

"Better," she said. Her voice was almost nothing. "That one's better."

Rei took her hand without meaning to. "Shimizu-san..."

"Don't," she said. "You'll make it worse. You always talk." Her other hand found Kuma's head on her chest. Her fingers moved in the red fur. Once. "Tell Kato. Tell him he's not too loud." A breath. "He never was. I should have said."

Her hand stopped moving.

Kuma did not move either. He lay across her lap with his head on her chest and his eyes open, the way the old dog was lying by the freight cars, and he did not lift his head again until morning.

[break Grief]

The grey one came last.

The great slow shape like a hill of wet ash. It did not come fast. It did not come laughing. It came the way grief comes, slowly, heavily, without any hurry at all, because it has all the time in the world and it knows you cannot outrun it.

It did not come for anyone.

It came for the door.

The great white door painted on the side of the rusted freight car. The door that agreed with every doorstep in Kita. The one still pouring lights, thinner now, but still pouring, home. And holding it, a boy with white paint to his elbows and his whole body shaking, and another boy behind him with a silver ring, and an old man in a dark haori with his hand flat on the frame.

The grey shape lay down on the door.

That was all it did. It did not attack. It did not push. It simply lay down on top of it, the way grief lies down on top of you in the night, its whole slow enormous weight, and pressed.

And the door began to close.

Rei felt it. Through the open frame in his own chest. He felt the agreement between the yard and a thousand doorsteps begin to come apart. One doorstep. Then another. Fires going out in the dark all over Kita. Not because anyone was kicking them over. Because nobody could remember, any more, under that weight, why they had lit them. What was the point. They were gone. They were not coming home. Why light a fire.

Itsuki screamed.

Not with fear. With effort. With his whole body, both white palms flat against the painted door, holding it, the thing in his spine awake now, Rei could feel it, the first of his twenty-four doors standing wide open and something long and grey and faceless leaning out of it to look, to help, *doors, doors, you're holding a door, let me*.

Sei was crying. He had both arms round his brother from behind and his face pressed against Itsuki's back and he was crying out loud, like a child.

And Sōun stood up straight.

[break Again]

He took his hand off the frame.

Rei saw his face. The long still closed face. And for the first time, it was not closed. Every curtain in the house was open. Every window. It was the face of a man looking at his two sons holding a door against the end of the world, and for the first time in his life not knowing how to keep it from his face what he felt.

He stepped in front of the door.

Between it and the grey shape. Between his sons and the weight. He stood with his back against the painted white wood, with Itsuki's hands on either side of him, and he faced the hill of wet ash, and he opened his arms.

A Kuroda opens doors.

He did not open this one. He was this one. He made himself the frame. Rei felt him do it. Felt the old man take every gate he had ever opened in his life, every agreement between every two places he had ever asked to be next to each other, every sea house in Kamakura and every river and every district, and put all of it into his own body. And stand. In front of the door. And say *yes*.

Not to the grief. To the doorsteps. To every fire in Kita. *Yes. Here. This way. I am the way. Come home.*

The grey shape pressed down on him.

The whole of it. All that slow enormous weight. On one old man in a dark haori with his arms open.

And the door held.

It did not close. It did not even narrow. It stayed exactly as wide as it had been, white and wet and enormous on the side of the rusted car, and through it, past the old man, past his open arms, around him and over him and through him, the lights went on pouring. Thicker now. Brighter. Fires coming back on all over Kita, one after another, as if somebody had reminded them.

The grey shape lifted.

Slowly. Heavily. Like a cloud lifting off a hill. It rose off the door and off the old man, and drifted back toward the river, and sank into the grey sea, and did not come out again.

Sōun stood in front of the door for a moment longer.

Then he turned round. He looked at Itsuki. At his face. White. Wet. Shaking. With paint on it and tears on it and the long grey faceless thing behind the first of his twenty-four doors pulling back, slowly, satisfied, into the dark.

The old man's mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner. "Again."

Just that. The one word. The first and last thing he had ever said to his son that sounded like pride. In a sea house in Kamakura, when Itsuki was seven, with sand on his shoes.

*Again.*

Then his knees went.

Itsuki caught him. Sei caught both of them. They went down together in the weeds in front of the painted door, the three of them, and the lights went on pouring over their heads, home, home, home, and the old man lay with his head in his younger son's lap and his hand in his elder son's hand and his eyes open, looking up at the door, as if he were checking it was still there.

It was.

He closed his eyes.

[break What Rei did]

Rei was kneeling in the weeds between Shimizu and the wreck of the freight cars.

He could feel all three of them. Through the empty frame where his door had been. Three places in the yard where something had been, and was not any more. Three shapes in the dark like the dent in Watanabe's chair. Warm, still. Fading.

He did not cry. He could not. Everything was coming in, and there was no room left for anything to go out.

He reached into the wreck.

He did not touch it with his hands. With the other hand. The one that moves things. He reached into the twisted metal and found the place where a broad man in dark clothes lay very still, and he found the jacket, and the inside pocket, over the heart, and he found the thing inside it.

He drew it out. Gently. Through the rust and the iron. Into his palm.

A school photograph. The size of a train ticket. Soft and creased at the corners. A girl of twelve in a navy uniform with her hair in two short plaits and a serious face, looking straight at the camera as if she were trying very hard to remember something.

He turned it over.

On the back, in small square pencil, an address. In the north.

And under it, in the same hand, newer, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly gone through the paper, as if it had been written very recently, by somebody who had been carrying the words for a long time and had only just found out how to put them down.

*I held up ceilings. I'm sorry I never came. Your dad.*

Rei put the photograph inside his shirt, against his chest, next to Gin's key.

Then he stood up.

On the grey beach, the woman in the soft white dress was looking at him. And at the yard. At the three still shapes. At the dog by the wreck, and the red dog in Shimizu's lap, and two brothers holding their father in front of a door.

She looked satisfied.

*There,* she said, in the air, in the weeds, in every ear. *There. You see? Everyone who holds something up gets crushed by it in the end. Everyone. It's the only rule there is.* She turned, at last, to the body standing beside Rei, with two people in it. *Now.*

And she said the name.
