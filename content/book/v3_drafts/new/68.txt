#title Face to Face

They came down the stairs of the Archive into the street at a quarter to seven, and the light had gone strange.

There is an hour in the evening, in Tokyo, in summer, when it does that. The sun is down behind the buildings but the sky has not let go of it yet. Everything goes orange and long. Every shadow is three times the length of the thing that casts it. Every window on every tower burns like a coal. The crows go home. The cicadas change their note. And for half an hour, maybe less, the whole city looks like an old photograph of itself, something remembered rather than seen.

Chen Lian stood on the step of the Archive with her arms wrapped round herself and looked up at it.

"*Ōmagatoki,*" she said. "The old people had a name for it. The hour when you meet demons." She did not smile. "I always thought it was a nice name. I'm going to stop thinking that."

Mori had gone ahead in a taxi with Hoshino and Inoue, east, to the river. Kato had the car. Itsuki was coming from Ueno on foot, running, because the trains were full of families going home from the graves with paper lanterns, and he had rung twice already to say so, out of breath, and once to say he had drawn something and would show them when he got there and would not say what it was.

"The freight yard," said Kato, at the wheel, as they pulled out. "Past Tabata, by the river. That's where Chen says the line goes. That's where everybody's going." He looked at Rei in the mirror. "You're grey round the edges. You need to eat something."

"Later."

"You always say later."

[break The bridge]

They went north through the city in the orange light, slowly, because every road was full. Obon traffic. Families in cars full of watermelons and grandmothers. Taxis. On every balcony they passed, on every doorstep, people were setting out little clay dishes, and in the dishes, small piles of hemp stalks, ready for the sending fires. Children were running about with sparklers not yet lit. An old man in a doorway was arguing with a cucumber horse that would not stand up on its chopstick legs.

At Nishi-Nippori the traffic stopped altogether. They sat on a bridge over the railway lines with the windows down and the engine ticking, and below them the tracks ran away north in the orange light, silver and straight, toward Tabata and the river.

Rei's wrist went cold.

Not a little. All at once. The latch on his wrist went so cold it burned, and Gorō, on the back seat, stood up and opened his wings and made a sound Rei had never heard him make. Low. Not a scream. Like a growl.

At the end of the bridge, where a flight of concrete steps went down to the street below, there was a white chrysanthemum lying on the top step. Fresh. And on the step beside it, a family. A man and a woman and a boy of about six, with a paper lantern on a stick and a bag of shopping, who had stopped there to let the boy light a sparkler, and the man had his lighter out, and the woman was laughing at something.

And coming up the steps toward them, from the street below, slowly, there was a young man with black eyes.

Rei was out of the car before Kato had finished saying his name.

[break The steps]

He ran along the pavement of the bridge between the stopped cars. The young man was on the steps. About twenty. A thin student in a T-shirt with a supermarket logo on it and a white chrysanthemum in his left hand and a box cutter in his right, the kind they use in shops to open cartons, with the blade all the way out. He came up the steps one at a time, without hurrying, with his black eyes on the boy.

The woman saw him and stopped laughing. The man turned round with his lighter still lit in his hand.

Rei was too far. He knew it. Thirty metres of pavement and a woman with a pram in the way. He lifted the latch on his wrist and threw the door in his chest open as he ran, all the way, and reached, as he had reached for the thing in the old woman at the kite market, and for Gin across a hot street. Clumsy and strong and much too open. He felt the thing in the student. Small. Grey. Lit up like a lamp by the white flower in his hand. It did not even notice him.

The student lifted the box cutter.

And somebody stepped out of the shadow at the bottom of the steps behind him, and put a hand on the back of his neck.

Just a hand. Long-fingered, pale, very steady. Laid flat on the back of the student's neck, under the hair. The student stopped as if a switch had been turned off. The box cutter fell out of his fingers and rang on the concrete. His knees went, and the hand on his neck went down with him, easily, and laid him on the steps on his side, the way you lay a sleeping child on a bed.

The man who had done it straightened up.

He was tall. He wore a grey jacket with the sleeves pushed up, though it was much too hot for a jacket. His long black hair was tied back loosely at the neck. He stood very straight, with his weight even on both feet, like an officer waiting to be inspected. In the orange light his face was all bones and shadows.

He did not look at Rei.

He looked at the boy on the step, with the unlit sparkler in his hand and his mouth open. The boy had a cut on his forearm. Not deep. A long thin line where the blade had caught him on the way up, beading red.

The tall man crouched down in front of the boy. He took the boy's arm in his long fingers, quite gently, and turned it to the light, and looked at the cut with great attention, as a man looks at a puzzle he has only lately learned to solve. Then he laid his thumb along it, and drew it down, slowly, from the elbow to the wrist.

When he took his thumb away, there was no cut. There was only a thin pink line, and a little blood on the boy's skin, and then not even the line.

"There," said the tall man. His voice was low and flat and very polite. It was Gin's voice. It used the words carefully, one at a time, as if it were paying for each of them. "That is mended."

The boy looked at his arm. Then at the man. "Thank you," he said, in a small voice.

The tall man looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," he said. And then, as if he had remembered something, and was not sure he had remembered it correctly, "You are welcome."

[break Not him]

Rei stopped at the top of the steps.

He was close enough now. Three metres. Closer than the kite market, closer than the tree in Asagaya. Close enough to see the cut on the jaw, healed now to a thin white scar. Close enough to see that the jacket was Gin's jacket, the grey one he wore to the office, with the button missing from the left cuff that Rei had been meaning to sew back on for a year. Close enough to smell him. Barley tea and hot concrete and something else, something like the air before a thunderstorm.

"Gin," he said.

The tall man turned his head and looked at him.

And Rei knew.

He had known on the bridge, and in the car, and in Asagaya, and at the kite market, and he had told himself every time that he did not know, that it was too far, that the light was wrong. Now it was three metres and the light was very clear.

It was Gin's face. Gin's eyes. Gin's straight back.

Nobody was looking out of them.

Not nobody. Somebody. Somebody calm and courteous and very old and not at all interested in him, looking at him as you look at a stranger who has come up the steps behind you at a station, to see if he is going to be a problem. The eyes went over his face, once, and over the latch on his wrist, and the crow on the railing behind him, and came back, and there was nothing in them at all that knew who he was. Not even Gin's way of looking. Gin looked at things sideways first, always, and then straight. These eyes looked straight, and stayed.

"You are the younger one," said the tall man. Not a question. Information. "Move. There are more coming."

Then he looked past Rei, up at the bridge, at the stopped cars and the orange sky and the long silver railway lines running away north toward the river. His nostrils moved, very slightly, as if he were smelling something on the wind.

"East," he said. "And north. Many." He turned his head back to Rei, and for one second something moved behind the eyes. Very far down. Like a fish turning over once in deep water.

Then it was gone, and the tall man went down the last three steps into the street, and round the corner, and was not there any more.

[break The step]

Kato found him sitting on the top step beside the white chrysanthemum, with the student asleep on the concrete below him and the family gone, and Gorō on the railing with his feathers flat.

Kato sat down beside him. He did not say anything for a while.

"That wasn't him," said Rei.

"No."

"I mean it was him. His body. His jacket. I could see the button." Rei looked at his own hands. "But it wasn't him. It didn't know me. It looked at me like I was a bin."

"I know," said Kato. "I heard it." He turned the knight over in his pocket. "It's very quiet in there, the thing. It thinks in straight lines. Like a ruler." He hesitated. "And under it. Right at the bottom. Something else, Rei. I heard it for half a second, when it looked at you. Before it went."

Rei turned his head.

"Loud," said Kato. "Like you. In capitals." His voice cracked a little. "Just one word. Your name."

Rei sat on the step.

Below them, on the street, the first of the sending fires were being lit on the doorsteps. Small orange flames in little clay dishes, one after another, all down the street, like a runway. The smoke went up straight in the still air.

Gorō came down off the railing and stood on the step beside the white chrysanthemum, and looked at it, and then picked it up in his beak, very deliberately, and carried it to the edge of the step, and dropped it over into the street.

"Come on," said Kato, and stood up, and held out his hand. "Itsuki's two streets away. He says he drew this an hour ago. He says he drew what happens next, too, and you're not going to like it, and he's not going to tell you on the phone."
