#title Face to Face

The message came on the Capturer at noon on Friday, while Rei was standing in the queue at the konbini with a rice ball in each hand.

The box buzzed in his pocket. He took it out. The TALK button was lit, and on the narrow green screen, in those neat, careful, spaced letters, like the handwriting of someone taught by a strict teacher with a brush, there were four lines.

*saturday. the old man's garden.*

*at the meeting hour. when the light goes orange.*

*bring whoever you wish. i will come anyway.*

*i owe you a name.*

[break The meeting hour]

There is an hour in the evening, in Tokyo, in summer, when the light goes strange.

The sun is down behind the buildings but the sky has not let go of it yet. Everything goes orange and gold and long, every shadow three times the length of the thing that casts it, every window on every tower burning like a coal. The air is thick and warm and still. The crows go home. The cicadas change their note. And for half an hour, maybe less, the whole city looks like an old photograph of itself, something remembered rather than seen.

The old people used to have a name for it. *Ōma-ga-toki.* The hour when you meet demons.

At six forty on Saturday the sixth of August, Rei stood in Tanizaki's garden among the long grass with his back to the step and watched the lane.

It was nearly two weeks since the funeral. Nobody had cut the grass. Nobody would. The fig tree hung over the broken gate. The weeds with their tiny yellow flowers had stood up again where the crowd had trampled them. The stone lantern still had its strip of white paper on it, curling now, and grey, and there was another strip across the sliding doors of the house, and another across the windowsill where the third cup had stood upside down. Someone from upstairs had sealed the house again after Okada unsealed it. Nobody knew who. Nobody's name was on the paperwork.

The step was not sealed. Nobody had thought to seal a step.

They had come, the ones Rei had brought. Not all of them in the garden. Mori had said, very flatly, *I am going to be in that garden, Uedera, and if that thing so much as breathes wrong I am going to burn your brother's body down to the shoes, and then I will sit in a boiler room for a year and hate myself, but I will do it.* So Mori was in the garden, at the far corner, by the fence, with his arms folded and the air round him shimmering. Majima was outside the gate, on the low wall across the lane, with the old dog lying at his feet. Kato was in the car round the corner by the fox shrine, with the windows down, listening. Itsuki was on the roof of the house next door, sitting astride the ridge tiles with a jar of iron filings between his knees and a stick of chalk behind his ear, because he had said that if anything went wrong he wanted to be above it, where he could open something underneath.

Gorō was on the stone lantern. His feathers were flat. He was not making a sound.

At six forty-seven, the cicadas in the fig tree stopped.

[break Who came]

He came up the lane on foot.

Not fast. Not slow. Walking the way Gin walked, a long easy stride with his hands in his pockets. He was wearing a white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow and dark trousers, and over one arm, in spite of the heat, he carried a dark jacket. Gin's jacket. His black hair was long, longer than Rei had ever seen it, down past his shoulders, tied back at the nape with a plain dark band. He was thinner. Much thinner. The bones of his face stood out under the skin like the frame of a house under a sheet.

He stopped at the gate.

He looked at Majima on the wall. He inclined his head, very slightly, the way you nod to a man across a train platform. Majima did not nod back. The old dog stood up and growled, low, and Majima put a hand on its back and the growl stopped.

He looked up at the roof of the house next door. At Itsuki astride the ridge. He held Itsuki's eyes for a moment, and something in the long face changed, very slightly, as if it recognised him and was not sure how to say so.

Then he looked into the garden.

At the long grass. At the fig tree. At the stone lantern with its curling strip and the crow sitting on it. At the fence, and the big man standing by it with the air burning round him like a heat haze over a road.

At the step.

At Rei.

Rei had thought about this moment for a year. On the sofa, at night, with his foot off the end. On the platform at Ōji. In the white. He had thought about what he would say. What he would do. Whether he would run at him. Whether he would cry. Whether he would be able to stand up at all.

He did not do any of it.

He looked at his brother's face across the garden in the orange light, and his brother's face looked back, and it was Gin's face. Exactly Gin's face. The long nose. The heavy eyebrows. The dark attentive eyes. The mouth that was always about to tell somebody to stop it.

And it was not Gin. Not at all. Because Gin had never once in his life stood so still with nothing in his hands. Gin always had something. A pencil. A notebook. A cup. A book open on one knee. Gin noticed things and wrote them down, and his hands were always on their way to the next thing he would need to write. This man stood at the gate with his hands at his sides and his weight perfectly even, like a statue somebody had carved of Gin by looking at photographs, very carefully, and getting everything right except the thing that made it him.

It shifted the jacket on its arm, very slightly. "Good evening."

It was Gin's voice. That was the worst thing. Gin's voice, low and a little rough, the voice that had told Rei to get off the sofa and eat something every morning for nineteen years. Saying *good evening* in a way Gin had never said anything in his life. Formal. Careful. As if each word were a cup being set down on a saucer.

It did not put a hand on the gate. "May I come in?"

[break The step]

Rei could not speak. He nodded.

The man in Gin's body came through the broken gate. He walked up the path through the long grass. The grass bent under his feet, the way grass bends under anybody's feet. He did not look at Mori. Mori did not move. But the air round Mori went from shimmering to something else, something that made the weeds near the fence go brown at the tips and curl.

He stopped at the bottom of the step. Where Kanna-sensei had stopped. He looked at the boards. At the place where an old man had sat cross-legged with a cup held out.

He did not sit on the step.

He knelt. On the path. In the dust. Facing the step, with his back to the gate. He knelt the way you kneel in a temple, both knees down, his hands on his thighs, his back straight. And he bowed. Low. His forehead almost to the ground. In front of the empty step.

He stayed like that for a long time.

Rei stood beside the step and looked down at the back of his brother's head. At the dark band holding the long hair. At the nape of Gin's neck, which he had seen every day of his life, bent over the rice cooker, bent over a book, bent over the kitchen table at three in the morning writing something in pencil on the frame of the window.

When the man in Gin's body straightened up, his face was wet.

"I would have told him first," it said quietly. To the step. Not to Rei. "That was what I intended. To sit at his table, and drink the third cup, and say it to him, and then to you. He gave me tea for ten months and never asked my name. I think he would have liked to hear it." It looked at the step for a moment longer. "I did not come because he told me not to. I have never regretted obeying anyone. I did not know that was a thing that could be regretted."

It turned its head and looked up at Rei.

"I am sorry," it said. "For him. For the old man. That is not a debt. I have tried to make it one, for two weeks, and I cannot. There is nothing owed. There is only sorry." It said the word carefully, as if it were trying it on. "Your brother says that is how it is for people. All the time. He says it is the worst thing about being one."

Rei sat down on the step.

He did not decide to. His legs did it. He sat down on the boards where Tanizaki had sat, with his knees up, and looked at his brother's face kneeling in the path in front of him, a metre away, and his whole body was shaking, and he could not stop it.

"Is he there," he said. His voice did not work properly. "Right now. Is he there."

Gin's eyes did not leave his face. "Yes."

Rei pressed his knees together to stop them shaking. "Can he see me?"

"Yes." The thing in Gin's body was quiet for a moment. "He is very angry with me, at this moment. He wants me to move his hand. He wants to put it on your head. He used to do that, he says. When you were small. When you were frightened. He says you would not remember. He says you were four."

Rei put his face in his hands.

His voice came out muffled through his fingers. "I remember."

"He says, *of course you do*." A pause. "He says, *you remember everything, you idiot, you always did*."

Across the garden, by the fence, Mori made a sound. Rei did not look up. He did not want to see Mori's face.

[break The other side]

"I am going to tell you my side," said the thing in Gin's body. "Then I am going to tell you my name. Then I am going to go. Not for long. The night the veil is thin is in a week, and she will not wait, and neither can I. But tonight I am going to go." It folded its hands on its knees. "Your brother has asked me to tell it to you straight. Without, he says, *all the bowing*. I will try."

Rei took his hands away from his face.

"I am old," it said. "I am a great deal older than this city. Older than the river it stands on, I think, though it is hard to remember. I am what the old books in the Archive call a Shell. The dark side of a seat. The thirteenth. Duty." It said the word as if it were the name of a town it had been born in and had never been able to leave. "I am what Duty becomes when there is no love in it. Debts. Claims. Accounts. Everything owed and everything paid and nothing ever, ever given. I have kept the accounts of this city since before it had a name. I have taken what was owed. I have paid what I owed. I have never once in all that time done anything for any reason except that it was owed."

The orange light lay across the garden. The long shadows of the grass. The fig tree's shadow reaching all the way to the step.

"Seven years ago, the thirteenth chair went dark," it said. "Its holder died. And something came and held it from underneath. Something from the light side. You have seen it. The man at the bottom of the column." It looked at Rei steadily. "When a holder's chair is empty, the Shell of that seat can sit in it. That is the rule. Older than me. I could have. For seven years I could have walked into that round room and sat down in that chair, and the current of all the Duty in this city would have run the wrong way, through me, and I would have been the only Duty there was. Debts. Claims. Accounts. For everyone. Forever."

Rei found he had leaned forward. "Why didn't you?"

The thing in Gin's body was quiet for a long time.

"Because he was holding it," it said at last. "The man at the bottom of the light. Holding it from underneath, alone, with no one to help him. Seven years. And I did not want to take it from him." It frowned, Gin's frown, very slightly, as if puzzled by its own words. "There was no debt. I owed him nothing. But I did not want to. I did not know why. I still do not entirely know why."

It looked down at its hands.

"Last July, she came to me. The Shell of Despair. Kishin. The one in the soft dress. She has been my creditor for longer than I can remember, and I hers, and that is not a good story, and I will not tell it tonight. She came, and she said, *it is time. Obon is coming. The veil is thin. Take your chair, and I will take mine, and we will call the others, and we will sit, all thirteen, and run this city backwards for a thousand years.* And she said, *if you do not take it, I will call in what you owe me, and you will take it anyway, and you will hate it.*"

Rei's hands closed on his knees. "And you took Gin."

"I needed a body. I needed hands. I needed someone who could sit in that round room and lift a teapot and walk past the guards at the Archive and not be stopped. I needed someone who had seen what she was planning, so that I could use what he knew." It closed its eyes. Gin's eyes. "He had sat in her white room for an hour and listened, and walked out of her door. Three weeks later, I was waiting for him on the walkway outside your door. He was the right shape. He was Duty all the way down, like a stone. He was the only one who could have held me." It opened its eyes. "I did not ask him. I did not know how to ask. I took him. And he has told me every day for a year exactly what kind of thing does that."

Rei shook his head slowly. "Why didn't you just sit in the chair? If she was going to make you anyway. Why take him at all?"

The thing in Gin's body looked at him.

"Because I thought," it said slowly, "if I had him, I could learn. How the light side does it. How a man can hold Duty without the accounts. How you can owe and not take. How you can give and not be owed." It paused. "I thought if I could learn that, then when she called in her debt and put me in the chair, I could sit in it the right way round. I thought I could make myself into something she could not use."

Rei watched the hands on the thighs, the stillness of them. "Did it work?"

It was a long time before the answer came.

"I do not know," it said. "I am different. I know that much. I have grieved an old man I owed nothing. I have said *please*. I have said *sorry* and meant it with no debt behind it. I have obeyed a turned cup." It looked at Rei. "But I do not know if it is enough. I do not know if, when she says *sit*, I will be able to say *no*. Nobody has ever said no to her. Not in four hundred years. Not in a thousand."

Rei looked at the boards beside him, at the place where a cup had stood. "Tanizaki did."

The thing in Gin's body went very still.

It bowed its head, very slightly, toward the same place. "Yes. He did. With a cup of tea."

[break Name]

The orange was going out of the sky. The long shadows were beginning to blur at their edges, the way shadows do at the very end of the meeting hour, when the day lets go. On the stone lantern, Gorō had not moved.

The thing in Gin's body put its hands flat on the dusty path, either side of its knees, and bowed again. Not as low. Just enough.

"Where I come from," it said, "a name is a debt. A name given is a claim. Whoever holds your name can call you. Can find you. Can make you answer." It straightened. "She knows my name. She has always known it. I have given it to nobody else. Not in all the time I have been."

It looked at Rei. And for a second, just one second, in the very last of the orange light, Rei saw something behind his brother's eyes that was not the polite old thing at all. Something that moved. Something that pushed, hard, from behind, like a hand pressed flat against the inside of a window.

Gin's left hand, lying on Gin's thigh, closed slowly into a fist.

The thing in Gin's body lifted its chin. "My name is Yushiro."

The cicadas started again. All of them. All at once. As if someone had turned them back on.

Rei said it the way you test a step in the dark. "Yushiro."

The dark eyes closed for a second. "Yes."

Rei said it again, louder, to make sure it was real. "Yushiro."

"Yes." It sounded strange. Almost shy. "Now you have a claim on me. You can call me, and I will come. You can find me, wherever I am. You can make me answer." It paused. "I have never in my existence put that in anyone's hands."

Rei wiped his face with his sleeve. "Why me?"

Yushiro looked at the step. At the place where the old man had sat. At the boards worn soft by forty-one years of somebody sitting there, being in the way.

"Because I owe you an old man," it said. "And because your brother has been telling me for a year that you are the most stubborn person in this city, and that if anybody can make me keep a promise, you can." Gin's mouth moved, very slightly, at one corner. Not a smile. The ghost of one. "He says I will regret it. He says you will call me at three in the morning to ask if I have eaten."

Rei laughed.

He did not mean to. It came out of him all at once, wet and broken, and he put his hand over his mouth, and it kept coming. He sat on Tanizaki's step and laughed with his hand over his mouth and his eyes streaming, and across the path, on his knees in the dust, his brother's body watched him do it with an expression on its face that Rei had never once seen on any face, a sort of terrible, careful wonder, as if it had found something very small and very valuable lying in the road and was afraid to pick it up.

[break What came after]

It stood up. Slowly. The way an old man stands, one hand on its knee.

"The freight yard," it said. "Past Tabata. By the river. Where the goods trains used to stop. Your brother asked the old woman at the bath house about it, last August, on the afternoon before I took him. White lights that nobody carries." It looked toward the north, toward the river, where the sky was already dark. "That is where she will gather them. Everyone who has drunk her tea. Every hollow one in the city. On the thirteenth, at the meeting hour, when the dead come home. She will open the far shore there, where the old lines run into the river, and she will walk them all through it."

Rei leaned forward on the step. "And Yua?"

"The girl is in the white house. Not the grey one in Asagaya. The other. The one with the square hedge. She was moved on the night the old man died." Yushiro paused. "She is still counting. I can feel her. She has not stopped once."

Rei stood up.

He looked at the dark beyond the fig tree. "A week."

"A week." Yushiro looked at Mori, across the garden, by the fence. At the burning air. At the brown curling weeds. It inclined its head to him. Low. A bow between equals. "Holder of Rage."

Mori did not unfold his arms. "I don't like you."

"That is correct of you," said Yushiro. "I would not like me either. I am working on it." It turned back to Rei. "I will come when you call. I will come on the thirteenth whether you call or not. Your brother says..." It stopped. Its face did something complicated. "Your brother says to tell you to stop sleeping on the sofa. He says he means it. He says if he comes home and finds your foot hanging off the end of it one more time he is going to cut it off."

Rei wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "Tell him I'll stop when he comes home and makes me."

Yushiro was quiet for a moment, listening to something nobody else could hear.

A corner of Gin's mouth moved. "He says," it said slowly, "*deal*."

It turned and walked down the path through the long grass, and out through the broken gate, and past Majima on the wall, and the old dog lifted its head and watched it go and did not growl. It walked down the lane in the blue dusk with its hands in its pockets and the jacket over its arm.

At the corner, by the fox shrine, it stopped.

It stood there for a moment, very still, the way Gin had never once stood. And then, just before it turned the corner and was gone, its head turned. By itself. Not toward the garden. Up. Toward the step, which stood a little above the lane, and the boy standing on it in the dark.

The way Gin had looked up every night of Rei's childhood when he came round the corner at the bottom of the hill under the street lamp with his coat over his arm, and found a small boy in pyjamas at the railing three floors up, waiting. Gin always looked up. He always saw him.

Then the head turned away again, and the thing in Gin's body walked on round the corner, and was gone.

Rei stood on Tanizaki's step in the dark with the cicadas screaming and that one look burned into the backs of his eyes, and said the name once more, very quietly, to nobody, to try the weight of it.

*Yushiro.*

On the stone lantern, Gorō opened his wings, and closed them, and finally made a sound. Not a scream. The soft wet clicking a crow makes in its throat when it has found something it was looking for.
