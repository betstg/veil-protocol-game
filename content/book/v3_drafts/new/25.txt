#title The Heads of the Houses

Haruki came to the Kuroda house in the last week of November. Rei did not know he was coming. He was in the training hall at seven in the morning, blindfolded, with Grandmother Mitsu hitting him on the knee with a fan every time he slammed the door in his chest instead of opening it, when he heard the old boards creak in the corridor and smelled coffee.

When he took off the blindfold, Haruki was standing in the doorway in a dark suit and a long coat, the first time Rei had ever seen him in anything but a sweater, holding a paper cup. Beside him stood Mrs. Fujii, bowing.

"Haruki-kun," said Grandmother Mitsu.

"Grandmother Mitsu." Haruki bowed very low. "You look exactly the same as when you set my wrist."

"You were twenty-two and you'd been thrown through a wall by something in a hospital basement. You were very rude about it."

"I was in a lot of pain." Haruki straightened up. He was looking at Rei. Not smiling. Looking at him as he looked at coffee while he poured it, in slow circles, with complete attention. "I came to ask Sōun-sama something about Thursday." He paused. "But now I think I've come for something else."

[break The council]

"Once a year," Haruki said, "in the last week of November, the heads of the seven Tower Families meet. It's very old. It's very boring. Mostly they argue about money and who insulted whose grandfather in 1890." They were walking in the Kuroda garden, by the pond, after training. The carp moved under the black water like slow orange lamps. "When that many old powerful people sit in one room, things on the other side notice. So somebody strong sits at the back, in case something comes through. Somebody with no family of their own in the room. This year they asked me."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because I need someone to carry the tea tray." Haruki stopped by the pond. "And because I think you need to see it."

"See what?"

Haruki looked at the water for a while.

"Who your brother really worked for," he said.

The families did not meet in a family house. They met in an office.

The building was in Ōtemachi, two streets from the Division, a tall dark glass tower with the name of a holding company on a granite block outside, a name Rei had seen on the side of trucks and on the back of bank statements all his life and never thought about. *Shichisei Holdings.* Seven Stars. In the lobby there were security guards and a fountain and a reception desk of white marble, and a young woman behind it who looked at Haruki's face and did not ask for anything, and pressed a button.

The lift went to the forty-first floor. The doors opened onto a different world.

Somebody had built an old Japanese hall inside the top of a glass tower. A floor of a hundred tatami mats. Paper lamps in the corners. Screens of old dark wood with paintings of cranes and pines on them, very faded, that must have come out of some house that no longer existed. Through the gap between two screens, on one side, Rei could see the real windows, floor to ceiling, and through them the whole of Tokyo spread out under the night, millions of lights, all the way to the black edge of the bay. In front of the windows someone had made a small garden of raked white gravel on the floor, with three stones in it.

"They used to meet in a house," said Haruki quietly, as they took off their shoes. "Then the house burned down. That was a long time ago. Now they meet here. It's easier to keep secret. Nobody looks at an office."

Seven people knelt in a wide circle in the middle of the hall. Seven heads of seven families. All old. All in formal black. Rei, in a plain dark suit Haruki had lent him, too long in the sleeves, knelt at the back by the wall with a tray of tea things and his eyes down, and looked at them through his eyelashes.

He knew one of them. Kuroda Sōun knelt on the far side of the circle, very straight, with his face like a stone. He had seen Rei come in. He had not shown it by even a flicker.

Haruki whispered the others to him, one by one, as he poured. A tiny old woman with white hair to her waist and eyes like black glass. *Tsukishiro. My grandfather's cousin. She doesn't know my name.* A broad red-faced man with a booming laugh. *Orimoto. Industrial money.* A thin grey man with a doctor's soft hands. *Higashiyama. Medicine.* A quiet woman who never spoke. *Nishimura.* A man who argued with everyone. *Akiyama. They're having a family war. Don't make eye contact.* And a man of about sixty with silver glasses and a beautiful dark suit, who sat a little apart and smiled a great deal. *Fujiwara-Kage.*

Behind the Fujiwara-Kage head, kneeling a little back from the circle, was a young man of about twenty-four. Very neat. Very still. With a face like a closed file. He had looked at Rei once when Rei came in, and his eyes had stayed on Rei for exactly one second too long.

*His son,* Haruki whispered. *Sena. Division Auditor. Very clever. Very dangerous. Don't spill anything on him.*

[break The report]

For an hour they talked about money, and old insults, and who owned a temple in Saitama. Rei poured tea and his knees went numb.

Then the Tsukishiro grandmother tapped a small fan on the mat in front of her, once. The hall went quiet.

"The Division," she said.

A screen slid open at the side of the hall. A woman came in.

She was in her early fifties. She wore a pale grey suit, perfectly cut. Her black hair was smooth and pinned back and there was not one grey strand in it. She carried a slim leather folder. She knelt in the centre of the circle, in front of the seven heads, and bowed so low that her forehead almost touched the mat. When she sat up, she was smiling.

Rei had never seen her before. He knew her at once.

He knew her by her words, when she began to speak. Warm and smooth and calm. The voice of someone who had said these sentences a thousand times and still sounded like she meant them. He had heard those sentences before, on the first day, in the lobby in Marunouchi, out of the mouth of a young man in a neat tie who had turned away to talk on the phone. *I completely understand how worrying that must be.* The young man had not written them. He had been reading them. This was the woman who wrote them.

"Hagiwara Mizuki," Haruki breathed, beside him. "Director of Public Confidence. She reports to the council every year." His hand, on the teapot, was not quite steady. "Rei. Keep your eyes down."

Rei kept his eyes down. He could not stop listening.

Hagiwara Mizuki gave her report. She did it beautifully. She spoke of incidents, and responses, and public calm. She said that sightings in the city were up a great deal since last year, and that public anxiety was down, and that her office was very proud of that. She spoke of a *personal injury accident* at Ōji station in August, *very regrettable*, *handled*. She spoke of thin spots in Kita. *Seventeen were assessed as monitor only. Nine have since been closed by unregistered persons. We are looking into it.*

Behind Rei, very faintly, Haruki made a small sound in his throat that might have been a laugh.

Then she turned a page in her folder.

"The Uedera matter," she said.

Rei's hands went still on the tray.

"Field officer Uedera Gin, Kita district, has been absent since the twelfth of August," said Hagiwara Mizuki. Her voice did not change at all. "The Division's directors request that the council confirm its position. That the matter remain internal. That no family involve itself. That the officer's younger brother, who is unregistered, continue to be monitored but not approached."

She closed the folder.

"The directors ask for the council's approval," she said, and bowed again.

[break The vote]

Rei understood then. Not slowly. All at once, like a light going on in a dark room.

The Division did not decide. The Division asked.

The posters in the stations. The smiling owl. The blue suits on Ōji platform. Okada with her three notebooks and her flat voice. Kato at his counter in the Arsenal, listening to nobody think about Gin. All of it. All of it came, in the end, to this room. To seven old people kneeling on tatami under paper lamps, in a house older than the city, deciding.

His brother's name was on their agenda between a temple in Saitama and the price of salt.

"The Fujiwara-Kage approve," said the man with the silver glasses, smoothly. "The matter is delicate. The directors are best placed."

"Orimoto approves." The red-faced man waved a hand. "Not our business."

"Higashiyama approves."

"Nishimura approves." The quiet woman's first words of the night.

"Akiyama approves," said the Akiyama head, "and wishes to note that Akiyama also approved last year's thing about the bridges and was ignored."

The Tsukishiro grandmother turned her black glass eyes across the circle.

"Kuroda?"

Kuroda Sōun did not move. He knelt with his hands on his thighs and looked straight ahead, at the cold white garden through the open screens.

"The Kuroda do not vote on the Uedera matter," he said.

A small stir went round the circle.

"Abstention is your right," said Hagiwara Mizuki pleasantly. "May I ask the reason, for the record?"

Sōun turned his head and looked at her. It was the same look he had given Rei on the first day. Cold water into a cup.

"No," he said.

The silence went on for a long moment. Then the Tsukishiro grandmother tapped her fan on the mat.

"Tsukishiro approves," she said. "Six and one abstention. The directors have the council's approval. The matter remains internal." She did not look at the back of the room, where her grandson was kneeling with a teapot, very white. "Next."

Hagiwara Mizuki bowed, and rose, and went out through the screen, smiling.

As she passed the back of the hall, on her way to the lifts, she glanced, very briefly, at the two young men kneeling by the wall with their tea trays. Her eyes went over Haruki. Over Rei. And they paused on Rei, for a fraction of a second, the way your eyes pause on a word you have read somewhere before.

Then she was gone.

[break The garden]

Afterward, Rei stood by the window behind the screens, in front of the little garden of raked gravel and three stones, with the whole lit city under his feet and his hands in fists in the sleeves of the borrowed suit. Haruki stood beside him.

"You knew," said Rei.

"I knew they'd talk about him. I didn't know what they'd say." Haruki's voice was flat. "I'm sorry. I thought you should see it. I thought you should know who's really in charge. Before you made any choices."

"They voted on him. Like he was a temple in Saitama."

"Yes."

"The Tsukishiro voted yes."

"Yes." Haruki looked at the gravel. "She always votes yes. That's how you stay the oldest family in Japan. You never vote no." He was quiet for a while. "Kuroda didn't, though."

"No."

"I've sat at the back of four of these," said Haruki. "Other people have sat at the back of forty. Nobody's ever seen Kuroda Sōun abstain on anything." He turned and looked at Rei. "Whatever he's seen in you in that training hall. Whatever his son has told him, or not told him. It's enough to make him refuse to put his family's name on your brother's file."

Footsteps on the tatami behind them. They turned.

It was the neat young man. Fujiwara-Kage Sena. He was standing three metres away in his formal black, with his hands folded in front of him, looking at Rei with his closed-file face.

"Excuse me," he said politely. "I don't think we were introduced. I'm Fujiwara-Kage Sena. Division Auditor." A small bow. "You pour tea very well. I've never seen you at one of these before."

"He's with me," said Haruki.

"Of course." Sena did not look at Haruki. He was looking at Rei's face, carefully, as if he were comparing it to a photograph. "You have your brother's jaw, Uedera-san." He smiled. It was a perfectly correct smile. "*Monitored, but not approached.* I approve of the council's wisdom." A small bow. "I'm sure we'll meet again."

He walked back across the tatami to the lifts, and did not look back.

Under Rei's shirt, against his breastbone, Gin's key had gone cold. Not the cold it always had. A sharper cold, clear and sudden, like a hand of ice laid flat on his chest.

He did not know if it meant Sena. He did not know if it meant this room, high over the city, where seven old people decided things. He did not know if it meant the whole building.

"Haruki," he said. "Get me out of here."

"Yes," said Haruki, and did.
