#title The Heads of the Houses

Itsuki opened the gate in the training hall on a Tuesday morning at the end of November, in front of everyone.

He did not make a speech about it. He walked to the black lacquered frame at the end of the hall, put his hand on the post, closed his eyes, and the plaster wall through the frame turned into the green iron railing of a footbridge over a grey river, with ducks on the water. Cold river air blew into the hall. One of the cousins dropped a cushion. Grandmother Mitsu said "Ha!" very loudly, like a gunshot.

Kuroda Sōun was standing at the side of the hall with his hands behind his back.

He looked at the bridge through the frame for a long moment. Then he looked at his son.

"Shirahige," he said.

"Yes."

"Your mother took you there."

"Sei told me."

If that name hurt him, Sōun did not show it. He looked at the bridge again. Then he nodded, once, very slightly, the way a man nods to a stranger in the street. And he turned and walked out of the hall.

Itsuki closed the gate. The bridge went away. He stood with his hand on the black post, breathing hard.

"That's it?" he said, to nobody. "Twenty years, and that's it? A nod?"

Grandmother Mitsu came and patted his cheek, hard, almost a slap.

"For your father," she said, "that was a parade."

[break The visitor]

Haruki came to the Kuroda house that same week. Rei did not know he was coming. He was in the training hall at seven in the morning, blindfolded, with Grandmother Mitsu hitting him on the knee with a fan every time he slammed the door in his chest instead of opening it, when he heard the old boards creak in the corridor and smelled coffee.

When he took off the blindfold, Haruki was standing in the doorway in a dark suit and a long coat, the first time Rei had ever seen him in anything but a sweater, holding a paper cup. Beside him stood Mrs. Fujii, bowing.

"Tsukishiro," said Grandmother Mitsu, without warmth.

"Grandmother Mitsu." Haruki bowed very low. "You look exactly the same as when you set my wrist."

"You were nine and you fell off our wall stealing persimmons."

"I was eight. And they were very good persimmons." Haruki straightened up. He was looking at Rei. Not smiling. Looking at him the way he looked at the coffee when he poured it, in slow circles, with complete attention. "I came to deliver a letter to Sōun-sama. From my grandmother." He paused. "But now I think I've come for something else."

[break The council]

"Once a year," Haruki said, "in the last week of November, the heads of the seven Tower Families meet at our house in Bunkyō. It's very old. It's very boring. Mostly they argue about money and who insulted whose grandfather in 1890." They were walking in the Kuroda garden, by the pond, after training. The carp moved under the black water like slow orange lamps. "My grandmother hosts it. I'm supposed to sit at the back and pour tea and learn."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because I need someone to carry the tea tray." Haruki stopped by the pond. "And because I think you need to see it."

"See what?"

Haruki looked at the water for a while.

"Who your brother really worked for," he said.

[need ch25_council | A great old tatami hall in a Bunkyō estate at night lit by paper lamps, seven elderly heads of families kneeling in a wide circle in formal black, an immaculate woman in her fifties in a grey suit kneeling in the centre reporting from a folder, two young men in plain black at the back with tea trays]

The Tsukishiro house was much bigger than the Kuroda house, and much older. It took up a whole hillside in Bunkyō behind a wall of black wood. Inside, there were so many buildings and corridors and gardens that Rei lost count. Everywhere there were paper lamps, and polished floors, and very quiet people in dark clothes walking very quickly. In one corridor they passed a closed door with a thick rope of white paper across it and a small dish of salt on the floor in front of it. Haruki did not look at it. He walked a little faster past it.

"What's in there?" Rei whispered.

"An arrangement," said Haruki. "Don't ask. Nobody asks. I grew up in this house and I've never asked."

The council met in a great hall at the centre of the house, with a high dark ceiling and a floor of a hundred tatami mats. Paper lamps stood in the corners. Through the open screens on one side was a garden of raked white gravel under the moon, very cold and very still.

Seven people knelt in a wide circle in the middle of the hall. Seven heads of seven families. All old. All in formal black. Rei, in a plain black kimono Haruki had lent him, knelt at the back by the wall with a tray of tea things and his eyes down, and looked at them through his eyelashes.

He knew one of them. Kuroda Sōun knelt on the far side of the circle, very straight, with his face like a stone. He had seen Rei come in. He had not shown it by even a flicker.

Haruki whispered the others to him, one by one, as he poured. A tiny old woman with white hair to her waist and eyes like black glass. *My grandmother. Tsukishiro.* A broad red-faced man with a booming laugh. *Orimoto. Industrial money.* A thin grey man with a doctor's soft hands. *Higashiyama. Medicine.* A quiet woman who never spoke. *Nishimura.* A man who argued with everyone. *Akiyama. They're having a family war. Don't make eye contact.* And a man of about sixty with silver glasses and a beautiful dark suit, who sat a little apart and smiled a great deal. *Fujiwara-Kage.*

Behind the Fujiwara-Kage head, kneeling in the place where Haruki was kneeling for the Tsukishiro, was a young man of about twenty-four. Very neat. Very still. With a face like a closed file. He had looked at Rei once when Rei came in, and his eyes had stayed on Rei for exactly one second too long.

*His son,* Haruki whispered. *Sena. Division Auditor. Very clever. Very dangerous. Don't spill anything on him.*

[break The report]

For an hour they talked about money, and old insults, and who owned a temple in Saitama. Rei poured tea and his knees went numb.

Then the Tsukishiro grandmother tapped a small fan on the mat in front of her, once. The hall went quiet.

"The Division," she said.

A screen slid open at the side of the hall. A woman came in.

She was in her early fifties. She wore a pale grey suit, perfectly cut. Her black hair was smooth and pinned back and there was not one grey strand in it. She carried a slim leather folder. She knelt in the centre of the circle, in front of the seven heads, and bowed so low that her forehead almost touched the mat. When she sat up, she was smiling.

Rei had never seen her before. He knew her at once.

He knew her by her words, when she began to speak. Warm and smooth and calm. The voice of someone who had said these sentences a thousand times and still sounded like she meant them. He had heard those sentences before, on the first morning, in the lobby by Ōji station, out of the mouth of a young man in a neat tie who had turned away to talk on the phone. *I completely understand how worrying that must be.* The young man had not written them. He had been reading them. This was the woman who wrote them.

"Hagiwara Mizuki," Haruki breathed, beside him. "Director of Public Confidence. She reports to the council every year." His hand, on the teapot, was not quite steady. "Rei. Keep your eyes down."

Rei kept his eyes down. He could not stop listening.

Hagiwara Mizuki gave her report. She did it beautifully. She spoke of incidents, and responses, and public calm. She said that sightings in the city were up by forty percent since last year, and that public anxiety was down by six percent, and that her office was very proud of that number. She spoke of the Arakawa river, and eight elderly persons, and *natural causes*. She spoke of a *personal injury accident* at Ōji station in August, *very regrettable*, *handled*. She spoke of thin spots in Kita. *Seventeen were assessed as monitor only. Nine have since been closed by unregistered persons. We are looking into it.*

Behind Rei, very faintly, Haruki made a small sound in his throat that might have been a laugh.

Then she turned a page in her folder.

"The Uedera matter," she said.

Rei's hands went still on the tray.

"Field officer Uedera Gin, Kita district, has been absent since the twelfth of August," said Hagiwara Mizuki. Her voice did not change at all. "His file has been transferred to Protocol for assessment. Protocol requests that the council confirm its position. That the matter remain internal. That no family involve itself. That the officer's younger brother, who is unregistered, continue to be monitored but not approached."

She closed the folder.

"Protocol asks for the council's approval," she said, and bowed again.

[break The vote]

Rei understood then. Not slowly. All at once, like a light going on in a dark room.

The Division did not decide. The Division asked.

The posters in the stations. The smiling owl. The blue suits on Ōji platform. Okada with her three notebooks and her flat voice. Kato in the copy room. The floor that was not on the directory. All of it. All of it came, in the end, to this room. To seven old people kneeling on tatami under paper lamps, in a house older than the city, deciding.

His brother's name was on their agenda between a temple in Saitama and the price of salt.

"The Fujiwara-Kage approve," said the man with the silver glasses, smoothly. "The matter is delicate. Protocol is best placed."

"Orimoto approves." The red-faced man waved a hand. "Not our business."

"Higashiyama approves."

"Nishimura approves." The quiet woman's first words of the night.

"Akiyama approves," said the Akiyama head, "and wishes to note that Akiyama also approved last year's thing about the bridges and was ignored."

The Tsukishiro grandmother turned her black glass eyes across the circle.

"Kuroda?"

Kuroda Sōun did not move. He knelt with his hands on his thighs and looked straight ahead, at the cold white garden through the open screens.

"The Kuroda do not vote on the Uedera matter," he said.

A small stir went round the circle.

"Abstention is your right," said Hagiwara Mizuki pleasantly. "May I ask the reason, for the record?"

Sōun turned his head and looked at her. It was the same look he had given Rei on the first day. Cold water into a cup.

"No," he said.

The silence went on for a long moment. Then the Tsukishiro grandmother tapped her fan on the mat.

"Tsukishiro approves," she said. "Six and one abstention. Protocol has the council's approval. The matter remains internal." She did not look at the back of the room, where her grandson was kneeling with a teapot, very white. "Next."

Hagiwara Mizuki bowed, and rose, and went out through the screen, smiling.

As she passed the back of the hall, she glanced, very briefly, at the two young men kneeling by the wall with their tea trays. Her eyes went over Haruki. Over Rei. And they paused on Rei, for a fraction of a second, the way your eyes pause on a word you have read somewhere before.

Then she was gone.

[break The garden]

Afterward, Rei stood in the cold garden by the raked gravel with his breath smoking in the moonlight and his hands in fists in the sleeves of the borrowed kimono. Haruki stood beside him.

"You knew," said Rei.

"I knew they'd talk about him. I didn't know what they'd say." Haruki's voice was flat. "I'm sorry. I thought you should see it. I thought you should know who's really in charge. Before you made any choices."

"They voted on him. Like he was a temple in Saitama."

"Yes."

"Your grandmother voted yes."

"Yes." Haruki looked at the gravel. "She always votes yes. That's how you stay the oldest family in Japan. You never vote no." He was quiet for a while. "Kuroda didn't, though."

"No."

"I've been coming to these since I was sixteen," said Haruki. "I've never seen Kuroda Sōun abstain on anything. Not once." He turned and looked at Rei. "Whatever you and Itsuki did in that training hall this autumn. Whatever he's seen in you. It's enough to make him refuse to put his family's name on your brother's file."

Footsteps on the gravel behind them. They turned.

It was the neat young man. Fujiwara-Kage Sena. He was standing three metres away in his formal black, with his hands folded in front of him, looking at Rei with his closed-file face.

"Excuse me," he said politely. "I don't think we were introduced. I'm Fujiwara-Kage Sena. Division Auditor." A small bow. "You pour tea very well for a Tsukishiro servant. I've never seen you in this house before."

"He's new," said Haruki.

"Of course." Sena did not look at Haruki. He was looking at Rei's left wrist, where the sleeve of the kimono had slipped back a little. At the edge of the new black mark on the thin skin. The latch. The little bridge. "That's a very fine piece of work. Unusual. Tattoos make our instruments so unreliable." He smiled. It was a perfectly correct smile. "I'm sure we'll meet again."

He bowed once more, and walked back along the gravel path into the house, and did not look back.

Rei pulled his sleeve down over his wrist. It was cold. Not from the night. The mark was cold, the clear sharp cold that meant *something is near*.

He did not know if it meant Sena. He did not know if it meant the closed door in the corridor with the rope of white paper and the dish of salt. He did not know if it meant this whole house.

"Haruki," he said. "Get me out of here."

"Yes," said Haruki, and did.
