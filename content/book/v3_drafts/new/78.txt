#title The White House

Yua counted.

Two quick, one slow, two quick. Her own heart, under her own hand, under the white cotton shirt that was not hers. She had been counting it for thirty-six days. Since the eighth of July, when she had walked through the open door of the grey house in Asagaya on her own, with her three phones switched off in her hoodie pocket and her hands in fists, because the woman in the grey coat had left a note in Nakano with Rei's name on it, and nobody wrote Rei's name on anything without Yua finding out why.

Two quick, one slow, two quick.

It was not a real heartbeat. Hers was faster than that. Hers had been fast her whole life, Mina used to say, like a rabbit's. The counting was something else. It was a thing to hold. A rope to keep your hand on in the dark. If you were counting, you were awake. If you were awake, you were you.

The white room had tried very hard to make her stop counting.

[break What the white room did]

It was kind. That was the worst part. Nobody had warned her it would be kind.

She had been ready for horrible. She had been ready for the black eyes and the hollow people and the thing Mina had come back from with nothing behind her face. She had been ready to bite somebody.

Nobody gave her anything to bite.

They gave her a futon. Clean. With a pillow. They gave her rice in the morning and miso soup and a boiled egg, every morning, at the same time, without her having to ask or pay or do anything for it. They gave her a white shirt that smelled of washing powder. They gave her tea.

And Kanna-sensei sat with her.

Not every day. Not too much. Just sometimes. In the evenings. Kneeling on the cushion next to her in the long white room, not touching her, not saying anything, just there. And once, on the tenth day, when Yua had been sitting with her arms round her knees staring at the wall and counting, Kanna-sensei had said, very quietly, without looking at her, *you've been looking after everyone on that street since you were twelve. Haven't you. Who looks after you?*

Yua had nearly stopped counting then.

She had felt it. Like a rope going slack in her hand. Two quick, one slow, two... and then nothing. Just the white room and the warm quiet and somebody kneeling next to her who knew. Who had looked at her properly. Who had seen her.

*Nobody's ever looked at her properly in her whole life,* Kanna-sensei had said to Rei, in July, in front of everyone, while Yua pretended not to know him. *Not even you.*

It had been true. That was why it had worked. That was why Yua had nearly let go.

She had not let go.

Because at the end of that day, after Kanna-sensei had gone, Yua had looked down at her own hands and seen that she was holding something. She had not noticed picking it up. A packet of tissues. The cheap kind from a konbini, with a cartoon on the plastic. She must have had it in her pocket when she walked in. She did not remember putting it there.

And she thought, very clearly, *that one's free too.*

And she started counting again.

[break The thirteenth]

They moved her on the night the old man died. Rei's old man. The one with the tea. She did not know then that he had died. She only knew that Kanna-sensei came back very late, and very still, and did not kneel by anyone, and that in the morning they put Yua in a car with blacked-out windows and drove her west, a long way, to a white house with a square hedge, and put her in a white room there.

The white house was quieter than the grey one. Fewer people. More silence. The windows had blinds that never opened.

On the thirteenth of August, in the afternoon, the house emptied.

Yua heard them go. Feet on the stairs. The front door opening and closing, again and again. Cars. A long line of people walking down the street outside in silence, every one of them carrying something white. She lay on her futon with her hand on her heart and counted, and listened, and by five o'clock the house was nearly silent.

Nearly.

Two of them had stayed. She could hear them. A man and a woman, downstairs, in the hall by the front door. Not talking. Sitting. Waiting. The man had been a postman once, she thought. The woman had been somebody's mum. They had both drunk the tea a long time ago. Their eyes were black all the time now. They did not need to be kind any more.

They were there to make sure she stayed.

Two quick, one slow, two quick.

[break The knock]

At half past five, somebody knocked on the front door.

Not rang. Knocked. Three times. Politely. The way somebody knocks when they have been taught that knocking is the correct thing to do and have practised it.

Yua sat up on her futon.

Downstairs, the front door opened. She heard it. And then she heard nothing at all for a very long time.

Then footsteps on the stairs.

Slow. Even. Unhurried. Somebody coming up the stairs of the white house the way somebody walks up the stairs of their own house at night when they are tired. One step. Then another. They stopped outside her door.

Yua stood up. She stood in the middle of the white room in the white shirt with her hands in fists and her heart going like a rabbit's, and she did not count. She did not need to. She was very, very awake.

The door slid open.

There was a man in the doorway.

Tall. Thin. Very thin. With long black hair loose past his shoulders and a white shirt with the sleeves rolled up and a dark jacket over his arm, even though it was boiling. He stood in the doorway very still, the stillest person she had ever seen, stiller than a statue, with his hands at his sides.

Yua knew his face.

She had seen it on the wall. In Asagaya. In the long white room, on the left, at eye level, by the door. A photograph of a man on a concrete walkway in summer with a bag of shopping in each hand, squinting at the camera, about to say *stop it*. She had looked at it every day for two weeks while she pretended not to know the boy who had taken it.

Her fists did not open. "You're the one on the wall."

The man looked at her.

"Yes," he said. His voice was low and a little rough and extremely polite. "In a manner of speaking."

Yua took one step closer, and stopped. "You're Rei's brother."

"Partly." He did not move from the doorway. "I am also something else. Something that made Rei a promise. I promised that you would come out first. Before anything." He paused. "It is the first promise I have ever made that was not owed. I would like to keep it. May I?"

Yua stared at him.

She thought about the man and the woman downstairs by the front door. The postman. The mum. With their black eyes. Waiting.

She jerked her chin at the floor, at downstairs. "What did you do to them?"

"Nothing." He seemed almost surprised. "I asked them to sit down. They are sitting down. They will be very tired when they wake up. They will not remember why." He looked at her. "I have stopped taking things from people. It is a new habit. I find it difficult."

Yua laughed. She did not mean to. It came out of her like a hiccup.

The man in the doorway looked at her laughing with an expression she had never seen on a grown-up's face. Puzzled. Interested. As if she had done something clever that he wanted to understand.

"Your brother," said Yua. She wiped her nose on the back of her hand. "Rei's brother. The real one. Is he in there?"

He inclined his head. "Yes."

Yua's voice went very small. "Can he hear me?"

The still face did not change. "Yes. He is listening very hard."

Yua took a breath.

"Tell him," she said. "Tell him his brother's an idiot. Tell him his brother went to the grey house on his own after I told him not to, and paid me five thousand yen for a train time, and owes me about forty hamburgers, and never once looked at me like I was a kid who needed saving." Her voice cracked. She let it. "Tell him his brother looked at me properly. The first time. In November. With a crêpe. Tell him the white lady was wrong."

The man in the doorway was very quiet.

Then something happened to his face. Something moved behind it, the way a curtain moves when there is somebody behind it. The very still mouth twitched. Once. At one corner.

"He says," said the man slowly, "that he knows. He says he has heard Rei talk about you every night for a month, out loud, to an empty kitchen, with your phone number in his hand. He says, *she's the toughest person on that street, and she's sixteen, and she's in there because of him, and if she isn't out first I will tear you apart from the inside*." A pause. "He says it is very rude of me to make him wait this long to say it."

Yua wiped her face with both hands.

She pulled her sleeves down over her hands. "Okay. Okay. Fine. Get me out of here."

[break The hedge]

He did not take her hand. He walked down the stairs ahead of her, slowly, the way you walk ahead of somebody in the dark so they can follow your shape.

At the bottom of the stairs, in the hall by the front door, the postman and the mum were sitting on the floor with their backs against the wall and their eyes closed, breathing slowly, the way people breathe when they have been very tired for a very long time and have finally been allowed to stop. The black was gone from round their eyes. They looked like two strangers asleep on a train.

The man in the white shirt stepped round them carefully.

He opened the front door, and stood aside, and let Yua go out first.

She walked out into the evening.

It was hot. It smelled of somebody's dinner and hot tarmac and the square hedge. The sky over the low roofs of the west was going orange. Long shadows. The meeting hour. In thirty-six days she had not seen the sky once.

She stood in the little front garden with her face turned up and breathed.

Behind her, the man came out and closed the door of the white house very quietly, the way you close a door on people who are sleeping.

"There is a policewoman," he said, "at a cordon past Tabata. Her name is Ono. She will look after you. I will take you there. Then I have to go a little further." He shifted the jacket on his arm. "There is a very old debt I have to stand in front of. And a boy who said he would come home."

Yua looked at him.

At his stillness. At his jacket. At the face from the wall, which was not quite being worn right, the way a coat is not quite worn right by someone who has borrowed it.

She dug in the pocket of her white shirt that was not hers.

She found the packet of tissues. The cheap ones, with the cartoon.

She held them out.

The man looked at them.

"Your nose," said Yua. "It's bleeding. Has been since you came up the stairs." She pushed them into his hand. "Gross. That one's free."

He looked down at the tissues in Gin's long hand for a long time, as if nobody had ever given him anything before and he did not know which way up it went.

He closed his fingers round the packet. "Thank you," he said, very carefully. "I will owe you."

"No," said Yua. She shoved her hands in her pockets and started walking toward the station. "You won't. That's what *free* means. Keep up."

Behind her, after a moment, she heard him follow.
