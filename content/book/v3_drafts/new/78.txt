#title The White House

Yua counted.

Two quick, one slow, two quick. Her own heart, under her own hand, under the white cotton shirt that was not hers. She had been counting it for sixteen days. Since the first of August, when she had walked through the open door of the grey house in Asagaya on her own, with her three phones switched off in her hoodie pocket and her hands in fists, because the woman in the grey coat had left a note in Nakano with Rei's name on it, and nobody wrote Rei's name on anything without Yua finding out why.

Two quick, one slow, two quick.

It was not a real heartbeat. Hers was faster than that. Hers had been fast her whole life, Mina used to say, like a rabbit's. The counting was something else. It was a thing to hold. A rope to keep your hand on in the dark. If you were counting, you were awake. If you were awake, you were you.

The white room had tried very hard to make her stop counting.

[break What the white room did]

It was kind. That was the worst part. Nobody had warned her it would be kind.

She had been ready for horrible. She had been ready for the black eyes and the hollow people and the thing Mina had come back from with nothing behind her face. She had been ready to bite somebody.

Nobody gave her anything to bite.

They gave her a futon. Clean. With a pillow. They gave her rice in the morning and miso soup and a boiled egg, every morning, at the same time, without her having to ask or pay or do anything for it. They gave her a white shirt that smelled of washing powder. They gave her tea.

And Kanna-sensei sat with her.

Not every day. Not too much. Just sometimes. In the evenings. Kneeling on the cushion next to her in the long white room, not touching her, not saying anything, just there. And once, on the tenth day, when Yua had been sitting with her arms round her knees staring at the wall and counting, Kanna-sensei had said, very quietly, without looking at her, *you've been looking after everyone on that street since you were twelve. Haven't you. Who looks after you?*

Yua had nearly stopped counting then.

She had felt it. Like a rope going slack in her hand. Two quick, one slow, two... and then nothing. Just the white room and the warm quiet and somebody kneeling next to her who knew. Who had looked at her properly. Who had seen her.

*Nobody's ever looked at her properly in her whole life,* Kanna-sensei had said to Rei, on that Sunday, in front of everyone, while Yua pretended not to know him. *Not even you.*

It had been true. That was why it had worked. That was why Yua had nearly let go.

She had not let go.

Because at the end of that day, after Kanna-sensei had gone, Yua had looked down at her own hands and seen that she was holding something. She had not noticed picking it up. A packet of tissues. The cheap kind from a konbini, with a cartoon on the plastic. She must have had it in her pocket when she walked in. She did not remember putting it there.

And she thought, very clearly, *that one's free too.*

And she started counting again.

[break The sixteenth]

They moved her on the night the old man died. Rei's old man. The one with the tea. She did not know then that he had died. She only knew that Kanna-sensei came back very late, and very still, and did not kneel by anyone, and that in the morning they put Yua in a car with blacked-out windows and drove her round and round for an hour so she would not know where she was, and stopped four streets away, at a white house with a square hedge, and put her in a white room there.

The white house was quieter than the grey one. Fewer people. More silence. The windows had blinds that never opened.

On the sixteenth of August, at dusk, the house emptied.

Yua heard them go. Feet on the stairs. The front door opening and closing, again and again. Cars. A long line of people walking down the street outside in silence, every one of them carrying something white. She lay on her futon with her hand on her heart and counted, and listened, and by nine o'clock the house was nearly silent.

Nearly.

Two of them had stayed. She could hear them. A man and a woman, downstairs, in the hall by the front door. Not talking. Sitting. Waiting. The man had been a postman once, she thought. The woman had been somebody's mum. They had both drunk the tea a long time ago. Their eyes were black all the time now. They did not need to be kind any more.

They were there to make sure she stayed.

Two quick, one slow, two quick.

[break The knock]

At ten to two, somebody knocked on the front door.

Not rang. Knocked. Hard. Three times. And then a girl's voice, rough and impatient, said something very rude through the letterbox, and then the door came off its hinges.

Yua sat up on her futon.

Downstairs, there was a lot of noise all at once. A crash. The postman's voice, not his own, saying something soft and patient. A girl swearing. A thud. A second thud, heavier. And then a boy's voice, very low, very quiet, saying a name she did not know, *Ishida-san*, as if he were talking to somebody who had just woken up.

And then silence.

Then footsteps on the stairs.

Not slow. Fast. Uneven. Somebody coming up two at a time and catching their foot on the top step and saying *ow* under their breath.

Yua stood up. She stood in the middle of the white room in the white shirt with her hands in fists and her heart going like a rabbit's, and she did not count. She did not need to. She was very, very awake.

The door slid open.

Rei was in the doorway.

He looked terrible. His shirt had been white once. There was blood under his nose and dried at the corners of his mouth and in a line down his neck from one ear. There was something wrong with his eyes. Not black. The opposite. Too open. As if there were nothing in front of them at all, no door, nothing, and the whole world was going straight in. He was holding on to the frame of the door with one hand.

Behind him on the landing, a girl Yua had seen once at the Gathering, with her hood down and her nose bleeding too, was leaning against the wall with her eyes shut.

"Yua," said Rei.

Yua's fists did not open.

"You came on your own," she said. "Again. After I told you not to."

"I'm not on my own. Hana's here. Kato's in my head." He swallowed. "He says you've been counting. He says he could hear you from the road. Two quick, one slow, two quick."

Yua's face did something she did not let it do.

"Kanna-sensei said you'd never look at me properly," she said. Her voice came out wrong. "In front of everyone. She said nobody ever had. Not even you."

Rei let go of the door frame.

He looked at her. Properly. Not at the white shirt or the room or the futon. At her. The way he had looked at her in November across a crêpe stand in Harajuku, when she had sold him a train time for five thousand yen and he had paid and not argued and asked if she had eaten.

"She was wrong," he said. "You're the toughest person on that street. You're sixteen. You came in here on your own because she wrote my name on a note. You kept yourself awake for two weeks by counting." He held out his hand. "And you owe me about forty hamburgers. Come on. You're coming out first. Before anything."

Yua stared at his hand.

Then she crossed the white room in three steps and did not take his hand. She hit him in the chest with both fists, not hard, and then grabbed the front of his ruined shirt and held on and put her forehead against his collarbone and did not cry, because she did not cry, she had decided that when she was eleven.

Rei put his hand on the back of her head.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I've got you. Come on."

[break The hedge]

He went down the stairs ahead of her, slowly, so she could follow his shape in the dark.

At the bottom of the stairs, in the hall by the broken front door, the postman and the mum were sitting on the floor with their backs against the wall and their eyes closed, breathing slowly, like people who have been very tired for a very long time and have finally been allowed to stop. The black was gone from round their eyes. They looked like two strangers asleep on a train.

"What did you do to them?" said Yua.

"Gave them something back," said Rei. "I don't know what it was. I'm getting better at it."

Hana was in the front garden already, sitting on the edge of a stone planter by the square hedge with her head between her knees again.

Yua walked out into the night.

It was hot. It smelled of somebody's dinner and hot tarmac and the square hedge. The sky over the low roofs was orange from the city. In sixteen days she had not seen the sky once.

She stood in the little front garden with her face turned up and breathed.

Then she looked at Rei. At the blood. At his eyes.

She dug in the pocket of the white shirt that was not hers and found the packet of tissues. The cheap ones, with the cartoon.

"Your nose," she said. "And your ear. Both ears. Gross." She pushed them into his hand. "That one's free."

He looked down at the tissues in his hand.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't." Yua shoved her hands in her pockets. "There's a policewoman at a cordon past Tabata, isn't there. With a big grin. You're going to dump me on her."

"Her name's Ono. She'll like you."

"Everybody likes me." She looked at Hana on the planter. "Is she going to throw up?"

"Probably," said Hana, without lifting her head. "Last jump. The yard. Three of us. Hold on to something you like."

Yua took hold of the back of Rei's shirt with one fist, and Hana's sleeve with the other, and held on.
