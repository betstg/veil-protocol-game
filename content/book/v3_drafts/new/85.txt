#title Morning

The sun came up over Kita at eighteen minutes to five, as it does in August, too early and already warm.

It came up over the freight yard and found nothing there that it would not have found on any other morning. Weeds. Rails gone brown with rust. A line of old freight cars, one of them crushed flat at the end of the line as if something very heavy had sat on it. A chain-link fence with a hole in it. The river beyond, wide and brown and ordinary, with a heron standing in the shallows on one leg looking at it as if it were late with the rent.

There was no grey beach. There was no low white table. There was a patch of trampled weeds by the water, and a small river stone on a cord lying on the stones, broken in two along a white band. Fukai Rinka had been taken away in the night in the back of one of Okada's cars, wrapped in a blanket, still crying, and nobody yet knew what to do with her.

Rei woke up sitting on the stones with his back against something warm.

He did not know where he was for a moment. He knew what he was. That had not stopped. Somewhere very far down, under the river and the city and the morning, there was a ring of thirteen lights, and one of them was his, and he could feel the weight of the wall resting on all of them together, like a sleeping cat on the end of the bed without opening your eyes. It was heavy. It was not on his back. It was just there, like his own heartbeat.

And under it, standing at the door of his own house with his hands in his sleeves, a man in an indigo coat, awake, watching the street.

He breathed out.

The warm thing he was leaning against breathed out too.

Rei turned his head.

Gin was sitting on the stones beside him with his long legs stretched out and his back against the side of the crushed freight car and his head tipped back against the metal, asleep. His mouth was open. There was dried blood under his nose and a year of stubble on his jaw that nobody had bothered about. His hair was still too long and still tied back. His hands lay in his lap, palms up, open.

Rei watched him sleep for a long time.

He watched Gin's eyes move under the lids. Quick. Restless. All over the place. Noticing things in his sleep. As they had always moved, every night of Rei's life, on the other side of a thin wall, when Gin fell asleep at the kitchen table over a report and Rei came in at two in the morning and found him there.

Not still. Not still at all.

Rei put his hand on his brother's knee.

Gin woke up as he always woke up. All at once, with a jerk and a swear, as if somebody had dropped him.

He looked at Rei. He looked at the river. He looked at his own hands, open in his lap. He closed them, slowly, and opened them again, and closed them, as if to check they still did what he told them.

Rei squeezed the knee. "Morning."

Gin's voice came out cracked and dry. "What day is it?"

Rei kept his hand where it was. "Wednesday. The seventeenth."

Gin squinted at the heron. "Of August."

Rei nodded. "Yeah."

Gin's eyes came back to him, very slowly. "Same year?"

Rei bit his lip. "Different year."

Gin shut his eyes. He sat there with his head against the freight car and his eyes shut for a long time, and his jaw set, and then unset. Then something moved behind his face. Rei saw it. Like a curtain moving when there is somebody behind it.

"He says it is the seventeenth of August and a Wednesday," said Gin, in his own voice, with his eyes still shut. "He says I ought to have known that, because there was a calendar on the wall of the konbini by the bridge. He's very pleased with himself." He opened his eyes. "He's still here, Rei. Beside me. We said together. In and out. We haven't finished coming out yet."

"I know," said Rei. "I can feel him."

"Right," said Gin. "Okay. Right." He rubbed his face. "How's the rent?"

Rei laughed so hard he had to put his forehead on his knees.

He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand when he could. "Paid. Okada's the guarantor."

There was a short silence.

Gin sat up off the freight car. "*Okada?*"

Rei grinned into his knees. "Yeah."

Gin stared at him. "*Records* Okada? Grey suit? Three notebooks and a rubber band?"

"She's been watching me for five years. You told her to." Rei lifted his head. "She caught me on the platform at Ōji. She held out her hand. She signed for the flat when the agency wanted a guarantor and I didn't have one. She's standing over there by the fence right now pretending she isn't crying."

Gin turned his head.

Okada was standing by the hole in the chain-link fence in her grey suit with the shiny elbows, with her three notebooks under her arm and a paper cup of convenience-store coffee in each hand, very upright, looking at the river.

She was not pretending very well.

Gin looked at her for a long moment. Then he lifted one hand off his knee, not very high, like a hand lifted to somebody across a station platform. Okada saw it. She nodded, once, sharply, as if he had handed in a report on time for the first time in his life. And kept looking at the river.

Gin let his hand fall back to his knee. "Five years," he said quietly.

Rei leaned his shoulder against his brother's. "You told her to."

"I did." Gin rubbed his face with both hands. "God. I'm going to have to buy her so much coffee."

[break The yard]

The yard woke up round them.

Rei watched it happen. All along the weeds and the rails, the people who had walked down to the river in the night were waking up. The hollow ones. Except they were not hollow any more. They sat up in the weeds and looked at their own hands and their own shoes and the sky, like people waking on a train that has gone past their stop. A man in a work shirt. A student with a rucksack. The woman with the handkerchief, folding it and unfolding it in her lap and crying quietly with her eyes open.

Tanaka went from one to the next with his bag, crouching, touching wrists, saying things quietly. Kurosawa walked behind him with his hands back in his pockets, saying nothing at all. Inoue Misaki was sitting on top of a freight car with her knees drawn up and the ginger cat in her lap, eating a rice ball Usami had given her, watching the heron. Usami was sitting beside her in his cardigan, and beside Usami, where nobody else could see him, a thin shy young man with his hands folded.

Kumagai's ambulances came up the Tabata road at five with their lights on and no sirens, because Kumagai had rung ahead and told them sirens would only upset people. Ono came with them. She stood at the gate in her uniform with a clipboard and could not write anything on it, and after a while Kumagai came and took it off her and wrote *gas leak* at the top in very neat handwriting and gave it back.

Then Kumagai came down through the weeds to the river with two cans of hot coffee from the machine by the gate, and stopped in front of Rei, and held one out.

"You said you'd come and tell me the rest," said Kumagai. "I said I'd buy the coffee."

Rei took it. "It's a long rest."

"I've got thirty-one years of drawers." Kumagai looked at Gin. At the face. At the eyes, moving. He held out the other can. "Uedera-san."

Gin took it. His hand shook. "Kumagai-san."

"Welcome back." Kumagai straightened up, with a hand in the small of his back. "Don't do that again. The paperwork was terrible."

[break Kato]

Kato was sitting in the weeds where Shimizu had sat down.

They had taken her away an hour ago, very carefully, in the first ambulance, and Moriya had gone with her on his crutch, and Kato had let go of her hand when they asked him to and then sat back down in exactly the same place. He had not moved since. Rei did not think he had slept. His knight was in his fist, a chess piece worn smooth from holding.

Rei sat down beside him.

Kato did not look at him. "You don't have to say anything."

"I do, though." Rei looked at the trampled weeds where she had been. "She said it to you herself. I heard. But Gin's got one for you too. From the cemetery. He didn't get to finish."

Kato's hand tightened on the knight.

"He said tell Kato I can hear him too," said Rei. "Sometimes. Through the walls. Through all of it."

Kato put his face in his hands and cried the way Rei had never seen anyone cry, all at once and without any sound, with his whole back shaking.

Rei sat with him until the ambulances had gone.

Then Gin came and sat down on Kato's other side. He did not say anything either. He put his arm round Kato's shoulders, as Tendo used to in the corridor on the way to the lifts. Kato did not shrug it off.

Over by the wreck of the freight cars, Mika was sitting in the weeds with Majima's dog lying against her legs. The dog had not eaten. She had not got up. But she had let Mika put a hand on her side, and Mika had not taken it away. Hara Setsu came down through the weeds with a paper bag from the yakitori stall by the station, which had opened at five for the ambulance men, and crouched down with a great deal of grumbling about her knees, and took a stick of chicken skin out of the bag, and pulled the skin off the stick with her fingers, and held it out.

Kinako looked at it for a long time.

Then she ate it.

When the sun was high enough to be hot, the dog got up and shook herself and looked once at the crushed freight car and then walked to the gate behind Mika, close at her heels, as if they had arranged it.

[break The river]

Before they left, Rei went down to the concrete block at the edge of the water.

Haruto was sitting on it with his knees up and his chin on them, dripping, with the small brown dog lying beside him with its ear inside out. He looked up when Rei came.

"Nobody went in," he said. "Not one. Not the living. Not the dead. I sent them all up the rails."

"I know. I felt it."

"You owe me a game." Haruto stood up on the block. "Tag. And the painter. You promised."

Rei turned round and shouted up the bank. Itsuki came down through the weeds with white paint dried to his elbows and his eyes swollen, and looked at the boy on the block, and at Rei, and understood at once, because he always did.

"Tag?" he said. "Now? I've had no sleep. My dad's in hospital. I've got paint in places paint shouldn't be."

"You promised," said Haruto.

"*He* promised," said Itsuki, pointing at Rei. Then he looked at the boy's face, and sighed, and took off his trainers. "Fine. Fine. I'm it."

They played tag on the riverbank at six in the morning, among the weeds and the old rails, a ten-year-old dead boy and a twenty-year-old painter with no sleep and a twenty-year-old with no door, while the ambulance men stopped and stared and Gin sat on a rail and laughed until he had to hold his ribs, and the small brown dog ran in circles barking without a sound. Haruto was very fast. Nobody caught him. He caught both of them, twice.

At the end, out of breath, he stood on his block and looked at the river.

"I could go now," he said. "Through the door the painter made. Somebody would be there."

"Yes," said Rei.

Haruto looked down at the dog.

"Not yet," he said. "Somebody's got to watch the river." He sat back down on his block with his chin on his knees. "Come and play again. In winter. Nobody comes in winter."

"In winter," said Rei. "I promise."

[break The box]

The old dark pager was lying in the weeds by the water where Gin had dropped it.

It was empty. The orange light was out. The seals round its edges had gone grey and soft, like paper left in the rain.

Rei picked it up and turned it over in his hands.

*He went back,* said the voice in Gin, very quietly, through Gin's mouth, when Rei showed it to him. *At dawn. To the table. Before you woke. He stood in the shallows until the last of the small ones had gone home, and then he walked into the fog, singing.* A pause. *He told me to tell you. He said, I saw brave people. You were right. Tell the old man in the grey suit his officer kept his word.*

Rei held the empty box for a long time.

Then he put it in his pocket, to give back to the Director.

[break The key]
[break The key]

They went home that afternoon.

Itsuki drove. He had borrowed his cousin Takuya's car, which smelled of dog and incense, and he drove it as he did everything, badly and with total confidence, with a cigarette he had not lit hanging in the corner of his mouth.

Rei leaned forward between the seats. "Gin says stop smoking."

Itsuki looked at Gin in the mirror. Gin looked back at him.

Itsuki's eyebrows went up. "Did he."

"He said it in the cemetery," said Rei. "It was on a list. He didn't get to the end of the list."

Itsuki took the cigarette out of his mouth and looked at it for a long time at a red light, and then put it back.

He drummed one finger on the wheel. "Tomorrow."

Rei snorted against the glass. "That's what you said in January."

"And look how well January went." The light changed. Itsuki drove on. His eyes went to the mirror again, to Gin, to the face, to the eyes, moving, and he looked away quickly and blinked a lot, and did not say anything else until they got to the building.

The walkway on the third floor was the same as it had always been. Potted plants. Somebody's futon over the railing. Outside every door, a little clay dish of grey ash from the sending fires, and in front of the Yamadas' door their cucumber horse and aubergine cow, wilting now in the heat.

Then they were outside 204.

Rei felt Gin stop beside him. Felt it as he had felt the wall come down. All at once and all over. This was the walkway. Here. A year ago. A man with his keys in his hand at the end of a long day, and then nothing.

Gin looked at the door.

Rei put his hand in his pocket and took out the key.

It was warm from his body. It had been in his fist or his pocket or under his pillow every night since Gin closed his hand round it at a quarter to four in the morning and said *when.* He had not put it on the hook. He had not given it to anyone.

He held it out.

Gin looked at it. Then he looked at Rei. His eyes were moving very fast.

He took it. He put it in the lock. It turned as it had always turned, a little stiff halfway, and the door opened onto the dark narrow hall and the smell of the flat, tatami and old tea and Rei, and the light from the little yellow kitchen at the end.

Gin stood in the doorway.

Then he went in. And he took his shoes off, and put them side by side, as he always had, and walked down the hall to the kitchen, and stood by the table for a moment with one hand on the back of his own chair.

And then he turned round and hung the key on the hook by the door.

It swung once and was still.

Rei stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long time.

[break The kitchen table]

They sorted it out at the kitchen table. After. As they had said.

It took three nights.

Gin sat in his own chair, with the wobbly leg, and Rei sat in his, and Itsuki sat on the step stool from the bathroom because there were only two chairs and he refused to sit on the floor, and Gorō sat on the back of the empty chair at the end. And the voice in Gin talked, in Gin's mouth, slowly and politely, setting each word down like a cup, and Gin talked over it whenever he disagreed, which was often, and Rei and Itsuki listened, and sometimes shouted.

It told them what it was. A mender. From the other side. A man, once, very long ago, called Tachibana, with a first name it was still trying to remember. It told them why it had come through, and why it had chosen Gin, and how it had pushed him down, and for how long, and what it had done with his hands at a shrine in Ōji in February and on a kerb in Higashi-Jūjō in November. It did not leave anything out. When it got to the kerb, Gin's voice stopped working, and the voice waited, and then went on.

Itsuki got up and went out onto the walkway and smoked a cigarette and came back in and sat down again.

It told them about the table on the far side. About the empty seat there that was its own, and the other empty seat, at the head, that nobody had sat in since before the river.

"I am going back," it said, on the third night. "To sit at my place. Someone has to mend things on that side. They break all the time. Nobody there knows how to put them back." It looked at Gin's hands on the table. "And he needs his body. All of it. To himself. He has been very patient about the elbows."

"I have not been patient about the elbows," said Gin.

"No," said the voice. "You have not."

Rei looked at his brother's face. At the two people behind it.

"Will you come back?" he said.

The voice was quiet for a long time.

"On Tuesdays," it said at last. "Perhaps. If the man under the light permits it. I am told they are very busy on the other side. I should like to hear about this side, sometimes. The rice. The trains. Whether the painter has stopped smoking."

"He hasn't," said Gin.

"Then I shall have something to ask about."

It went on the third night, at a quarter to four in the morning, at the kitchen table, with Gin's hands flat on the wood. Rei felt it go. Gently, one finger at a time, like a hand letting go of another hand so that it would know each finger as it went. And then there was only one person in Gin's face.

Gin sat very still for a long time.

"It's quiet," he said. His voice cracked. "I forgot it could be this quiet."

Then he got up, and put the kettle on, and took down three cups, and poured all three. He put the third at the end of the table, by the empty chair, in front of the crow.

"For Tuesdays," he said.

[break Monday]

On Monday morning Gin went to Ueno.

Rei went with him, because the old man at the counter knew Rei and did not know Gin. The ticket was in the back of Gin's black field book, where Rei had kept it since September, folded in four, soft at the edges from being taken out and looked at.

The pawn shop behind the station had its shutters up. The old man behind the counter looked at the ticket, and then at Rei, whom he knew, and then at Gin, whom he did not.

He tapped the ticket with one finger. "This your brother?"

Rei put his hands flat on the glass counter. "Yeah."

The old man looked at Gin a while longer. Then he went into the back, and came out with a small box, and set it on the counter, and opened it.

A plain silver watch with a black strap. Not expensive. Not anything. The kind of watch a young man buys with his first proper pay because he has decided he is going to be the sort of person who is on time.

Gin did not touch it.

"He paid the interest every month," said the old man. "On the first. Three thousand. Never missed one." He pushed the box an inch across the counter. "Never asked for it back, either. Just paid, and went."

Gin put the watch on.

He did it slowly. His fingers were clumsy on the buckle. He got it on the third try, and turned his wrist over, and looked at the face.

It had stopped. Of course it had stopped. It had been in a box for a year.

It said thirteen minutes to four.

Gin looked at it for a long time. Then he wound it, carefully, and set it right, and held it to his ear to hear it go.

"Right," he said, to nobody in particular. His voice was not quite steady. "Okay. Right."

Rei counted the loan out onto the counter in notes, as he had counted the interest every month, and the old man counted it again and nodded. On the way out Gin took out his phone, which Rei had charged for him in the night, and looked at the message that had been waiting on it since six o'clock that morning, from a number with no name.

*My office. Tomorrow. Bring nothing. Your appointment. A.*

Rei read it over his shoulder. "Adachi."

"Adachi." Gin put the phone away. "He's going to give me my job back."

Rei fell into step beside him. "Are you going to take it?"

Gin walked a few steps along the side street with his hands in his pockets, in the heat, listening to the watch on his wrist.

He shrugged, and the watch slid down his thin wrist. "Probably. Somebody has to."
