#title Lanterns

The sun came up over Kita at eighteen minutes to five, the way it does in August, too early and already warm.

It came up over the freight yard and found nothing there that it would not have found on any other morning. Weeds. Rails gone brown with rust. A line of old freight cars, one of them crushed flat at the end of the line as if something very heavy had sat on it. A chain-link fence with a hole in it. The river beyond, wide and brown and ordinary, with a heron standing in the shallows on one leg looking at it as if it owed him money.

There was no grey beach. There was no low white table. There was a patch of trampled weeds by the water where a woman's soft white dress lay folded on the stones, and beside it a small river stone on a cord, broken in two along a white band.

Rei woke up sitting on the stones with his back against something warm.

He did not know where he was for a moment. He knew what he was. That had not stopped. Somewhere very far down, under the river and the city and the morning, there was a ring of thirteen lights, and one of them was his, and he could feel the weight of the wall resting on all of them together, the way you can feel a sleeping cat on the end of the bed without opening your eyes. It was heavy. It was not on his back. It was just there, the way his own heartbeat was there.

And on the other side of it, further down, at a low table in the fog, something very straight with good manners was sitting quite still with its hands folded, keeping the account.

He breathed out.

The warm thing he was leaning against breathed out too.

Rei turned his head.

Gin was sitting on the stones beside him with his long legs stretched out and his back against the side of the crushed freight car and his head tipped back against the metal, asleep. His mouth was open. There was dried blood under his nose and a year of stubble on his jaw that nobody had bothered about. His hair was still too long and still tied back. His hands lay in his lap, palms up, open.

Rei watched him sleep for a long time.

He watched the way Gin's eyes moved under the lids. Quick. Restless. All over the place. Noticing things in his sleep. The way they had always moved, every night of Rei's life, on the other side of a thin wall, when Gin fell asleep at the kitchen table over a report and Rei came in at two in the morning and found him there.

Not still. Not still at all.

Rei put his hand on his brother's knee.

Gin woke up the way he always woke up. All at once, with a jerk and a swear, as if somebody had dropped him.

He looked at Rei. He looked at the river. He looked at his own hands, open in his lap. He closed them, slowly, and opened them again, and closed them, as if to check they still did what he told them.

Rei squeezed the knee. "Morning."

Gin's voice came out cracked and dry. "What day is it?"

Rei kept his hand where it was. "Sunday. The fourteenth."

Gin squinted at the heron. "Of August."

Rei nodded. "Yeah."

Gin's eyes came back to him, very slowly. "Same year?"

Rei bit his lip. "Different year."

Gin shut his eyes. He sat there with his head against the freight car and his eyes shut for a long time, and his jaw set, and then unset.

"Right," he said. "Okay. Right." He opened his eyes. "How's the rent?"

Rei laughed so hard he had to put his forehead on his knees.

He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand when he could. "Paid. Okada's the guarantor."

There was a short silence.

Gin sat up off the freight car. "*Okada?*"

Rei grinned into his knees. "Yeah."

Gin stared at him. "*Records* Okada? Grey suit? Three notebooks and a rubber band?"

"She's been watching me for five years. You told her to." Rei lifted his head. "She caught me on the platform at Ōji. She held out her hand. She signed for the flat when the agency wanted a guarantor and I didn't have one. She's standing over there by the fence right now pretending she isn't crying."

Gin turned his head.

Okada was standing by the hole in the chain-link fence in her grey suit with the shiny elbows, with her three notebooks under her arm and a paper cup of convenience-store coffee in each hand, very upright, looking at the river.

She was not pretending very well.

Gin looked at her for a long moment. Then he lifted one hand off his knee, not very high, the way you lift a hand to somebody across a station platform. Okada saw it. She nodded, once, sharply, as if he had handed in a report on time for the first time in his life. And kept looking at the river.

Gin let his hand fall back to his knee. "Five years," he said quietly.

Rei leaned his shoulder against his brother's. "You told her to."

"I did." Gin rubbed his face with both hands. "God. I'm going to have to buy her so much coffee."

[break The yard]

The yard woke up round them.

Rei watched it happen. All along the weeds and the rails, the people who had walked down to the river in the night were waking up. The hollow ones. Except they were not hollow any more. They sat up in the weeds and looked at their own hands and their own shoes and the sky, the way you wake up on a train that has gone past your stop. A man in a work shirt. A student with a rucksack. The woman with the handkerchief, folding it and unfolding it in her lap and crying quietly with her eyes open.

Tanaka Ryu went from one to the next with his bag, crouching, touching wrists, saying things quietly. Kurosawa walked behind him with his hands back in his pockets, saying nothing at all. Inoue Misaki was sitting on top of a freight car with her knees drawn up, eating a rice ball someone had given her, watching the heron.

Kumagai's ambulances came up the Tabata road at five with their lights on and no sirens, because Kumagai had rung ahead and told them sirens would only upset people. Ono came with them. He stood at the gate in his uniform with a clipboard and could not write anything on it, and after a while Kumagai came and took it off him and wrote *gas leak* at the top in very neat handwriting and gave it back.

Then Kumagai came down through the weeds to the river with two cans of hot coffee from the machine by the gate, and stopped in front of Rei, and held one out.

"You said you'd come and tell me the rest," said Kumagai. "I said I'd buy the coffee."

Rei took it. "It's a long rest."

"I've got thirty-one years of drawers." Kumagai looked at Gin. At the face. At the eyes, moving. He held out the other can. "Uedera-san."

Gin took it. His hand shook. "Kumagai-san."

"Welcome back." Kumagai straightened up, with a hand in the small of his back. "Don't do that again. The paperwork was terrible."

[break Kato]

Kato was sitting by the gate on the ground with Kuma lying across his legs.

He had not moved since midnight. Rei did not think he had slept. His king was still in his fist, a chess piece worn smooth from holding. The red dog had his chin on Kato's knee and his eyes open and he was looking at the place by the gate where Shimizu had sat down, as if she might get up again.

Rei sat down beside them.

Kato did not look at him. "You don't have to say anything."

"I do, though." Rei put his hand on Kuma's back. The fur was warm from the sun. "She left you a message. And so did he."

Kato's hand tightened on the king.

"She said," said Rei, and had to stop, and start again. "She said tell Kato he's not too loud. He never was."

Kato made a sound like something tearing.

"And Gin." Rei swallowed. "A week ago. He said to tell you he can hear you too. Sometimes. Through the walls. Through all of it."

Kato put his face down on the dog's neck and cried the way Rei had never seen anyone cry, all at once and without any sound, with his whole back shaking, and Kuma turned his big head and licked his ear, once, very seriously, and lay still.

Rei sat with them until the ambulances had gone.

Over by the wreck of the freight cars, Mika was sitting in the weeds with Majima's dog lying against her legs. The dog had not eaten. She had not got up. But she had let Mika put a hand on her side, and Mika had not taken it away, and in the end, when the sun was high enough to be hot, the dog got up and shook herself and looked once at the crushed freight car and then walked to the gate behind Mika, close at her heels, as if they had arranged it.

[break The key]

They went home that afternoon.

Itsuki drove. He had borrowed his cousin Takuya's car, which smelled of dog and incense, and he drove it the way he did everything, badly and with total confidence, with a cigarette he had not lit hanging in the corner of his mouth.

Rei leaned forward between the seats. "Gin says stop smoking."

Itsuki looked at Gin in the mirror. Gin looked back at him.

Itsuki's eyebrows went up. "Did he."

"He said it a week ago." Rei leaned his head on the window. "It was on a list."

Itsuki took the cigarette out of his mouth and looked at it for a long time at a red light, and then put it back.

He drummed one finger on the wheel. "Tomorrow."

Rei snorted against the glass. "That's what you said in January."

"And look how well January went." The light changed. Itsuki drove on. His eyes went to the mirror again, to Gin, to the face, to the eyes, moving, and he looked away quickly and blinked a lot, and did not say anything else until they got to the building.

The walkway on the fourth floor was black with soot. All along it, outside every door, forty little piles of burnt hemp stalks lay in forty little heaps of ash, and in front of the Yamadas' door their cucumber horse and aubergine cow stood on their matchstick legs, slightly scorched, and the horse was perfectly straight.

Haruto was sitting on the top step with his phone.

He stood up when they came round the corner. He looked at Rei. He looked at the tall thin man beside Rei with the long hair and the dried blood on his lip. He went red, and then white, and then red.

Gin stopped.

He looked at the boy, and at the soot still on his fingers. "You're the one who lit the fires."

Haruto opened his mouth and nothing came out.

"Thank you." Gin held out his hand. "Somebody I met last night asked me to say so. He was very specific."

Haruto looked at the hand like it might bite him. Then he took it.

Then they were outside 204.

Rei felt Gin stop beside him. Felt it the way he had felt the wall come down. All at once and all over. This was the walkway. Here. A year ago. A man with his keys in his hand at the end of a long day, and then nothing.

Gin looked at the door.

Rei put his hand in his pocket and took out the key.

It was warm from his body. It had been in his fist or his pocket or under his pillow every night since Gin closed his hand round it at a quarter to four in the morning and said *when.* He had not put it on the hook. He had not given it to anyone.

He held it out.

Gin looked at it. Then he looked at Rei. His eyes were moving very fast.

He took it. He put it in the lock. It turned the way it had always turned, a little stiff halfway, and the door opened onto the dark narrow hall and the smell of the flat, tatami and old tea and Rei, and the light from the little yellow kitchen at the end.

Gin stood in the doorway.

Then he went in. And he took his shoes off, and put them side by side, the way he always had, and walked down the hall to the kitchen, and stood by the table for a moment with one hand on the back of his own chair.

And then he turned round and hung the key on the hook by the door.

It swung once and was still.

Rei stood in the doorway and looked at it for a long time.

[break Monday]

On Monday morning Gin went to Ueno.

Rei went with him, because the old man at the counter knew Rei and did not know Gin. The ticket was in the back of Gin's black field book, where it had been since September, folded in four, soft at the edges from being taken out and looked at.

The pawn shop behind the station had its shutters up. The old man behind the counter looked at the ticket, and then at Rei, whom he knew, and then at Gin, whom he did not.

He tapped the ticket with one finger. "This your brother?"

Rei put his hands flat on the glass counter. "Yeah."

The old man looked at Gin a while longer. Then he went into the back, and came out with a small box, and set it on the counter, and opened it.

A plain silver watch with a black strap. Not expensive. Not anything. The kind of watch a young man buys with his first proper pay because he has decided he is going to be the sort of person who is on time.

Gin did not touch it.

"He paid the interest every month," said the old man. "On the first. Three thousand. Never missed one." He pushed the box an inch across the counter. "Never asked for it back, either. Just paid, and went."

Gin put the watch on.

He did it slowly. His fingers were clumsy on the buckle. He got it on the third try, and turned his wrist over, and looked at the face.

It had stopped. Of course it had stopped. It had been in a box for a year.

It said thirteen minutes to four.

Gin looked at it for a long time. Then he wound it, carefully, and set it right, and held it to his ear to hear it go.

"Right," he said, to nobody in particular. His voice was not quite steady. "Okay. Right."

Rei counted the loan out onto the counter in notes, the way he had counted the interest every month, and the old man counted it again and nodded. On the way out Gin took out his phone, which Rei had charged for him in the night, and looked at the message that had been waiting on it since six o'clock that morning, from a number with no name.

*My office. Wednesday. Bring nothing. A.*

Rei read it over his shoulder. "Adachi."

"Adachi." Gin put the phone away. "He's going to give me my job back."

Rei fell into step beside him. "Are you going to take it?"

Gin walked a few steps along the side street with his hands in his pockets, in the heat, listening to the watch on his wrist.

He shrugged, and the watch slid down his thin wrist. "Probably. Somebody has to."

[break Okuribi]

On the sixteenth, the last night of Obon, the city sent its dead home.

They lit the send-off fires on the walkway at dusk. Haruto went down the line with the matches again, door to door, all forty, and this time Gin went behind him with a bucket of water, because Gin did not trust anybody with matches, and Haruto said so, loudly, and Gin said he was right not to, and Haruto went bright red and was happy for the rest of the evening.

The old man in the grey summer kimono did not come back. Nobody did, that night. The doors were shut again. The wall was held. The dead had come home and seen what they needed to see and they were going back now, quietly, the right way, following the little fires down to the river like people following the lights of a station.

And then everybody went to the river.

Not the yard. Further down, where the bank was concrete and there were steps, and on the last night of Obon people came with paper lanterns and candles and put them on the water to float away.

There were a lot of them that year. More than anybody could remember. The neighbourhood association had run out of lanterns by six o'clock and somebody's grandmother had started folding new ones out of shopping bags on a card table.

Yua was on the top step in a clean T-shirt, eating a hamburger out of a paper bag with great seriousness. Gin had bought it. She held up one finger at him as he went past.

She chewed and swallowed and pointed the finger at him. "One."

Gin held up one finger back without breaking stride. "Thirty-nine to go."

Rei wrote the names.

He sat on the concrete steps with a felt pen and a stack of white paper lanterns and wrote them one by one in his best handwriting, which was not very good.

*Tanizaki.*

*Watanabe Jun.*

*Shimizu.* Kato sat beside him while he wrote that one, with Kuma, and took the lantern off him afterwards, and carried it down to the water himself.

*Majima Kenji.* Mika put that one on the water, with the dog sitting on the step above her watching it go.

*Kuroda Sōun.* Itsuki did that one. He did it with both hands, very carefully, like a man setting down a cup, and Sei crouched beside him, and neither of them said anything. Then Itsuki took his cigarettes out of his pocket, and looked at them, and put the whole packet in the lantern beside the candle, and pushed it out.

Grandmother Mitsu, three steps up, said *finally* in a voice that carried right across the river.

Then Rei wrote one more, and sat looking at it.

Gin leaned over his shoulder. "Who's that one for?"

Rei turned it round so he could see. There was no name on it. Just a little drawing, in felt pen, not very good. A cup, with steam coming off it.

"Somebody who waited a really long time at a gate," said Rei. "With the tea."

Gin looked at it for a moment. Then he took the pen out of Rei's hand, and under the cup, in his own small hard handwriting, he wrote *Sorry he was late.*

They put it on the water together.

It floated out into the current and turned once, slowly, and went away downriver among all the others, a little square of orange light in a long line of orange lights going away into the dark toward the sea.

On the steps behind them, Choi Hana sat down next to Rei with her hood up and her sleeves pulled down over her hands. She watched the lanterns for a long time. Then she pushed her left sleeve up, and looked at the scar on her forearm, and took a breath.

"I was going to tell you on the roof," she said. "Before Kato rang. About this." She turned her arm over. "It happened when I was..."

On the far bank, somebody let off the first firework of the night.

It went up white and burst gold right over the river, enormous, with a bang that bounced off every building on both banks, and everybody on the steps went *oh*, all at once, the way people do. And by the time the sparks had come down and gone out in the water, Hana had pulled her sleeve down again and was looking at the sky with her mouth a little open, like everybody else.

"Another time," she said, and smiled, very slightly. "It's a long story."

[break Three cups]

They went home late.

Gin made tea. He made it the way he always had, too strong, in the brown pot with the chipped lid. He put three cups on the kitchen table without thinking about it. Then he looked at the third one and did not take it away.

He poured all three.

They sat at the kitchen table, the two of them, in the little yellow kitchen with the frosted window, and drank their tea, and did not say very much. The third cup sat at the end of the table by the empty chair and steamed for a while and then stopped.

Gin pushed his empty cup away, eventually. "Go to bed."

Rei looked at the sofa.

"*Bed,*" said Gin. "Your actual bed. In your actual room. With your feet inside the actual end of it."

Rei went.

He lay down in his own bed for the first time in a year. It was too soft. He had forgotten how soft. He lay on his back and looked at the ceiling and listened, the way he had listened every night for a year to an empty flat, and this time there was something to hear. A chair scraping. A cup set down. The kitchen light under the door. And then the small sound he had listened for at night his whole life, on the other side of a thin wall, until a year ago, when it stopped.

A pen. On paper. Scratching away.

He was asleep before he could count to ten.

[break The archive]

In the kitchen, at the table, under the yellow light, Uedera Gin opened his black field book to a page near the back.

There was a chapter heading on it, in his own handwriting. *Fourteen.* And under it, nothing. He had written the heading a year ago, on the twelfth of August, at this table, before he went out. He had meant to finish it when he got home.

He sat and looked at it for a long time.

Then he took the cap off his pen.

He did not write it the way he had written the others. Those had been reports. Facts and dates and grounding objects and margin notes about his brother's eating. This one would not fit in a report. This one had a beach in it, and a table, and a man in an indigo coat, and a demon with good manners who said thank you for the tea. This one had his brother in it, taking his door off with his own hands on a black beach, because nobody had ever taught him anything else to do with a door except open it.

So he wrote it the way you write about somebody you love when you are trying very hard to be fair to them. From the outside. In the third person. As if it had happened to somebody else, a little brother called Rei, who lost his big brother on the first night of Obon and went looking for him all year through a city full of the dead.

He wrote all night.

At about four in the morning he got up and went to the end of the table and picked up the third cup, which had sat there full since midnight and gone stone cold, and drank it in one go, the way you drink something you were brought a long time ago by somebody who was waiting for you.

It was terrible tea. It always was. He drank it anyway.

Then he sat back down, and looked at the last page, and at the window going grey with the morning, and at the key on its hook by the door, and at the door of the little room down the hall where his brother was asleep with his feet inside the end of the bed.

And he wrote the last line.

And that is how I got back to my brother.
