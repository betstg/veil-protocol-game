#title Hands That Make Tea

*Gin.*

The first thing Gin knew was the smell of tea.

Not any tea. Hōjicha, the roasted kind, brown and smoky, the one he drank on bad nights because it had almost no caffeine and tasted like a bonfire somebody had put out politely. He knew that smell as well as he knew his own handwriting. It was the smell of the kitchen table at three in the morning, the book open under the lamp, Rei asleep on the sofa in the next room with one foot hanging off the end like it was trying to leave without him.

For one warm second he thought he was home.

Then he saw his hands.

They were pouring water from a kettle into a small clay teapot. They were doing it well. Slowly, in a thin steady stream, the way Tanizaki had taught him when he was nine and too impatient to be taught anything. Not too fast, or the leaves scorch. Not too slow, or the water sulks and goes cold. His hands knew. They had done this ten thousand times.

He had not told them to.

He tried to stop. He thought *stop*, as clearly as he had ever thought anything. Nothing happened. The water went on pouring. His right hand set the kettle down on a small gas ring. His left hand fitted the lid onto the pot with a tiny clay click. Neither of them asked him.

It was like sitting in the back seat of his own car with the doors locked, watching a stranger drive it very carefully, at exactly the speed limit, to somewhere he had not chosen.

He did not know the room. A small kitchen. A steel sink with a drip that landed every four seconds, which he counted because counting was a thing he could still do. One gas ring. A window of frosted glass. The walls were papered in an old yellow that had probably been cheerful in nineteen seventy and had since given up. A calendar hung on a nail, a free one from a dry-cleaning shop, and it still showed July, as if whoever lived here had stopped turning pages. Through the frosted glass it was night. He could hear rain.

His body lifted the teapot and poured into one cup.

One. Not two.

That was the thing that frightened him most, in the end. Not the locked doors. Not the hands. It was that the hands poured one cup. Every night since he was nine years old he had set out two cups, as Tanizaki did, because the old man had told him that was what a person did if he wanted to stay a person. Two cups when Rei was a baby who could not drink tea. Two cups when Rei was asleep. Two cups when Rei was away on a school trip and the flat was so quiet it rang. Two cups for nineteen years, and his hands had done it without asking him every single time.

Now they did not. Something else was in charge of his habits, and it had gone through them like a man going through a stranger's wardrobe, and decided which ones to keep.

His body lifted the cup and drank. He tasted it. Good tea, made properly. He felt the heat go down his throat and spread in his stomach. He could feel everything. He just could not do anything about any of it.

*You're awake,* said a voice.

It did not come from the room. It came from inside him, from somewhere behind his eyes, close and quiet, as if someone had leaned in to speak in a library. It was a man's voice, low and calm and very courteous, with an old-fashioned way of putting its words in order, like a very old doctor in a very old hospital who still wrote his notes with a fountain pen. It was the most reasonable voice Gin had ever heard. It sounded like something that had never had to shout in its life, because things had always simply been done.

*You should not be awake,* it said. It did not sound worried. It sounded like a man noticing a draught from a window. *Go back down.*

*Who are you,* Gin thought. The thought came out ragged and small, a shout through bathwater. *What are you. Get out of me.*

*You know what I am,* said the voice. *You have spent six years with things like me. You know enough to be afraid. That will do.*

*Where's Rei.*

*I do not know,* it said. *He is not my concern. I have work to do, and he is not part of it.*

*If you go near him...*

*I have no interest in him.* The voice was perfectly calm. *I need this body. I do not need you in it, and I do not need anybody else. Rest.*

His body lifted the cup and drank again.

And Gin looked, with the eyes that were not his any more, at the shelf over the sink. There were three cups on it. Odd ones. A white one with a crack. A green one. A small blue one with a fish painted on the side, the kind a child would choose.

Two cups. Every night for nineteen years. One for him, and one for the boy who was asleep, or away, or too small to drink it.

His left hand moved.

He felt it move. He moved it. Not much. The fingers lifted off the table and turned, a few centimetres, toward the shelf. Toward the blue cup with the fish. The nail of his little finger scraped the wood with a sound so small that no one in the world but him would have heard it.

The voice went very quiet.

Then, with no hurry at all, the hand was put back flat on the table, as you might straighten a fork that someone else had laid crooked.

*Do not do that again,* said the voice. There was no anger in it. That was the worst part. *You are in the way.*

*What do you want,* Gin thought. *What do you want with me.*

*Nothing,* said the voice. *I want the body. You are only what comes with it.*

The dark came up over him slowly, like warm water over a man who has fallen asleep in the bath. He fought it. He fought it with everything he had, which was not much, which was the smell of the tea and the sound of the rain and a small blue cup with a fish on it that he had not been able to reach.

The last thing he saw was his own face in the black glass of the window over the sink. Long black hair loose past the jaw. Tired eyes. Except that the eyes were not tired. The pupils had swallowed nearly all the brown, wide and black and drinking in the whole small yellow room at once, the drip, the rain, the steam off the cup, as if every single thing in it were interesting and might be wanted later. And under the eyes, a small, gentle, satisfied smile that was not his, that he had never in twenty-eight years made with that mouth, turned up only at the corners and touching nothing else.

Then nothing.

His hands washed the cup, and dried it, and put it away on the shelf. One cup.
