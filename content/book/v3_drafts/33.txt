#title The Third Cup
#part Book Three. The Thirteen

On the first Saturday of March, Tanizaki poured the third cup.

Rei almost did not notice. He had been coming every Saturday for six months. He knelt at the low table with his hands on his knees, and Tanizaki read the newspaper, and the wild garden did whatever it did. That morning it was raining, a soft cold spring rain, and the fig tree was dripping, and Gorō sat hunched on the stone lantern with his feathers puffed out, looking furious about the weather. Rei drank his first cup. He drank his second. He looked at the rain. He thought about nothing in particular. About Aoi in hospital with her leg in a frame, sending him photographs of terrible hospital food. About the bell.

Tanizaki put down his newspaper, lifted the teapot, and poured a third cup.

He pushed it across the table.

Rei looked at it. Then he looked at Tanizaki. The old man's face was calm and brown and lined, exactly as always. But his eyes were different. They were looking at Rei properly, all the way, the way they had never looked at him before.

"Drink," said Tanizaki.

Rei drank. His hands were not quite steady.

"Now," said Tanizaki. "Ask."

[break What Tanizaki said]

There were a hundred questions. He had carried them for six months. He found that most of them did not matter any more.

"What happened to Gin when he was nine?" he said.

Tanizaki looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked out at the rain.

"He started seeing things," he said. "Properly. Not shadows on the stairs. Faces. Every day. And he had no one to tell, and he was frightened, and he was nine." He was quiet for a moment. Somebody in the Grid told him my name. He knocked on that door. He was in a hurry. He wanted me to teach him everything at once, so that he would never be frightened again." He almost smiled. "I gave him two cups of tea and sent him home."

"For two years."

"For two years. And then one Saturday he sat in that place, where you are sitting, for an hour, and did not ask me for anything. And I poured the third cup." Tanizaki looked at the teapot. "He was eleven. He drank it and he asked me why the north of the city felt colder than the south. Nobody had ever asked me that. Nobody had ever noticed." He looked at Rei. "That is when I knew what he was."

"What was he?"

Tanizaki did not answer that. Instead he said, "Do you know what the Thirteen are?"

[break The Thirteen]

Rei did not. He knew the word. He had seen it in the archive. In a chapter he had read twice and not understood. He had seen it on a list in Hara Setsu's kitchen. He had heard the Font, in the white, say *twelve*.

"There are thirteen feelings," said Tanizaki, "that move people. Not all the feelings. The deep ones. The ones underneath. Grief. Rage. Love. Fear. Wonder. Despair. Joy. Shame. Hunger. Courage. Longing. Awe. And Duty." He counted them on his fingers, slowly, like a man counting the people at his table. "Every person alive is moved by one of them more than the others. Most people never know which. They think they want money, or love, or rest. They think they are angry, or sad, or tired. Underneath, there is one thing. One river. And everything they do runs down to it."

He poured himself tea.

"The veil is thin where feeling is strong. You know that already. You learned it in the bath. The thirteen feelings are like thirteen great currents running under the city. Under every city. If nothing held them, they would tear the veil apart. The dead would walk in the streets in their thousands. And so, for longer than anyone remembers, thirteen people have held them." He looked at Rei. "One for each feeling. Thirteen living people, each holding one current open and steady, so that it flows and does not flood. They are called the Thirteen. The people who hold them are called holders. They do not wear robes or sit on thrones. Most of them have ordinary jobs. One of them makes coffee in Kyoto. One of them," he said, "runs a set-meal restaurant in Kita, ten minutes from here."

"Gin knew them?"

"Some of them." Tanizaki drank. "In 2009, one of them died. The holder of Duty. He was old, and it was his time, and when a holder dies, someone else is supposed to take up the current. Someone with the right shape. It usually happens without anyone deciding. The current finds them." He put down his cup. "This time it didn't. There was nobody with the right shape near enough. The seat went dark."

Rei thought of Hara Setsu's green notebook. *2009. Corner cold in June. Did not get warm again.*

"Kita has been cold ever since," he said.

"Kita has been cold ever since." Tanizaki looked at the rain. "Something has been holding that current alone, from underneath, since 2009. Something very large. Something very tired. And the city has been thinning, a little, every year, waiting for someone with the right shape to come and take it up." He looked back at Rei. "Your brother spent the last two years of his life before he disappeared trying to find out who that someone was."

"Did he find out?"

Tanizaki was quiet for a very long time.

"Go and see Hoshino," he said at last. "On Sakae Street. Tell him I sent you. Eat his mackerel. It's very good." He picked up his newspaper. "Then come back next Saturday. There will be three cups now. There will always be three cups."

Rei stood. At the door, he stopped.

"Tanizaki-san. Why now? Why today? I didn't do anything different."

Tanizaki did not lower his newspaper.

"You didn't ask me about your brother," he said. "Not once. Not in your head. Not in your face. For the whole hour." He turned a page. "Six months. It took Gin two years. Go and eat."

[break Sakae Street]

[need ch33_hoshino | A tiny warm set-meal restaurant on a rainy Kita shopping street, eight stools at a wooden counter, steam, a stocky man in a work apron with short black hair and a kind plain face grilling mackerel, a young man in a black hooded jacket at the counter]

Sakae Street was a narrow old shopping street ten minutes south, with a covered pavement and small shops that had been there since the war. A tofu maker. A shop that sold only rice. A tiny bookshop with its books in boxes on the pavement under plastic sheets because of the rain.

The restaurant was halfway along. It had a dark blue cloth curtain over the door, wet with rain, and a handwritten menu on a board outside. *Grilled mackerel set. Ginger pork set. Hamburg set. Miso soup and rice refills free.* Inside it was tiny. Eight stools at a wooden counter. A kitchen behind the counter with a grill and two big pots and a rice cooker the size of a washing machine. Steam on the windows. The smell of grilling fish and soy sauce and rice.

And behind the counter, a man in a white apron, grilling mackerel.

He was in his thirties. Stocky and solid, with strong forearms and short black hair cut for a kitchen. His face was plain and round and kind. Not handsome. Not memorable. The kind of face you remember afterwards as *kind* before you remember anything else about it. He looked up when Rei came in, and smiled. And the smile went all the way up to his eyes and stayed there.

"Sit," he said. "You look cold. Mackerel?"

"Tanizaki-san sent me."

"I know. He called." The man turned the fish. "He said you'd be hungry and you wouldn't say so. Sit."

Rei sat. The man put a bowl of miso soup in front of him without being asked. Then rice. Then a small plate of pickles. Then, after a few minutes, a whole grilled mackerel, golden and spitting, with a heap of grated radish beside it and a wedge of lemon.

"Hoshino," said the man. "Haru. Eat first. Talk after. That's the rule in here."

Rei ate. It was very good. It was the best mackerel he had ever eaten. Halfway through, he realised he was smiling. He did not know why. He had been grey and heavy and cold since the temple, since Aoi's leg, since the bell. Now, sitting at this counter with the rain on the window and the steam and the smell of the fish, something had lifted. Just a little. Like a window opened in a stuffy room.

"There it is," said Hoshino, watching him.

"There what is?"

"That." Hoshino pointed at Rei's face with his cooking chopsticks. "The bit of you that likes mackerel." He grinned. "That's my current. That's what I hold. Joy." He shrugged. "People think joy means happiness. Laughing. Parties. It doesn't. Not really. It's this. A hot meal when you're cold. A good night's sleep. The first sip of beer after work. Finishing something with your own hands. It's small. It's everywhere. It's the thing that makes people get up in the morning when there's no other reason to." He started washing a pan. "I keep it running in this part of the city. That's my job. That, and the mackerel."

"You're one of the Thirteen."

"I am. Seven years now." Hoshino rinsed the pan. "I was a cook before. I'm a cook now. Nothing changed except I don't sleep as much and sometimes I feel half of Kita laughing at once and burn the rice." He turned and looked at Rei. "You want to know about your brother."

"Did you know him?"

"He ate here every Monday for two years." Hoshino nodded at the third stool. "That one. Always the ginger pork. He said mine was better than yours." He grinned at Rei's face. "He was lying. He told me yours was too sweet. He loved it."

Rei put down his chopsticks.

"He asked me a lot of questions," said Hoshino. "About the Thirteen. About the dark seat. About what shape a person had to be to hold Duty." He dried his hands. "I didn't know the answer. None of us did. But I told him something I'd noticed, from in here. Holding Joy. I can feel what people are moved by, a bit, when they're in front of me. Not their thoughts. Just the river underneath." He looked at Rei. "Most people don't know which river they are. They go their whole lives not knowing. They think they're one thing and they're another. I see a lot of people at this counter who think they run on joy, and they're grief all the way down. People who think they're angry and they're fear."

"What was Gin?"

Hoshino looked at the third stool for a long time.

"Duty," he said. "All the way down. Like a stone. I've never felt anything like it. I told him so." He picked up a cloth and wiped the counter, slowly. "He went very quiet. And then he asked me, *Haru, is it in families? Does it run in families?*" He folded the cloth. "And I said, sometimes. Sometimes it does."

The rain went on against the window. The rice cooker clicked.

"And me?" said Rei. His voice came out strange. "What am I?"

Hoshino looked at him. Kindly. Carefully. For a long time.

"I'm not going to tell you that," he said. "That's not mine to tell. You have to find it yourself. Everybody does." He took Rei's empty plate. "But I'll tell you one thing. When you came in that door, you were cold and grey and so heavy I could feel it from the grill. And you ate my mackerel and you smiled." He put the plate in the sink. "So whatever you are, kid, there's a bit of joy in you too. Look after it. You're going to need it."

He did not let Rei pay. At the door, under the wet blue curtain, he said one more thing.

"There's a meeting," he said. "The Thirteen. We don't meet often. Once or twice a year. There's one at the end of March." He hesitated. "Mori wants to see you first. Mori Takao. He's the one who decides who comes. He's hard to find. He'll find you." He looked out at the rain. "Be careful with Mori. He holds Rage. He's the best man I know, and he's been angry for twenty years, and it's tired him out in ways you can't imagine." He smiled again. "Come back Monday. Third stool. I'll do you the ginger pork. It's better than yours."
