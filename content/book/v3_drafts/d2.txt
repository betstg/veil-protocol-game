#title The Brother
#part Book Two. The Families

*The Healer.*

It saw the younger one on a Wednesday morning in December, from the other side of a road in Akabane.

It had not come to see him. It had come because the body walked here. Some mornings the body did that, when the Healer was not paying attention. It walked the way it had walked for six years, down the hill, onto the train, three stops, out at Akabane, along this street, toward that building. Habit. The body was full of habits. The Healer had kept most of them. They were useful, and the body was calmer when it was allowed to keep them.

It did not follow the younger one. Most days it did not know where he was. Once or twice, on nights when the body could not rest, it had sent a few rats to sit on the wall across from his building and look at his window, the way it had learned to do a long time ago, because nobody looks at rats. But the rats could not follow him into the Division, or the bath house, or the old house in Nezu with its high grey wall. Those places did not let small borrowed eyes inside. And most of the time the rats had other things to watch.

So it was standing at a crossing in Akabane at five to nine, holding a can of hot coffee it did not want, when the younger one came out of the station.

He was wearing a suit. A cheap dark grey suit, too long in the arms. A white shirt. A black tie, tied badly, slightly to one side. He walked fast, with his head down, and his left hand kept going to his chest, to something under his shirt. At the door of the Division building he stopped and looked up at it. Nine floors of grey glass. He stood there for a long moment, the way a swimmer stands at the edge of cold water. Then he pushed the door and went in.

The Healer stood at the crossing while the light went green, and red, and green again.

[break What it felt]

Something was happening in the body.

The Healer did not have a name for it. It was a heat, low in the chest. A tightness in the throat. The eyes stinging, slightly, in the cold wind. The hand round the coffee can squeezing harder than it needed to. It was like the feeling the Healer sometimes had when a debt was paid in full after a very long time. A settling. A rightness. But it was not that. There was no debt here. Nothing was owed.

And under the heat, something else. Something sharp. A fear. That the cheap suit was not warm enough. That the building was not safe. That the tie was wrong and somebody would laugh at him.

The Healer turned these feelings over carefully, as it had turned over the coins in the konbini, and understood, slowly, where they came from.

They were not its feelings. They were Gin's.

Even asleep, even pressed down to the very bottom of the body, the man was feeling them. He could not see. He could not hear. He did not know where he was. But some part of him, deep down, had felt his body stand still at a crossing and look at his brother in a suit, and had answered. The way a sleeping mother turns over in bed when her child cries in the next room.

*Pride,* thought the Healer, finding the word in the body's memory. *This is called pride.*

It had never felt pride. It did not know what to do with it.

[break A promise]

Deep down, it felt the man begin to stir.

It would be easy to let him wake. It would be easy to say, *Look. Look, your brother is well. He has a suit. He has gone into the building. He is brave.* The man would wake, and see, and the heat in the chest would grow, and perhaps for a minute the man would not hate it so much.

It would be easier still to make him a promise. The Healer knew exactly what the man wanted. It could feel the shape of it, always, like a stone in a shoe. *Promise me you won't touch him. Promise me he'll be safe.* If the Healer said those words, the man would stop fighting. It knew this for certain. He would go quiet, and sleep, and believe, because the man had read every book there was about things like the Healer, and he knew what every one of them said. *A demon never breaks its word.*

The Healer did not say the words.

It had not made a promise in four hundred years. A promise is a chain. Once made, it is made forever. You keep it to the letter even if it tears you apart, because that is what you are, and a thing that breaks its own nature does not survive the breaking. It had watched other things like itself make promises carelessly, in the old days, to kings and priests and frightened girls, and it had watched those promises eat them from the inside, word by word, for centuries.

And it did not know what was going to happen to the younger one. It did not know what it would need to do. There was a debt still to be paid, across the river, in a white house. Something old and cold that owed it a great deal. When that debt was called in, who knew who would stand in the way.

So it did not promise.

It pushed the man down again, gently, back to the bottom of the body, the way you settle a sleeping child who has half woken. *Hush. Not now. Rest.*

The heat in the chest faded slowly. The sting in the eyes went away.

[break The coffee]

The Healer stood at the crossing until the coffee in its hand was cold.

Then it did something it did not understand. It walked across the road to the vending machine outside the Division building. It put coins in, one at a time, slowly, counting them twice, the way it had learned. It pressed a button. A can of hot corn soup dropped into the tray with a clunk.

It put the can on top of the vending machine. Right on top, in the middle, where anyone coming out of the building would see it.

It did not know why. There was no debt. Nothing was owed. It was not a promise. It was only a can of soup, on a machine, in the cold.

Then it turned and walked back to the station with its hands in its coat pockets, and did not look back.

At six o'clock that evening, when the younger one came out of the building with his tie pulled loose and his face grey with tiredness, he stopped at the vending machine to buy something hot. And he saw the can on top. He picked it up. It was cold now, of course. Long cold.

He stood there holding it for a long time, under the street lamp, looking up and down the empty road.

Then he put it in his coat pocket, very carefully, and took it home.
