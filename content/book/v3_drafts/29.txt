#title Lowercase
#part Book Two. The Families

Itsuki came out of Room Nine on the twenty-sixth of January, at ten in the morning, forty-nine days after he went in.

The tall grey thing was still in him. It would always be in him, the Reintegration woman said. It was too deep. Wrapped too tight. Taking it out would take his spine with it. But it was asleep now. Properly asleep. The seals had settled round it like ice round a stone at the bottom of a pond. And on his back, down the line of his spine from the neck to the waist, there was a new tattoo.

He had drawn it himself. On the grey paper Rei brought him, with the soft charcoal pencils, over seven weeks, while the nurse checked every page for doors. It was a column of small closed doors, one above the other, each with a tiny lock. Twenty-four of them. One for each vertebra. Tomoe had come down to the basement of the Division building with her machine and her needles, with special permission that Okada had spent a week getting, and put them on him over three long nights while he lay on his front on the white bed and swore at her.

"Every door locked," Itsuki said, standing in the corridor in his own clothes for the first time in seven weeks. He was thinner. His face was sharper. There was a new stillness in him, under the jokes, that had not been there before. "It can dream about doors all it likes. They're all locked. And I've got the keys." He held up his right hand. Between his knuckles, on the backs of his fingers, there were four very small black marks. *Switches*. Like Gin's. "If it ever wakes up, I press here, and every door slams."

"Does it hurt?"

"Everything hurts. I've got two healing ribs and a demon in my back and a tattoo from my neck to my bum." Itsuki grinned his crooked grin. It was the same grin. Rei had been afraid it might not be. "I've never felt better in my life."

[break The car park]

Outside, in the Division car park, in the cold January sun, three people were waiting.

Kato was leaning against a grey Division car with his hands in his pockets, pretending to look at his phone. Okada stood by the door with her three notebooks, looking at her watch. And on the wall of the car park, Gorō sat with his feathers fluffed up against the cold, glaring at everybody.

And by the gate, a little apart from the others, a man in a dark formal haori stood very straight with his hands behind his back.

Itsuki stopped walking.

Kuroda Sōun had not come down to the basement once in forty-nine days. He had not called. He had not written. Mrs. Fujii had come four times, with food. Grandmother Mitsu had come twice, and talked to Itsuki through the intercom, and called him an idiot, and cried. Takuya had come once, with his arm still in a sling from being thrown against the training hall wall, and said, very stiffly, that he was glad Itsuki was alive, and then gone away again as fast as he could.

His father had not come.

Now he was standing by the gate of the car park, in the cold, looking at his son.

Itsuki stood still. Rei stood beside him. For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Sōun walked across the car park. He walked slowly, at an ordinary pace, the way he had walked across the training hall toward the thing wearing his son's face. He stopped in front of Itsuki. He looked at him. At the sharper face. At the thinner shoulders. At the small black switches on the backs of his fingers.

"You drew them," he said. "The doors on your back."

"Yes."

"They are very good."

Itsuki did not say anything. Rei could see his jaw working.

"I came to ask you to come home," said Sōun. "To Nezu. Your room is ready. Mitsu has been cooking for a week. The cousins..." He stopped. Started again. "The house is quiet without you."

"No," said Itsuki.

Sōun did not move.

"I'm going to my flat," said Itsuki. "In Ueno. The one you pay for. I'm going to go back to Geidai and paint. I'm going to see my friends." He swallowed. "I'll come and see you. On Sundays, maybe. Sometimes. If you want."

Sōun looked at him for a long time.

Then Kuroda Sōun, head of the Kuroda family, who had knelt among the heads of the seven houses and said *No* to the Division's Director of Public Confidence without explaining why, bowed to his son.

Not a nod. Not the small stiff bend of the neck that he gave to strangers. A real bow. From the waist. Deep, and slow, and held. The kind of bow you give to someone you have wronged.

He held it for five seconds. Then he straightened up, and turned, and walked back across the car park to the gate, and out, and was gone.

Itsuki stood in the cold sun and stared at the empty gate.

"Did that," he said. His voice cracked. "Did that just happen?"

"Yes," said Rei.

"In front of the Division? In a *car park*?"

"Yes."

"Grandmother Mitsu is never going to believe me." Itsuki wiped his face with the back of his hand, hard, and laughed, and it came out wet. "Nobody's going to believe me. I don't believe me."

"I believe you," said Kato, from the car. "I heard him think it. Out loud. On the way out." He did not look up from his phone. "He thought, *my son is braver than I am.*"

Itsuki turned and stared at him.

"Sorry," said Kato. "Was that private? It was really loud."

[break The table]

That night the three of them sat round the kitchen table in the flat on the hill above Ōji station.

It was a small table. It had been built for two. Rei sat where he always sat. Kato sat in Gin's chair, after asking, very carefully, if it was alright, and Rei said yes. Itsuki sat on an upturned crate from the konbini with a cushion on it, because there was no third chair, and complained about it for twenty minutes, and refused to swap.

They ate ginger pork. Rei made it the way he made it for Gin. Too much ginger. Kato said it was the best ginger pork he had ever had. Itsuki said it was the worst ginger pork he had ever had and ate three bowls of rice with it.

They talked about nothing. About Shimizu and her dog. About the Satō watch, which had been back on its shelf in row fifty-one the morning after Rei put it there, exactly where he had put it, which meant nothing, and which they did not talk about for very long. About Fujimoto Kenji in the cold flat full of paper, which they did not talk about for very long either. About the ninth floor, which Kato said he had walked past nine times this week, and which still had no handle. About the white house on the quiet street, and Kanna-sensei, and what they were going to do.

They did not decide what they were going to do. It did not matter yet. It mattered that there were three of them at a table built for two, and nobody had to sit alone.

At midnight Kato fell asleep with his head on his arms on the table, exactly where Itsuki had fallen asleep in August. Itsuki looked at him for a while. Then he took a pencil and a scrap of paper out of his pocket and began to draw. Kato asleep. The king piece on the table beside his hand. The empty rice bowls. Rei, across the table, with the key on its cord round his neck.

"He's okay," said Itsuki quietly, drawing. "Kato. I didn't think he would be. A telepath. I thought he'd be creepy."

"He is creepy."

"He's a bit creepy." Itsuki shaded Kato's hair. "He's nice creepy." He did not look up. "He came down to the basement nine times. Did you know that? Nine times in seven weeks. He never told you. He'd come at eight at night when you'd gone home and stand at the window and tell me stupid jokes through the intercom till I laughed. He said I was thinking too loud and it was keeping him awake on the second floor."

"I didn't know."

"He didn't want you to." Itsuki finished the drawing and looked at it. Then he tore it carefully along the bottom edge and wrote something under it, small, in lowercase, and pushed it across the table to Rei.

It was the three of them. Kato asleep. Rei watching. And a third figure, at the edge of the picture, on a crate, drawing, with his face turned away.

Under it Itsuki had written, *the worst trio in tokyo. jan 2016.*

"I'm going to paint that," he said. "Big. Properly. When I've got my studio back."

"Put Gorō in," said Rei. "He'll be offended if you don't."

"Gorō's already offended. Gorō's always offended." Itsuki took the drawing back and added, very fast, in the corner, a small black crow sitting on the window sill outside the steamed-up glass, glaring in.

They both looked at the real window. Outside, on the sill, in the dark, a crow was sitting with its feathers fluffed up against the cold, glaring in.

"*Kaa,*" it said, muffled, through the glass.

Itsuki laughed until he had to hold his ribs. Kato woke up and said "What? What's funny? Was it about me?" and they could not explain, and that made it worse.

At two in the morning Kato went home in a taxi. Itsuki slept on the sofa, and Rei slept on the floor beside it, because Itsuki said the sofa was his now, by right of being nearly killed. Just before he fell asleep, Rei's phone buzzed on the floor by his head.

It was Itsuki. From the sofa. Half a metre away.

*thank u for the door,* it said. Lowercase. *the one in higashi-jujo. and the other one. the one u kept open.*

Rei looked at it in the dark. Then he typed back.

*always.*

On the sofa, Itsuki's phone buzzed. Rei heard him read it. Heard him breathe out, slowly, in the dark.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them needed to.
