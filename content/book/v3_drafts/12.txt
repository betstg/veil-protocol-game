#title Kato
#part Book One. The Archive

The message came three days after Akabane, from a number Rei did not know.

*Family restaurant by Itabashi station. The one with the green sign. 11pm. Drink bar is on me. Okada says hi, by which I mean Okada said nothing, but she gave me your number, which for Okada is the same as a hug.*

Rei read it twice. Then he went to the back of the archive and turned the torn diary pages until he found the name he was looking for. He had a feeling. He was right.

*Kato Ryusei. Smarter than he presents, which he knows, which makes the performance slightly exhausting. King piece, jacket pocket. Will get you what you need through exactly the channels he pretends he doesn't use. Useful. Not warm. Has said approximately four genuine things to me in two years and they were all correct.*

[break The green sign]

Itabashi is the ward west of Kita. It is mostly flats and small factories and long straight roads. The family restaurant was on a corner by the station, under a big green sign, with tall windows full of yellow light. Family restaurants in Japan are open all night. They have big menus with photos of everything, from spaghetti to rice bowls to chocolate parfaits, and a machine called a drink bar where you pay once and refill your glass as many times as you like. At eleven at night they are full of students studying, taxi drivers eating, and people who do not want to go home.

[need ch12_famires | A Japanese family restaurant at 11pm, big windows, booths, students studying, a trim young man in a neat dark jacket in a corner booth turning a small white chess king in his fingers, glasses of melon soda from the drink bar, Rei sliding in opposite]

He saw Kato Ryusei at once.

He was sitting alone in a corner booth with his back to the wall, where he could see the whole room. He was about twenty. He was trim and neat and very still, in a dark jacket with the sleeves pushed up and a plain white T-shirt underneath. His hair was short and tidy. There were two tall glasses of bright green melon soda on the table in front of him. In his right hand, turning slowly between his fingers, was a small white chess piece. A king.

He did not look up when Rei came in. He said, while Rei was still five metres away, "Yes, I'm the one in the list."

Rei stopped.

"Sit down," said Kato. "You're blocking the waitress. She's about to ask if you want to see the dessert menu. You don't. You want the cheese hamburg steak with rice, but you're going to order fries because you're worried about money." He pushed one of the green glasses across the table. "I got you a melon soda. Everybody likes melon soda. It's the one thing the whole human race agrees on."

Rei sat down. The waitress came. "Would you like to see the dessert..."

"No, thank you," said Rei. "Just fries, please."

The waitress went. Kato turned the king between his fingers and did not smile, quite.

"How did you know that?"

"You were thinking it very loudly." Kato put the king down on the table, standing up, between the two glasses. "That's the first thing you should know about me. I'm a telepath. Level three, which means I hear the surface of people. What you're feeling. What you're about to say. What you want and whether you're lying about it. Not your secrets. Not your memories. Just the top layer. Like the foam on a beer." He shrugged. "It doesn't switch off. It's never switched off. I've been hearing everybody's foam since I was eleven."

He said it lightly, like a joke. His eyes did not look like it was a joke.

"So I'm sorry," he went on. "In advance. I'm going to hear things you don't say. Everyone hates it. Most people get very polite and very careful and think about the weather on purpose. Some people get angry. One guy at the academy threw a chair at me." He picked up his melon soda. "So, you know. Feel free to think about the weather."

Rei looked at him. At the neat jacket. At the king on the table. At the way he sat with his back to the wall, watching the whole room, as if waiting for someone to throw a chair.

He thought, very clearly, *Then just listen. It saves time.*

Kato's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

He looked at Rei. For the first time, his face did something unplanned. His eyebrows went up, and his mouth opened a little, and for a second he looked exactly as old as he was. Twenty. Surprised.

"Huh," he said.

Rei laughed. He did not mean to. It came out of him, the first real laugh since the twelfth of August, because the look on Kato's face was so ridiculous. Kato stared at him laughing, with his melon soda still in the air, and did not seem to know what to do with his own face.

"Nobody's ever," Kato started, and stopped. He put the glass down. He picked up the king again and turned it, faster. "Okay. That's new. That's new information. I'm going to need a minute with that."

"Take a minute."

"I'm taking a minute." He turned the king. "Okay. Minute taken." He cleared his throat. "Your brother did that, you know. The first time we met. I apologised, and he thought, *I'd rather you heard it than guessed it.* Very loud. Very clear. Then he asked me to pass the soy sauce like nothing happened." He looked at the king. "I've been thinking about that for two years."

[break The desk]

The fries came. Rei ate them. Kato stole three without asking and then said "sorry" and pushed the plate back.

"Right," said Kato. "Business. Okada says you found a thing on the river. A hunger thing, looking out through rats. She said you threw iron at it, which is either the bravest or the stupidest thing a civilian has done this year, and that you got a lady's name and three files that the Division didn't have." He took a folded paper from inside his jacket. "So I went and looked."

"Looked where?"

"In the building. Where I work. I'm a trainee. Second year. I make copies, I carry boxes, I get shouted at, I'm basically you with a lanyard." He unfolded the paper. "But I have a nice face and I talk to everyone and nobody notices me. And I can hear what people in the copy room are thinking while they wait for the copier. You would not believe what people think in a copy room."

He turned the paper round. It was a list of addresses. Seven of them. All along the Arakawa. All in the last two months.

"Your hunger thing," said Kato. "Seven deaths, not three. Elderly, alone, heart failure, rats. Somebody in the building put the pattern together a month ago and flagged it." He tapped the bottom of the page, where someone had written a short note and a reference number. "And then somebody else, two days later, closed the flag. *No action. Monitor only.* Signed by an office I'm not allowed to name in a family restaurant."

"Public Confidence."

Kato did not answer. That was an answer.

"Why would they leave it?" Rei said. "It's killing people."

"Old people. Alone. In the summer. Heart failure." Kato's voice was flat. "If the Division goes in loud along the river, people ask why. If people ask why, they want to know what else is moving in the city. And there's a lot moving in the city right now. Much more than there should be." He folded the paper again. "Somebody has decided that seven quiet deaths are cheaper than one loud question."

Rei put down his fry. He was not hungry any more.

"There's something else," said Kato. He hesitated. It was the first time he had hesitated about anything. "About your brother."

Rei waited.

"His desk. In the field office. Third floor, by the window, the one with the cactus. I sit four desks away." Kato turned the king over in his fingers, slowly. "Last Friday, two people from the office I can't name came up at seven in the evening, after everyone left. They emptied it. Every drawer. Every file. His computer. The cactus." He looked at the table. "The cactus, Rei. Who takes a man's cactus? You only take the cactus if you don't think he's coming back to water it."

Rei sat very still. The restaurant went on around him. A student at the next table yawned and turned a page. A taxi driver ordered a second bowl of rice. The drink bar machine hummed.

"I wasn't supposed to see it," said Kato. "I came back for my phone charger. They didn't see me. They didn't hear me." He paused. "I heard them. One of them was thinking about whether the family had been told yet. And the other one was thinking, very clearly, *Thank God it's not our problem any more. It's upstairs now.*"

"What does that mean? Upstairs?"

"I don't know." Kato looked up at him. "I don't know, and I'm scared to find out, and I've been thinking about it for a week. And then Okada gave me your number, and I thought, Gin's brother. Gin's brother is out there throwing iron at rat demons. Gin's brother would want to know." He put the king down, standing, in the middle of the table. "So I'm telling you. I'm probably going to get fired for it. Maybe worse. I don't really care. Your brother was the only person in that building who ever asked me to pass the soy sauce."

[break What Kato wanted]

"Why are you helping me?" said Rei. "Really."

Kato looked at him for a long moment. Rei had the feeling of being heard. Not his words. Underneath. It was like standing in the sun with your eyes closed and feeling it on your face.

"Do you want the funny answer or the real one?" said Kato.

"The real one."

"Nobody ever wants the real one." Kato almost smiled. "Okay. The real one is, I've spent ten years making jokes so people forget I can hear them. It works, mostly. People laugh. They relax. They forget. And then they remember, and they look at me the way you look at a camera in a changing room." He turned the king one more time and put it in his pocket. "And you just sat down and thought *listen, it saves time*, like it was nothing. Like I was a person and not a camera." He shrugged. "Gin did that too. So. I figure it runs in the family. I figure it's worth being around."

Rei did not know what to say. So he thought it instead, as clearly as he could. *Thank you.*

Kato heard it. Rei watched him hear it. Something happened at the corners of his eyes, and he looked away quickly at the window, at the dark road outside, at the reflection of the restaurant in the glass.

"Don't do that," he said. "Don't think nice things at me. I don't know what to do with my face."

"Sorry."

"You're not sorry. I can hear you not being sorry." Kato stood up and pulled his jacket straight. "I'll find out what *upstairs* means. It'll take time. Don't call me at the office. Don't text me anything you wouldn't want read out in court. If you need me, send me a photo of a cat."

"A cat?"

"Any cat. Everyone sends cats. Nobody reads them." He put a thousand-yen note on the table for the drink bar and the fries. "And Rei. The rats. The thing on the river. Seven people in two months, and nobody's going to stop it. It's going to do it again."

"I know."

"I know you know. I can hear you knowing." Kato looked at him one more time. The joke had gone out of his face completely. "I can also hear you thinking you're going to go back and stop it yourself. With a jar of iron and your friend who paints."

Rei said nothing.

"Okay," said Kato. "Just checking. Send me a cat when you go."

He walked out under the green sign into the night, with his hands in his pockets, and did not look back.

Rei sat alone in the booth for a long time with two glasses of melon soda. Then he took out his phone and looked for a photograph of a cat. He did not have one. He went outside and walked three streets until he found a stray tabby washing itself on top of a car, and took its photograph, and saved it, and went home.
