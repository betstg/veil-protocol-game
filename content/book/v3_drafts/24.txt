#title The Door
#part Book Two. The Families

The Division came in forty minutes. Not the blue suits. The other ones.

Four people in grey with the white owl on their chests, carrying a folding stretcher and two heavy metal cases. A woman in her forties led them, short and broad, with a calm face and hands that never stopped moving. She knelt beside Itsuki on the blood-drawn floor and laid her palm flat on his breastbone, the way Grandmother Mitsu had, and closed her eyes for a long time.

"Class C," she said at last. "Threshold dweller. Wrapped round the spinal cord from the third vertebra down. Not driving. Sleeping. Very tight." She opened her eyes and looked at Sōun. "You did well to pull it off the top. If it had stayed in the brain he'd be gone."

Sōun said nothing. He was still kneeling with his son's head in his lap.

"We'll take him to Reintegration," said the woman. "Akabane. We have a room ready. It's a slow process. Weeks. Maybe months." She was already opening one of the metal cases. Inside, packed in grey foam, were rolls of white paper covered in black writing, and small heavy iron bars, and a glass jar full of something that looked like coarse grey sand. "He'll be sealed in. No visitors inside the room. Family can come to the door."

"I'll come," said Rei.

The woman looked up at him for the first time. She looked at his face. At the blood on his hands from the floor. At his left wrist.

"You're not family," she said.

"I'm his family," said Rei.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she went back to her case. "The door, then," she said. "Only the door."

[break Reintegration]

[img cvdroom_containment_reintegration.png | Reintegration, Akabane]

The Reintegration ward was in the basement of the Division building in Akabane, two floors under the street, at the end of a long corridor with a polished floor that reflected the lights in the ceiling like water.

Down both sides of the corridor were doors. Heavy grey doors with a small window in each one at head height, a square of thick glass with wire inside it. Round each door, on the walls, someone had painted seals in black ink. Circles inside circles. Lines of tiny writing. Shapes Rei half knew from the back of the archive. The air smelled of disinfectant and old paper and, under that, very faint, of something sweet and burnt, like sugar left too long in a pan.

His wrist was cold the whole way down the corridor.

Itsuki's room was the ninth on the left. Through the small window Rei could see a white bed, a white chair, a white table. Walls covered with white paper sheets, each one written all over in black. Iron bars set into the floor in a square round the bed. And Itsuki, lying very still on the bed, with his left arm in a hard grey cast and his chest strapped tight under a white hospital shirt, and his eyes closed.

Okada was standing in the corridor outside the door when Rei arrived on the second day. She had a paper cup of tea in each hand.

"I heard," she said, and gave him one. "I work in this building. I hear everything eventually."

"They won't let me in."

"No. Nobody goes in. Not for weeks. The seals have to settle." She looked through the little window at Itsuki. "But there's an intercom. And I know the nurse." She pressed a small grey button beside the door. "Five minutes. Every day. That's what I could get."

The button made a click. Then a soft hiss of static.

"Itsuki?" said Rei.

On the bed, Itsuki opened his eyes.

[break Through the door]

"Rei?"

His voice came through the little speaker thin and crackly, like a voice on an old radio. But it was his voice. In the right order.

"Yeah. It's me."

"You sound terrible."

"*I* sound terrible? You've got three broken ribs and a demon in your spine."

"Two broken ribs. One's just cracked. The nurse said." A pause. "The demon's fine, thanks for asking."

Rei laughed. It came out of him like a cough, half a sob. Beside him, Okada looked at the floor.

Through the window he could see Itsuki turn his head on the white pillow, slowly, toward the door. Both his eyes were the same now. Dark and tired and ordinary. His face moved on both sides when he talked. His nose was not bleeding.

"I can feel it," said Itsuki. "Right here. In my back. Like a cold wire. When I'm awake it's quiet. When I sleep..." He stopped. "When I sleep I dream about doors. All the doors I could open. It shows them to me. It wants me to open them."

"Don't open them."

"I won't open them. I'm not stupid." A pause. "I'm a little bit stupid."

"You're very stupid."

"Thank you. That's very supportive." Itsuki closed his eyes again. "Is my father here?"

Rei looked at Okada. Okada shook her head very slightly.

"No," said Rei.

"Has he come?"

"Not yet."

"Okay." Itsuki's voice did not change. "That's okay. He's busy. He's got a family to run." A long silence. "Did he say anything? After?"

Rei thought of the training hall. Sōun kneeling with his son's head in his lap, his hand shaking on Itsuki's hair. *Call the Division. Tell them the Kuroda need help.* The shame in his face.

He thought of the cousin. *Bones of the trade.*

"He said to call the Division," said Rei. "He said, tell them the Kuroda need help."

Through the window he saw Itsuki's eyes open.

"He said that? Out loud?"

"Yes."

"In front of everyone?"

"Yes."

Itsuki lay very still for a long time, looking at the white ceiling.

"Huh," he said at last. Very quietly. Exactly the way Kato had said it in the family restaurant, when someone surprised him for the first time in years.

The intercom clicked. Five minutes.

"Tomorrow," said Rei, quickly. "Same time."

"Bring me something to draw with," said Itsuki. "They won't give me pens. They say I'll draw doors on the walls. They're probably right."

"I'll ask."

"And Rei."

"Yeah."

"Don't go back to Nezu," said Itsuki. "Not without me. I mean it. The threshold's not safe for you. You're not a Kuroda. And my family will call you bones too, if it happens to you. They'll be very sad and they'll call you bones." The static hissed. "I'm not having that. Okay? Promise."

The intercom clicked off before Rei could answer.

[break Every day]

He came every day for two weeks.

He came in the morning before work and in the evening after. The nurse, a tired kind man called Nomura, let him have five minutes at each end of the day instead of one, because Okada asked him to and because, he said, Rei was the only visitor Room Nine got. He brought a box of soft charcoal pencils and a thick pad of grey paper. The nurse checked every page and every pencil, and passed them through a slot in the door, and every evening took the finished pages out again and checked them for doors. There were never any doors. There were trains. There were crows. There was a fat old cat asleep on a cushion. There was Rei, again and again, from the other side of the little window, with his face too close to the glass.

They talked through the door.

About nothing, mostly. About what was on television. About the food in Reintegration, which Itsuki said was the worst food in Japan and possibly in history. About Gorō, who had followed Rei to Akabane and now sat on the wall of the Division car park every morning, glaring at the building. About Kato, who had come down to the basement one evening on some excuse and stood by the window and made Itsuki laugh so hard his ribs hurt and the nurse had to come and tell them to stop.

Sometimes, in the middle of a sentence, Itsuki's words would come out in the wrong order. *Hungry I'm. Window the.* He would stop, and close his eyes, and breathe, and start again. Neither of them talked about it.

Kuroda Sōun did not come.

On the twelfth day, Mrs. Fujii came instead. She stood in the corridor in her dark kimono, very small and very straight, holding a cloth-wrapped box. Inside it, she told the nurse, was rice cooked with red beans, the dish you make in Japan for a celebration or for someone who is getting better. She had made it herself. She did not ask to speak to Itsuki. She asked the nurse to pass it through the slot, and bowed to the closed door, very low, for a long time. Then she bowed to Rei.

"The young master's father has not left the old wing for twelve days," she said quietly. "He sits in front of the gate. He does not eat. He does not sleep." She looked at the floor. "I thought someone should know."

Then she went away down the long polished corridor, her white socks making no sound at all.

[break The choice]

On the fourteenth day, at the end of five minutes, Itsuki said, "Are you going back? To the family? To train?"

"No."

"Good." A crackle of static. "Because of what I said?"

"Because of what they said." Rei leaned his forehead on the cold glass of the little window. "Bones of the trade. They said it like it was the weather. They're good people, Itsuki. Your grandmother Mitsu. Takuya. Even your father, maybe. But they've been doing this for four hundred years. They've lost so many people they've got a name for it." He closed his eyes. "I'm not going to be one of their bones. And I'm not going to watch you be one."

"So what are you going to do?"

Rei had been thinking about it for two weeks, standing in this corridor, looking through this glass.

The families voted on Gin like a temple in Saitama. The Grid was brave and kind and full of people who would sell your name for a couch. Tanizaki taught him to sit. Haruki taught him to rest. Okada had lied in a report to keep him safe. And every day, for fourteen days, she had stood in this corridor with two cups of tea, in this building, two floors under the street, under the noses of the people who had emptied his brother's desk.

You could not find out what was on the ninth floor from outside the building.

"I'm going to call the front of the card," said Rei.

Through the window, Itsuki turned his head on the pillow and looked at him.

"The front," he said. "Not the back."

"The front."

"Okada said never the front."

"I know."

Itsuki was quiet for a long time. The static hissed softly in the little speaker.

"They'll write down what you are," he said.

"Yes."

"They'll put you on a list."

"Yes."

"And then you'll be inside." It was not a question. Itsuki was smiling, a little, on both sides of his face. "Inside the building. With the ninth floor."

"Yes."

"You're an idiot," said Itsuki softly.

"I know."

"I'm going to draw you a really good picture for it," said Itsuki. "Your first day. In a terrible suit. I'll put it on the wall in here. Nomura will check it for doors."

The intercom clicked off.

Rei stood in the corridor with his forehead on the glass. Behind him, at the far end, the lift doors opened, and Okada came out with two paper cups of tea. She walked toward him down the polished floor, and stopped, and looked at his face.

She did not ask. She had read his file twice, the way she read everything. She already knew.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "Nine o'clock. Assessment, third floor." She gave him the tea. "Wear a suit. If you don't have one, I'll lend you money for one." She turned away. "And Rei."

"Yes?"

"When they measure you," said Okada, not looking at him, "don't open the door in your chest any further than you have to. Let them think you're less than you are." She walked back toward the lift. "Your brother did."
