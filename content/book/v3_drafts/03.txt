#title The Book

The Kita Ward Police headquarters stood on the main avenue, ten minutes' walk from the CVD, and it did not look like a tax office. It looked like a police station, which in its own way was worse.

[img off_police.png | Kita Ward Police]

Rei had worked there for eighteen months as a criminal investigation intern. That sounds more exciting than it was. Mostly it meant photocopying, and filing, and carrying boxes up and down stairs, and making tea for detectives who did not say thank you. Sometimes it meant going out to a scene with one of the senior officers and standing behind the tape holding a clipboard while they did the real work. He liked those days and hated them in equal amounts. He liked them because he was good at it. He would stand at the edge of a room where something bad had happened, and before anyone told him anything he would know where it had happened, and roughly how, and sometimes roughly why. The detectives thought he had a good eye. He hated them because of what came home with him afterward. Eighteen months of other people's worst days, soaked into him a little at a time, the way smoke soaks into a coat. He dealt with that the way a lot of nineteen-year-olds deal with things. Badly, and with cigarettes, and by not thinking about it.

(He had never told Gin about the scenes. Gin had never asked. Gin had simply started leaving a clean shirt on the back of the bathroom door on the evenings Rei came home from one, and a pot of hōjicha on the table, and had never said a word about either. The Uedera brothers were extremely good at not saying things. It was, in a way, the family business.)

He was late. He had never been late before. The sergeant on the front desk looked at his face and at his creased shirt from the night before and said nothing, and waved him through.

[break The form]

His supervisor was a tired inspector in his fifties named Kumagai, who had a desk by the window on the second floor and a framed photograph of a very ugly dog. Rei waited until Kumagai had finished his coffee, because that was the rule on the second floor, and then stood in front of his desk and asked for three days' leave, and for a missing person report.

Kumagai looked at him over his glasses.

"Who's missing?"

"My brother. Uedera Gin. Twenty-eight. He didn't come home last night. He doesn't answer his phone. His key was outside our door at quarter to four this morning."

"Your brother's the CVD one."

"Yes."

Kumagai took his glasses off and rubbed the red marks on either side of his nose. He was not an unkind man. He was a man who had been doing this job for thirty years, and the job had worn him down to the shape of the job.

"Uedera," he said, "it's Obon. Your brother works for the Civil Veil Division. In Obon week those people don't sleep, they don't eat, and they don't call their mothers. He's been gone, what, one night?"

"He always comes home."

"He's twenty-eight." Kumagai leaned back in his chair until it creaked. "Twenty-eight, single, works nights, and it's a festival. Come on, Uedera. You've been here long enough. You know how many grown men we get reported missing on a Friday night who turn up on Saturday morning with a headache and a girl's phone number written on their hand?" He smiled, not unkindly. "He went for a drink after his shift. Somebody bought him another one. He fell asleep on a friend's sofa in Akabane. Happens every week."

"Gin doesn't drink like that."

"Everybody drinks like that at Obon."

"He doesn't. He has one beer and then he goes home. He hates bars. He says they're loud and the toilets are always wet." Rei heard his own voice getting faster and could not stop it. "He reads every message I send him. Every single one. Even when he's working. Last night he didn't read any of them. He didn't read *one*."

"So his phone died."

"His key was outside our door."

"So he dropped it." Kumagai spread his hands. "He was drunk, he dropped his key on the way out, he went back down the stairs to look for a taxi, and he's asleep in some love hotel in Ikebukuro right now with his shoes on. I've done it myself. Not the love hotel. The shoes."

"He didn't go out. He went to *work*. He left at six in the morning. He never came back."

"Uedera." Kumagai's voice went patient, which was worse. "Listen to yourself. Your big brother didn't come home for one night, and you've been up all night working yourself into a state, and now you've come in here looking like you've been dug up, asking me to open a missing person file on a CVD officer in the middle of Obon. You know what they'd say upstairs if I wrote that? *Adult male, did not come home after a public festival. No sign of a crime.* They'd laugh me out of the building."

"I went to the CVD. They said he's *on assignment.*"

"Well, then." Kumagai picked up a pen, which on the second floor meant the conversation was over. "He's on assignment. Or he's in a love hotel. Either way he's a grown man and he's not my case. Take today. Go home. Get some sleep. If he's not back in three or four days, come and see me and we'll think again."

"Three or four days."

"Adults don't go missing after one night, Uedera. They go out."

Rei stood in front of the desk with his hands at his sides. He could feel the key in his trouser pocket, against his leg, cold through the cloth. He wanted to put it on the desk next to the photograph of the ugly dog and say, *feel that, feel it, tell me that's a man who went out.* He did not. He had seen enough people do that kind of thing at that kind of desk, over eighteen months, to know exactly how it ended. They ended up in the small room downstairs with a cup of tea and a female officer being very kind to them.

"Thank you, sir," he said, and bowed, and went.

[break Arai]

On the stairs he passed Detective Sergeant Arai Makoto, who was coming up with a paper cup of vending machine coffee and a face like a closed shop.

Rei had worked in the building for eighteen months and had spoken to Arai perhaps three times. Arai was forty-eight, with grey brush-cut hair and a crumpled suit and a cheap watch, and he worked the cases that nobody else on the second floor wanted. The ones that did not fit. The deaths with no cause, the rooms that felt wrong, the files that got written up very carefully and then handed up some quiet channel and never mentioned again. People said he had been good once. People said a lot of things about Arai, quietly, when he was not there.

He stopped two steps above Rei and looked at him. Not at his face. At his hands. Rei realised, too late, that his right hand was in his trouser pocket, closed tight round the key.

"Uedera," said Arai flatly. "The intern."

"Yes, sir."

"Kumagai says no?"

Rei looked up at him, startled. Arai drank some of his coffee and made a face at it.

"Walls are thin," he said. "And you talk like a man reading a statement when you're upset. Everybody on that floor heard it." He looked at Rei's pocket again. "Your brother's CVD."

"Yes."

"Kita district. Long hair. Polite. Smokes half a cigarette and puts the rest out like it owes him money." Arai shrugged at Rei's face. "I've stood behind the same tape as him. Four or five times. He's careful. He's the kind who checks the floor before he puts his feet down." He was quiet for a moment. "What's in your pocket?"

Rei hesitated. Then he took the key out and held it up on his palm, the way he had held it out to the young man in the CVD lobby that morning.

Arai did not reach for it either. He looked at it for a long moment, with his tired, watchful eyes. A little bead of water was running down the steel ring, though the stairwell was hot.

"Put that away," he said quietly. "Don't show that to people."

"Why?"

Arai did not answer. He went up two more steps, past Rei, with his coffee. Then he stopped again without turning round.

"Kumagai's right about the form," he said to the wall. "There's no form for that. Not in this building." He started up the stairs again. "Go home, Uedera."

[break Night one]

Itsuki came at seven with his laptop and two bags of food from the konbini and his hair still full of blue paint.

He did not say anything when Rei opened the door. He just looked at him, and put the bags down in the genkan, and put both arms round him and held on, hard, for about five seconds, with his chin on Rei's shoulder and his laptop bag banging against Rei's back. Then he let go and went into the kitchen and started taking things out of the bags as if nothing had happened, talking about a man on the train who had been eating a whole cabbage like an apple.

That was Itsuki. You got exactly five seconds, and then a cabbage.

He set the laptop up on Gin's desk, next to the lamp with the green shade, beside the closed black book. He did not touch the book. He moved it very carefully two centimetres to the left to make room, with one finger, and then looked at Rei, and moved it back.

"Sorry," he said. "Reflex."

The program was on Gin's old computer, the one he used for the case notes he brought home. Itsuki typed. Itsuki swore. Itsuki typed more. Rei sat on the end of Gin's bed with his knees up and watched him and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say.

"Okay," said Itsuki at last, very quietly. "Last file he opened. Wednesday. Two fourteen in the morning."

He turned the screen round.

It was a scan of one of his own drawings. Rei knew it from the folder that morning. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table in the middle, and nothing else in it at all.

"I hate that drawing," said Itsuki.

"Why?"

"I don't know." He closed the laptop. "It makes me feel like I've been there."

They sat in the kitchen until late, not saying much. They ate the konbini food without tasting it. Rei smoked three cigarettes by the open window, one after another, and Itsuki took one from the packet and smoked half of it and then stopped with it halfway to his mouth, because he had remembered whose habit that was. He put it out very quickly against the windowsill and did not light another.

At midnight he fell asleep with his head on his arms on the kitchen table, the way he used to when they were fourteen and Gin had kept them up doing maths. Rei put a blanket over him. Then he sat across from him in the dark with the key on the table between them, and did not sleep, and at 3:47 he looked up and listened, and nothing happened at all.

[break Day two]

On the second day he went back to the CVD.

A different young man at the counter. The same smile. The same twenty-five minutes on the plastic chair under the white owl. The same words. *On assignment. Extended operations. As soon as he's able.* This time there was a business card, too, held out with both hands and a little bow, for a Public Confidence officer whose name Rei did not recognise and who, when he rang the number from the pavement outside, did not answer.

He walked the routes he knew Gin walked. Down the hill to the station. Along the river to the floodgate. Past the shrine where the dance had been, the tower already half taken down, a pile of red and white lanterns stacked on the steps. He stood outside the konbini at the bottom of the street where Gin bought his coffee every morning and asked the girl at the till if she had seen a tall man with long hair, in a dark coat, who always bought the same black canned coffee and said thank you in a voice like a radio announcer. She thought about it. She said he had come in two days ago, early, about six. He had bought his coffee. He had said thank you. He had seemed fine.

*He had seemed fine.*

Rei walked home and stood in the genkan and looked at the empty brass hook for a long time.

He did not eat. He sent Gin eleven messages. He called Gin's phone until the battery of his own phone ran down, and then he plugged it in and called some more. Itsuki texted every hour, short messages in lowercase, *anything?* and *eat something* and *i'm coming over at 7 again dont argue*. Haruto texted once, a photo of the river with the words *is he back yet*, and Rei looked at it for a long time and could not think how to answer a seventeen-year-old who wanted to be taken to see the lanterns.

At six o'clock that evening he went back to the police station.

He did not go to Kumagai. He went to the front desk and filled in the missing person form himself, every box, in his neatest handwriting, the way he had watched a hundred frightened people fill it in. Name. Age. Height. Clothing when last seen. Distinguishing features. *Long black hair past the jaw. Tattoos on both arms. Carries a black notebook.* He signed it and handed it over.

The sergeant on the front desk was a big cheerful man called Ono who had worked there for twenty years and called all the interns *kid*. He read the form slowly, from the top to the bottom, moving his lips a little. Then he looked up at Rei with a kind, crinkly, completely useless smile.

"Kid," he said. "Kumagai told me you might come down. He said three or four days."

"It's been two."

"Two days." Ono nodded, very sympathetic. "It's Obon, kid. Half the men in Kita are sleeping it off somewhere right now. Your brother probably met someone at the festival and went home with her, and now he's too embarrassed to call." He winked. "Good-looking man, is he? Long hair? Well, there you go. Give him till Monday. He'll walk in with a hangover and a stupid grin, and you'll feel like an idiot, and we'll all have a good laugh."

He slid the form back across the counter.

Rei looked at it.

He thought about the plate of ginger pork going grey on the counter. The word *Delivered* that would not change. The brass hook with nothing on it. The key that had been cold for forty hours and had not warmed up once. Someone at the festival. A hangover and a stupid grin.

He picked up the form and folded it in half, very neatly, along the line, and then in half again. He put it in his pocket, next to the key. And something inside him that had been holding very still for two days, very carefully, the way you carry a glass that is full to the brim, tipped over.

"Fuck this," he said. Quite quietly. And then not quietly at all. "Fuck this. Fuck the form. Fuck Monday. Fuck *some girl at the festival*. My brother didn't go home with any woman. He's not like that. He's gone, and nobody in this building gives a shit, and I'm done asking."

Ono stared at him with his mouth open. Two officers by the door turned round. A woman sitting on the bench by the wall with a little boy put her hands over the boy's ears.

"Sorry," said Rei to the woman. He meant it. He bowed to her, and to the boy. Then he turned round and walked out of the Kita Ward Police headquarters, and he did not bow to anybody else on the way.

[break The book]

He went home and stood in the doorway of Gin's room.

It was half past seven. Itsuki was on his way. The sky outside the window was turning the colour of a bruise, and the lamp with the green shade was off, and the book lay where it always lay, closed, squared to the edge of the desk, waiting.

Rei had never opened it. In all his life, not once. He had watched Gin write in it at the kitchen table a thousand times, page after page in that small exact hand, and Gin had always closed it, gently, without hurry, when Rei came near. And Rei had never minded. It was Gin's. Some things you leave alone because you love the person they belong to, and that had always been enough.

It was not enough now.

He sat down in Gin's chair. He switched on the lamp. The circle of yellow light fell on the black cover, worn soft and pale at the corners from years of being carried in a coat pocket.

"Sorry," he said, out loud, to nobody. To Gin. "Sorry. You can shout at me later."

He opened it.

[img res_archive.png | Gin's book]

The first page was not about ghosts. It was not about the CVD or the veil or anything that had happened on the twelfth of August. It was dated a long time ago, years ago, and it began with coffee.

[doc Foreword. Written in the morning.]
Coffee going cold on the left. I almost forgot my keys again today. I do this every morning. I put them on the hook by the door because I read somewhere that building habits around physical anchors helps people like us stay grounded. People whose minds tend to slip sideways before the rest of them catches up. I put them on the hook. I make coffee. I smoke half a cigarette on the step outside because Rei complains about the smell in the apartment even though he leaves his own cigarettes everywhere. I come back in lightheaded and slightly late and reach for the keys and they are exactly where I put them and I am surprised every time.

Rei thinks this is funny. He has started moving them slightly, just a few centimetres, to see if I notice. I notice. I don't tell him I notice. It's the most reliable part of my morning.
[/doc]

Rei stopped reading.

He sat in his brother's chair with both hands flat on the desk on either side of the book, and he did not cry, because he did not know how to start and was afraid that if he did he would not know how to stop. He sat there and breathed. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. The latch dropped in his chest. He took the key out of his pocket and put it on the desk, in the circle of lamplight, next to the book, and it lay there, cold, with its little bead of water.

Then he turned the page.

[doc Foreword, continued.]
If you are reading this and I am not available to explain it, use it. All of it. The official sections are accurate. The personal notes are more accurate. The things I have not written explicitly but have gestured toward in the margins, those are the most accurate of all, and I trust you to read them correctly.

If you are reading this and I am still around, hello. Stop reading my notes. Go eat something.

Rei, if this is you, and I know there is a reasonable chance this is you, the rent is paid through the end of the month. The contact number on page 47 is someone who will help you if you ask correctly. Do not lead with what you need. Ask how they are first. They will respect that.
[/doc]

Rei read that part three times.

*Ask how they are first.* That was Gin. That was exactly Gin. He had written it years ago, on some ordinary morning with his coffee going cold, for a day he hoped would never come, the way people write down where they keep the spare fuses. Rei could almost hear him saying it, in that low patient voice. *Question seven, Itsuki. Ask how they are first, Rei.*

He found page 47. There was a name on it, and a phone number, and the name meant nothing to him at all. He wrote it on the back of his hand in biro, the way Gin would have hated, and kept reading.

When Itsuki let himself in at eight through the door Rei had left unlocked for him, with two more bags of konbini food, he found Rei at the desk under the green lamp with the book open in front of him and a name written on the back of his hand, reading. He did not look up.

Itsuki stood in the doorway for a moment. Then he put the bags down very quietly and went and sat on the end of Gin's bed with his knees up, and waited, and did not say anything at all.

At a quarter to four in the morning Rei turned a page and found a single line, written in a slightly different ink, as if it had been added later, at the bottom of a page about how thin the veil grows in August.

[doc]
If you are reading this at 3:47 in the morning, that is approximately when Rei found it. That detail is in this book for a reason. Keep reading.
[/doc]

He looked at the clock on the desk.

It was 3:47.
