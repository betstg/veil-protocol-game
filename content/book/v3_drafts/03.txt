#title The Key
#part Book One. The Archive

When Rei got home the flat was hot and smelled of cold ginger pork. He opened every window. The cicadas came in with the heat, a solid wall of noise from the trees on the hill, like a hundred small machines that would never be switched off.

He lifted the plate off the plate. The pork had gone grey. The rice had dried at the edges. He scraped it into the bin and washed both plates and dried them and put them away. It felt important to do it properly. If he did small things properly, the big thing would wait.

Then he went to Gin's door.

He had not opened it last night. He had not wanted to. Now he knocked once, softly, feeling stupid, and pushed it open.

[img res_apartmentginroom.png | Gin's room]

The bed was made with square corners. The chair was pushed all the way under the desk. The window was shut and the blind was half down, so the room was full of a dim yellow light. And the desk lamp was on. A small circle of light lay on the bare wood of the desk, as if Gin had been working under it a minute ago and had stepped out to take a call.

In the circle of light lay Gin's archive.

That was what Gin called it. It was a thick field book with a black cover gone soft at the corners, and Gin carried it everywhere. Into places Rei was not allowed to know about, and out again, year after year. He wrote in it at the kitchen table late at night, and he always closed it when Rei came near. He did not leave it on tables. He did not leave it anywhere. And yet here it was, open, squared to the edge of the desk, as if someone had wanted it found by the first person to sit down.

Rei sat down.

It was open at the first page. The handwriting was Gin's, small and exact, the handwriting of a man who trusted ink more than memory.

[doc Personal Archive. Opening Note]
If you found this: the keys were on the hook because I put them there. The lamp was on because I was working. The door was not quite closed because I heard something in the corridor and I looked up and then I kept writing and I forgot to go back and close it.
Whatever you are looking for... the world is in here. I documented everything I know. Six years of field work, case files, entity taxonomy, practitioner profiles, the political landscape of who controls what and why. The archive is complete. It functions without me.
Start at chapter one. Read in order the first time. The world builds on itself and some things will not make sense until other things do. The index is in the field kit, left pocket.
I was going to annotate the later sections more thoroughly. I ran out of time. The notes in the margins are the annotations. Read the margins.
Uedera Gin. Kita-ku, Tokyo, 2015.
[/doc]

Rei read it twice. Then he sat back in the chair and looked at the room.

The keys had not been on the hook. They had been on the walkway, cold and wet, at 3:47 in the morning. The note had been written for a different night. A night when Gin heard something outside, looked up, and went back to his writing. It had not been written for a night when he got up and went out to see what it was.

Or it had been written for this night, by someone who already knew he would not come back. Rei did not know which was worse.

He turned the page. The next page was headed *The World*. It was dense, careful writing about openings and membranes, the kind of thing Gin said at dinner when he was tired and forgot who he was talking to. Halfway down, one sentence stopped Rei so hard that he put his palm flat on the desk.

[doc Personal Archive. The World]
If you are reading this at 3:47 in the morning: that is approximately when Rei found it. That detail is in the archive for a reason. Keep reading.
[/doc]

He had not found it at 3:47. At 3:47 someone had knocked on the door. He had found the key at 3:47.

He looked at the time on his phone. It was 11:20 in the morning. The pressure began behind his eyes, slow and heavy, and he let it come for a moment, because he was alone and he was tired of always closing the door. The room changed. The yellow light went thin and greyish, like light through old paper. The air had a smell under it, very faint, of salt and of something burnt. And on the desk, in the circle of lamplight, there were two shapes pressed into the dust that he had not seen before. The shapes of two hands. Large hands, Gin's size, laid flat on the wood on either side of the book. Holding it down. Or holding on.

Rei shut the door in his chest. The latch dropped. The room was a room again. There was no dust on the desk. Gin never let dust stay anywhere.

[break What the key is]

He read for three hours without stopping.

He did not understand most of it. There were words he had never seen. Register. Membrane. Stability. Rank. There were tables of numbers and drawings of circles and long lists of case numbers with short notes beside them. Case 7-114. Elderly woman in Nerima. *Cohabiting with her late husband for four years.* Case 11-08. The Car. *The entity was not in the apartment. It was in the car.* He recognised some of the drawings. They were Itsuki's. A staircase with a pale blue smudge on the fourth step. A car seen from above. Gin had glued them into the margins and written beside them in pencil.

And in the margins, there were other notes. Not about cases. About Rei.

*A Wednesday. Rei missed his exam prep again. We ate ramen. He fell asleep on the couch before I finished cleaning up.*

*Rei's light is still on under his door. He's supposed to be studying. I knocked and asked if he wanted food. He said he already ate. I checked the kitchen. He hadn't.*

*Rei is in the next room. I can hear him. That has been the most stable data point in the archive for six months.*

Rei stopped reading for a while after that one.

Then, about two hundred pages in, he found a section called *Grounding Objects*. He read it slowly, the way you read instructions for medicine.

Every practitioner, Gin wrote, has one object that keeps them in the world. It is not magic. It is something ordinary that you have carried for a long time and that means something to you. A grandfather's lighter. A bracelet. A photograph. When the veil gets too loud, when the job puts too much into you, you hold the object and it holds you. The longer you carry it, the more of you goes into it. After six years, Gin wrote, a grounding object is like a complete file on the person who carried it. Anyone with the right sight could read the whole of you from it. *That is why experienced practitioners do not leave their grounding objects in the field.*

Then one more line, underlined.

*It is also why the keychain I carry has been in my possession for six years. If something has happened to me, treat it as sensitive material.*

Rei took the key out of his pocket and put it on the desk in the circle of light.

A brass door key on a plain steel ring, with a small dent in the ring from a car door years ago. It looked like nothing. It looked like a thousand other keys in a thousand other pockets in this city.

It was Gin's grounding object. It had been with him for six years. And it had been lying on the concrete outside the door at 3:47 in the morning, wet and cold, next to half a cigarette.

Gin had not dropped it. Gin would never have dropped it. Someone had put it there for Rei to find. Or Gin had put it there himself, with his own hands, in the one moment he had them.

Rei picked the key up again. It was still cold. But the floor under the chair felt steadier, and the room felt more like a room, and the pressure behind his eyes stayed where it was. He understood something then, without words for it yet. Gin had carried this key for six years to stay in the world. Now Rei was carrying it. And it was working for him too.

He put it on a cord from the kitchen drawer and hung it round his neck, under his shirt, where he could feel it against his chest. It lay there like a cold coin.

[break The phone call]

At two in the afternoon he called the CVD.

There was a number on the posters in every train station. Rei had never called it in his life. A recorded voice thanked him for calling the Civil Veil Division and asked him to choose from five options. He chose the fifth. *All other enquiries.* He waited for nine minutes while a piano played the same eight bars of something soft.

Then a woman answered. She had a pleasant voice, warm and smooth, the voice of someone who had said these words a thousand times and still sounded like she meant them.

Rei explained. His brother. Uedera Gin. Field officer, Kita district. Did not come home last night. The front door. The key. He did not say anything about knocking at 3:47. He did not say anything about cold door handles.

"Thank you so much for calling," the woman said. "Could you hold for just one moment?"

He held for four more minutes of piano.

When she came back, her voice was exactly as warm as before. "Mr. Uedera, I've checked for you. Officer Uedera is currently on assignment. During Obon week our field officers sometimes work extended operations and are not able to contact family. I completely understand how worrying that is. I'm sure he'll be in touch with you as soon as he's able."

"On assignment," Rei said. "Where?"

"I'm afraid I can't share operational details. I hope you understand."

"He left his keys on the walkway."

There was the smallest pause. Less than a second. "I'll make a note of that for you," the woman said. "Is there anything else I can help you with today?"

Rei said no. She thanked him again, wished him a peaceful Obon, and the line went dead.

He sat holding the phone. Then he looked down at his hand, and saw that he had written something on the back of an envelope while he was on hold, without noticing. It was a list of the words she had used. *On assignment. Extended operation. As soon as he's able.* Under them, in letters that pressed hard into the paper, he had written one more thing.

*She already knew.*

[break The envelope]

He turned the envelope over. It was a letter from the property company that owned the building. He had brought it in from the mailbox yesterday and not opened it. He opened it now.

It was a polite reminder. Rent for September, eighty-four thousand yen, was due on the twenty-seventh of August. Thank you for your continued residence.

Rei did the sums on the back of the envelope under the list of words. His internship paid him a little money each month for travel and lunch. It was not enough for rent. It was not enough for half of rent. Gin had always paid for the flat. Gin's salary came into the shared account on the twenty-fifth of every month, and Gin moved the rent across on the twenty-sixth, and Rei had never once had to think about it.

If Gin was on assignment, his salary would come. If Gin was not on assignment, Rei did not know what would happen. He did not know if a missing man still got paid. He did not know who to ask.

He folded the envelope and put it inside the archive like a bookmark.

That night Itsuki came over with his laptop and two bags of food from the konbini. He found Gin's search program on the old computer in Gin's room. The last file Gin had opened was on Wednesday, at 2:14 in the morning. It was a scan of one of Itsuki's drawings. A long white room with cushions on the floor and a low table, and nothing in it at all.

"I hate that drawing," Itsuki said quietly.

"Why?"

"I don't know." He shut the laptop. "It makes me feel like I've been there."

They sat in the kitchen until late, not saying much. Itsuki smoked half a cigarette by the open window, then remembered whose habit that was and put it out quickly, and did not light another one. At midnight he fell asleep with his head on his arms on the kitchen table. Rei put a blanket over him and sat across from him with the archive and read until the sky went grey.

At 3:47 he looked up from the page and listened.

Nobody knocked.
