#title The Warm Side of the Line
#part Book One. The Archive

For a week Rei did what Tanizaki told him. He slept. He ate. It was harder than it sounded.

He went to the police station three days a week and sorted files. He cooked rice and ate it standing up. He read the archive at night, a chapter at a time, in order, the way Gin had asked. He did not call the CVD again. He did not go back to Ōji station. He took the long way to work, round by the river, so that he did not have to stand on that platform.

Daiki sent him eleven messages about the Jūjō summer festival on Saturday. Rei did not go. On Sunday Daiki sent one more.

*u ok?*

Rei looked at it for a long time. Then he wrote, *Gin's away for work. I'm just tired. Next one, I promise.*

*ok,* Daiki wrote. *ur buying the takoyaki tho.*

It was strange how much a stupid message about takoyaki could hold you up.

[break Thursday]

On Thursday morning he woke at six without an alarm and knew where he was going before he was fully awake. It was the twentieth of August. In the archive, on the page for the bakery in Jūjō, Gin had written *Go on Thursdays.*

Jūjō is two stops north of Ōji on the Saikyō line. Rei took the train with a carriage full of schoolchildren in summer club uniforms carrying baseball bats in long bags. The Saikyō line trains are green-striped and newer than the Keihin-Tōhoku ones, and the seats are softer. A girl beside him was asleep against her friend's shoulder with her mouth open. Her friend was reading a manga about a volleyball team and silently mouthing the words.

[need ch6_jujo_morning | Jūjō Ginza covered shopping arcade at 7am, shutters rolling up, fishmonger hosing the street, grandmothers in sun hats, a small bakery with a faded blue awning]

Jūjō Ginza is a shopping street. In Tokyo, many old neighbourhoods have a street called *something Ginza*, after the famous expensive shopping district in the centre of the city. It is a small joke. The Ginza in the centre has jewellers and department stores. The Ginza in Jūjō has a fishmonger, three greengrocers, a shop that sells only socks, a pharmacy, a tea shop, a shop that sells only brooms and buckets, and about forty shops that sell fried food. It is a long, narrow street with a glass roof over it to keep off the rain, so it is called an arcade. It is famous across Tokyo for being cheap. Old women came here from three wards away to buy croquettes for eighty yen.

At seven in the morning the arcade was just waking up. Shutters rolled up one by one with a rattle. A man hosed down the front of the fish shop, and the water ran in a silver line down the middle of the street. The smell of frying oil had already started. Two grandmothers in sun hats walked arm in arm, slowly, looking at the vegetables. A cat sat on a stack of plastic crates and washed its face.

The bakery was halfway down the arcade on the left. It had a blue awning that said KONDŌ BAKERY in white letters, faded almost to grey. The window was full of trays of bread. Melon bread with sugary crusts. Curry bread in fat golden pillows. And anpan. Round, soft brown buns, each with a small black sesame seed pressed into the top, filled with sweet red bean paste.

A man in his sixties stood behind the counter with flour on his forearms up to the elbow. He had a round face and an easy smile and a white towel tied round his head.

"Anpan?" he said, before Rei had opened his mouth. "Thursday. Everyone wants anpan on Thursday."

"Two, please," said Rei.

Mr. Kondō put them in a paper bag and folded the top over twice, neatly, with his thumbs. While he did it, he talked about the weather. It was going to be hot again. It had been the hottest August in ten years, the man on the television said. His wife wanted an air conditioner for the bedroom. He thought the fan was fine. A fan is a fan, he said. Rei liked him at once.

He paid, and stood outside with the warm bag in his hands, and waited.

[break The detour]

At half past seven Mr. Kondō came out of the bakery with an empty plastic crate and walked toward the end of the arcade to fetch more flour from a storeroom.

Rei watched him go. And he saw it.

Halfway along, outside a shop with its shutter down and an old sign that said OKAMURA WATCHES AND CLOCKS, Mr. Kondō stepped to the right. He did it smoothly, without looking, the way you step round a puddle you have stepped round a thousand times. He walked in a small curve around a patch of the arcade floor about the size of a dinner table, and then stepped back to the middle of the street and walked on.

There was nothing on the floor there. No puddle. No crack. Just the same grey tiles as everywhere else.

Rei watched the other people in the arcade. A woman with a pushchair came from the other direction. When she reached the closed watch shop, she steered the pushchair round the same patch of floor, in the same small curve. She did not look down either. A boy on a bicycle did it. A delivery man with a trolley did it. Even the cat, when it jumped down from the crates and trotted up the street, swung wide round the same spot, with its tail low, and then went back to the middle.

Nobody stepped on that patch of floor. Nobody knew they were not stepping on it.

Rei walked slowly up to the closed watch shop and stood at the edge of the patch, and let the door in his chest open, just a crack.

It was cold.

Not the deep, wet cold from the platform. This was a smaller cold, like the air from an open fridge. And with it came a feeling that landed on Rei all at once, so strongly that he had to put his hand on the shutter to stay upright. It was the feeling of waiting. Of waiting for a very long time for someone who was late. Checking the time. Checking it again. Telling yourself they'll be here in a minute. Five more minutes. Ten more minutes. Of the clock moving on and the person not coming, and not coming, and never coming.

He opened the door in his chest a little more.

[need ch6_old_woman | In a morning shopping arcade, the faded pale figure of a small old woman in a wrongly buttoned brown cardigan and flowered apron, empty shopping basket, looking at her bare wrist as if at a watch, in front of a closed watch shop, shoppers unconsciously curving around her]

The arcade went pale and thin, like a photograph left too long in a window. And in the middle of the patch of floor, there was an old woman.

She was small and bent. She wore a brown cardigan buttoned wrong, one button too low, and a flowered apron over a long grey skirt. Her hair was white and thin and pinned up with a single plastic clip. She held a shopping basket over her arm with nothing in it. She stood facing the end of the arcade, toward the street outside, and every few seconds she lifted her wrist and looked at it. There was no watch on her wrist. She looked anyway.

She did not look frightening. She looked like any grandmother in any shopping street in Japan. That was what made Rei's throat tighten.

"Excuse me," he said quietly. "Grandmother?"

She turned her head. Her eyes were cloudy and pale, and they did not quite find him. They looked at a spot a little to the left of his face.

"He's late," she said. Her voice was very thin and very far away, like a voice on a phone in another room. "He went to buy cigarettes. He said, I'll be ten minutes. He's never late. Have you seen him? He's tall. He has a grey hat."

Rei did not know what to say. He had read about this in the archive, two nights ago. He tried to remember the page. *Spirits held in place by an unfinished expectation. Not dangerous. Not able to leave. They need the expectation answered, or released.*

"What day is it?" he asked her gently.

She looked at her empty wrist. "Thursday," she said. "It's Thursday. We always come on Thursday. For the anpan."

Behind him, in the street, a shutter rattled up. Someone laughed. A radio started playing in the greengrocer's. The old woman did not hear any of it. She lifted her wrist and looked at it again.

Rei thought of Gin. Of the plate upside down on the counter. Of the phone that said *Delivered* and never *Read*. Of checking it, and checking it again, and telling himself, a few more minutes.

He understood her better than he wanted to.

[break The line]

Here is how the archive said to do it. Rei had read the page so many times he could see it with his eyes closed.

You do not force a spirit like this one to go. You do not shout at it or throw salt at it. You make a line, and you show it which side is which.

He knelt on the tiles at the edge of the cold patch. He took a small bag of coarse salt out of his backpack. He had bought it at a supermarket the day before because the archive said to carry salt the way you carry a phone. He poured a thin line of salt across the floor between the old woman and the open end of the arcade, where the morning light came in. Then, on the side of the line closest to him, the street side, he put down one of the two anpan from the bakery bag, still warm.

"Grandmother," he said. "He's not coming. I'm sorry. He's not late. He's gone ahead."

She looked at the line of salt. Her face did not change.

"It's Thursday," Rei said. "There's anpan. It's on this side. On the warm side." He did not know if he was saying it right. He was making it up from a page in a book written by his brother, at four in the morning, on a street where he had never been before. "He's on the other side. He went ahead to wait for you. It's your turn to be late now. It's alright to be late."

For a long moment nothing happened. The cold sat against his face and hands. His knees ached on the tiles. Somewhere behind him a child asked her mother why the man was kneeling on the floor, and the mother said *shh*.

Then the old woman lowered her wrist.

She looked at the anpan on the tiles. She looked at the line of salt. She looked, for the first time, straight at Rei, and her eyes were not cloudy any more. They were brown and clear and very tired.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, it's so late."

She stepped over the salt line. Not toward Rei. Past him, toward the open end of the arcade and the white morning light. As she passed him she put her hand on his head, lightly, the way grandmothers do with small boys. He felt it. It was cold, and then it was warm, and then it was nothing.

The patch of floor was just a patch of floor.

[break After]

He tried to stand up and could not.

His legs had gone soft. His hands were shaking so badly that the paper bag rattled. The edges of his vision had gone dark and narrow, like looking down a tube. He could hear his own heart, very loud and very fast, in his ears. He felt exactly as if he had run up all the stairs of a tall building with a bag of rice on his back, and then been asked to run up again.

He found himself sitting on a plastic crate outside the closed watch shop. He did not remember getting there. He was breathing in big ragged gasps.

A shadow fell over him. It was Mr. Kondō, holding his crate of flour.

"You alright, son?" he said. "You've gone white as rice."

"It's the heat," said Rei. He heard himself say it. It was the same thing the blue suits had told the mother on the platform. It came out of his mouth so easily that it frightened him.

Mr. Kondō looked at him for a moment. Then he looked down at the floor, at the line of salt, at the anpan lying on the tiles. Then he looked at the patch of floor in front of the watch shop, the one he had walked round for twenty-three years without knowing why.

He did not say anything. He frowned slightly, like a man who has forgotten a word.

Then he put the crate of flour down, walked straight across the patch, the middle of it, back to the bakery, and came out again with a cold bottle of water and a curry bread in a paper bag.

"On the house," he said, and pushed both into Rei's hands. "Eat. You young people don't eat. My grandson's the same."

He walked back to his bakery, straight down the middle of the arcade, and did not step round anything at all.

Rei sat on the crate and ate the curry bread, and drank the water, and slowly the dark edges of his vision went away. He felt hollowed out and scraped clean. He felt like the inside of a bowl. He also felt, for the first time in eight days, that he had done something that was not waiting.

He took out his phone and, without thinking about it, wrote to Gin.

*I helped someone today. In Jūjō. With salt and anpan. I think I did it right.*

*Delivered.*

He put the phone away before it could not say *Read*.
