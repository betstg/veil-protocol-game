#title The Six Paths
#part Book One. The Archive

At six in the morning the training hall was cold, even in September.

It was a long, high room in the oldest part of the Kuroda house, with a floor of wide dark boards worn smooth and shiny by four hundred years of bare feet. Itsuki had not been exaggerating. In places the wood had gone soft and dipped, like the middle of an old stone step. The walls were plain wood. At one end hung a single scroll with one character painted on it in thick black ink. 門. *Gate.* The tall windows along one side were open, and the morning came in grey and damp, with the smell of the wet garden and the sound of the carp splashing in the pond.

[need ch10_training_hall | A long high old wooden training hall at dawn, wide dark floorboards worn into soft dips, tall open windows onto a wet garden, a single scroll with the kanji 門 at the end, young people in plain training clothes kneeling in a row]

There were six other people in the hall when they arrived. Four were young, cousins of Itsuki's, in plain grey training clothes. One was an old woman with her hair in a white bun, kneeling by the wall with a teapot and a small wooden box beside her. And one was Kuroda Sōun, standing in the middle of the floor in a plain dark kimono, with his hands behind his back.

Itsuki's cousins looked at Itsuki the way you look at a dog that has walked into a restaurant.

"Morning," said Itsuki brightly. "I'm back. Did everyone miss me? Don't answer that."

Nobody answered it.

[break What you are]

"Before you learn anything," said Sōun, "you will learn what there is to learn."

He walked slowly down the hall as he talked. His voice was quiet, but it filled the room without effort.

"There are six paths," he said. "Six ways a person can touch the veil. Most people who can touch it at all have one path open. Some have two. Very few have more. Each path is a different kind of hand. You do not choose your hands. You are born with them."

He stopped by the first of the cousins, a tall young man with a shaved head.

"Telekinesis. The hand that moves things."

The young man did not move. On the floor in front of him lay six flat floor cushions in a row. As Rei watched, the first cushion lifted off the boards, slowly, as if someone were picking it up by the corners. It rose to the height of the young man's chest and hung there. Then the second rose and stacked itself on top of the first. Then the third. Then all six were stacked in the air, neat as folded laundry, and the young man had not lifted a finger. His face was perfectly calm. Only a small vein in his temple stood out.

"Put them down, Takuya," said Sōun. The cushions settled back to the floor one by one, like snow.

He moved to the next cousin, a girl of about sixteen with a long ponytail.

"Psychokinesis. The hand that makes heat, and cold, and lightning."

The girl held up her right hand, palm open. On her palm was a short white candle, unlit. She looked at it. The wick blackened, smoked, and caught. A small yellow flame stood up from it, steady. Then it went blue. Then it went out, and the candle was covered in frost, white and crystalline, and the girl's breath came out as a cloud in the September air.

"It is the bluntest of the six," said Sōun. "It does not talk to the dead. It burns them. It burns the bodies they are wearing, too." He looked at Rei. "Remember that."

The old woman by the wall did not wait to be introduced. She beckoned to Itsuki. Itsuki went, reluctantly, and knelt in front of her, and she took his right hand in both of hers and turned it over. There was a fresh cut across his knuckles, a scrape from something in the studio, that Rei had not even noticed.

"Psychometabolism," said Sōun. "The hand that reads the body. Living or dead. And mends it."

The old woman held Itsuki's hand and closed her eyes. Rei watched the scrape. Slowly, while he watched, the red edges of it went pink. The pink went pale. The skin drew together from both sides like a zip being closed. In less than a minute there was nothing on Itsuki's knuckles but a faint white line.

"You don't sleep," the old woman said to Itsuki, without opening her eyes. "You don't eat breakfast. You drink too much coffee from cans. You smoke. Your heart is fine. Your liver is annoyed." She let go of his hand. "Stop smoking."

"I'll think about it, Grandmother Mitsu."

"You won't." She patted his cheek, hard, almost a slap. "Go."

[break The other three]

"Telepathy," said Sōun. "The hand that touches minds."

He did not demonstrate it with a cousin. He looked at Rei.

*You are frightened,* said a voice inside Rei's head.

It was Sōun's voice. Low and dry and careful, exactly the same as his speaking voice, but it was not in the room. It was inside, behind Rei's eyes, close as a thought. Rei jerked back so hard that he almost fell. The cousins laughed. Sōun did not.

*You are frightened,* the voice went on, calmly, *and you are angry, and under the anger you are very tired. And you keep a door in your chest, and you think it was given to you for sleeping.* A pause. *It was not.*

Then it was gone. Rei's head was his own again. He was breathing hard. He felt like someone had opened his bedroom door while he was undressed.

"That was rude," Sōun said aloud. "I did it to show you how rude it is. A telepath can read the surface of a person without asking. Fear. Anger. What they want. What they are hiding. It is the easiest of the six to abuse and the hardest to be trusted with." He looked at Itsuki's cousins until they stopped smiling. "A good telepath spends his whole life asking permission. Most of them are very lonely people."

Rei would remember that sentence for a long time.

"Clairsentience," said Sōun. "The hand that reads what has happened. Rooms. Objects. Places. The structure of things. Who has power over whom. Where it will break." He looked at Rei again. "That one, I think, you know."

"And the sixth?" said Rei.

Sōun turned to the end of the hall, where the scroll hung. 門. *Gate.*

"Teleportation," he said. "The hand that opens doors. You saw it last night. To step from one place into another without crossing the space between. It is the rarest of the six. It is the Kuroda path. Every person in this family has carried it in their blood for four hundred years." He did not look at his son. "Some of them open it."

Itsuki was looking at the floor.

"That is the wheel," said Sōun. "Six hands. Every practitioner has a rank, which is how far their hands can reach. A Null cannot reach at all. A Sensitive feels the veil and nothing more. Postulate. Adept. Ritualist. Arbiter. Vanguard. Guardian. Each one a little further. Beyond Guardian there is a rank called Singularity, and I have met two people in my life who held it, and I hope never to meet a third." He paused. "And every practitioner also has a stability, which is how much of themselves is left at the end of the day. Rank is how far you can reach. Stability is how much you have left to reach with. They are not the same. Fools mix them up. Fools die."

He looked at Rei.

"You have been reading the dead for years with no training and no protection," he said. "Opening and closing that door in your chest like a child slamming a gate. I would guess your rank is very high and your stability is very low. That is the most dangerous kind of person there is." He turned away. "So we will begin with the door."

[break The door]

For the first week, they did nothing but the door.

Rei knelt on the old boards from six until nine every morning before work, and opened and closed the door in his chest, and was told he was doing it wrong. He slammed it, Sōun said. He opened it all at once, like throwing a bucket of water, and shut it all at once, like slamming a car boot. Every time he did that, something in him tore a little. That was why the arcade had knocked him flat. That was why the cup in Lilac had left him shaking.

"Slowly," said Sōun, walking behind him. "A crack. A finger's width. Look through it. Then close it, a finger's width at a time. Do not slam. A slammed door is a sign. It tells everything on the other side that someone is here and is afraid."

On the third day Grandmother Mitsu set five plain clay bowls of water in a row on the floor in front of him and blindfolded him with a strip of white cloth.

"One of these bowls," she said, "was filled from the well at a funeral house this morning. The others came from the kitchen tap. Find it. Open slowly. If you slam, I hit you."

Rei opened the door in his chest a finger's width. The darkness behind the blindfold changed. It was hard to describe. The bowls were not things to him now. They were five small weights in the room, and four of them weighed nothing, and the fourth from the left weighed like a stone in his stomach. It smelled, very faintly, of chrysanthemums and wet earth and incense.

"Fourth," he said. "Fourth from the left."

There was a silence in the hall. When he took off the blindfold, the four cousins were all looking at him. Not the way they looked at Itsuki. A different way. The girl with the ponytail had frost on her fingertips and had not noticed.

"Three seconds," said Grandmother Mitsu, and for the first time, she smiled. "It took Takuya three months."

"It did not take me three months," said Takuya.

"It took you three months and you cried," said Grandmother Mitsu.

[break Itsuki's door]

Itsuki could not open his door at all.

Every morning, while Rei practised, Itsuki stood in front of the black lacquered doorframe at the end of the hall. The family's training gate. It was the same as the one in Sōun's room, two posts and a crossbeam with a rope of white paper across the top, standing by itself on the boards. A Kuroda opened it by putting a hand on the post and finding the door inside themselves. On the first day his father showed him once, without a word, and through the frame for a moment there was the pond outside, as if the hall had a hole in it.

Itsuki put his hand on the post. Nothing happened.

He stood there for three hours. Nothing happened. He stood there the next day, and the next. Nothing happened. His cousins stopped watching. His father never said a word to him, good or bad, which was worse than anything he could have said. By the end of the first week Itsuki had a bruise on his forehead from leaning his head against the black post in frustration, and he was smoking twice as much as before, and Grandmother Mitsu had started hitting him on the back of the head every time she smelled it on him.

On Friday morning Rei sat next to him by the gate while everyone else was at breakfast.

"It's not going to open," said Itsuki. He was looking at the plaster wall through the empty frame. "There's nothing there. I don't have one. My father's been wrong for twenty years and he's too proud to say it."

"You said you see what you paint."

"So?"

"So paint it."

Itsuki turned and looked at him.

"I'm serious," said Rei. "You've been painting the same bridge for two years. You said you don't know why. What if it's a door? What if that's what your door looks like to you?" He shrugged. "I don't know anything. I've been here a week. But you don't open things by staring at them. You open them by knowing what's behind them."

Itsuki stared at him for a long moment.

"That's the stupidest thing anyone has ever said to me," he said. "That's so stupid. That's so stupid it's annoying."

"Okay."

"Shut up. I'm thinking."

He put his hand on the black post again. He closed his eyes. Rei watched his face. It changed slowly. The frown went out of it. His mouth moved a little, the way it did when he was painting and trying to get a colour right. His hand on the post went still.

The plaster wall through the frame wavered.

Not much. It went soft for a moment, like a road in the heat. For half a second Rei thought he saw, through the frame, something dark and wet and moving. Water. A dark river at night, and a light on it.

Then Itsuki opened his eyes and the wall was a wall, and he snatched his hand off the post as if it were hot, and stared at his palm.

"Did you," he said. "Did you see..."

"Yes."

"Don't tell my father."

"Why not?"

"Because I want to do it properly first," said Itsuki, and his voice shook, and he was grinning like an idiot. "I want to do it all the way and see his stupid face. Okay? Promise."

"Promise."

From the doorway at the far end of the hall, where neither of them was looking, Kuroda Sōun watched his son grin at a doorframe. He stood there for a long time. Then he turned and walked away down the old corridor, very quietly, so that no one would hear him go.
